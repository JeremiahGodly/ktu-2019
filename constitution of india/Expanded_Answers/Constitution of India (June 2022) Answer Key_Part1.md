# Constitution of India (June 2022) Answer Key - Part 1

## PART A (3-mark questions expanded)

### 1. Explain the preamble of Indian Constitution.
The Preamble to the Constitution of India serves as a brief introductory statement that sets out the guiding purpose, principles, and philosophy of the Indian constitutional framework. It essentially acts as the soul of the Constitution, illuminating the intentions of its framers and the historical context of its creation. The Preamble begins with the historic words "We, the people of India," emphasizing the concept of popular sovereignty, implying that all political power emanates from the citizens. It declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic state. 'Sovereign' signifies that India is an independent entity, free from external control. 'Socialist' and 'Secular', added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, underscore the state's commitment to social and economic equality and its neutral stance towards all religions. 'Democratic' ensures a government elected by the people, and 'Republic' means the head of state is elected, not a hereditary monarch. Furthermore, the Preamble outlines the core objectives of the state: securing Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and opportunity), and promoting Fraternity among all citizens to ensure the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation. It was adopted by the Constituent Assembly on November 26, 1949, and it acts as a guiding light for the interpretation of the Constitution, helping the judiciary when the language of any article is ambiguous.

### 2. Give any five features of Constitution of India.
The Constitution of India is a unique and comprehensive document, characterized by several salient features that distinguish it from other global constitutions. Firstly, it is the longest written constitution in the world. Originally comprising 395 articles in 22 parts and 8 schedules, it has since grown significantly due to numerous amendments, reflecting the immense diversity and complexity of the nation. Secondly, it exhibits a unique blend of rigidity and flexibility. While some provisions can be amended by a simple parliamentary majority, others require a special majority and ratification by at least half of the state legislatures, ensuring stability while allowing for progressive changes. Thirdly, it establishes a parliamentary system of government, both at the center and in the states, where the executive is accountable to the legislature, drawing inspiration from the British model but adapted to Indian conditions. Fourthly, it provides for a quasi-federal structure; it has strong unitary bias, especially during emergencies, but functions as a federation in normal times with a clear division of powers between the Union and the States. Fifthly, the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights to all citizens, protecting them against arbitrary state action. These rights are justiciable, meaning citizens can approach the courts for their enforcement. Additionally, it features an independent judiciary, single citizenship, and universal adult franchise, making it a dynamic and robust framework for the world's largest democracy.

### 3. Differentiate Rights and Duties with example.
Rights and duties are two sides of the same coin, intrinsically linked in any democratic society. Rights refer to the fundamental freedoms, entitlements, and claims that individuals possess, which are recognized and protected by the state. They are essential for the holistic development of an individual's personality and for leading a dignified life. For instance, the Right to Freedom of Speech and Expression allows a citizen to voice their opinions without fear of unwarranted government censorship. In contrast, duties are the moral, legal, or societal obligations that individuals are expected to fulfill towards the state, society, and their fellow citizens. They represent the responsibilities that come with the enjoyment of rights. An example is the Fundamental Duty to abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem. The core difference lies in their enforceability and nature: fundamental rights are justiciable, meaning one can approach the courts for their enforcement if violated, whereas fundamental duties, generally, are non-justiciable and act more as a moral compass for citizens. While rights empower the individual against state overreach, duties remind the individual of their obligations towards collective harmony and national progress. Without duties, rights can lead to anarchy; without rights, duties can lead to tyranny. Therefore, a harmonious balance between the two is crucial for a thriving democratic republic.

### 4. What protection are available to the Indian citizen against conviction?
Article 20 of the Indian Constitution provides crucial protections to citizens (and non-citizens) against arbitrary and excessive punishment in respect of conviction for offences, ensuring the rule of law. It encompasses three fundamental safeguards. First, it guarantees protection against ex post facto laws (Article 20(1)). This means a person cannot be convicted for an act that was not legally an offence at the time it was committed. Furthermore, a person cannot be subjected to a penalty greater than what was prescribed by the law in force at the time of the commission of the act. Second, it provides protection against double jeopardy (Article 20(2)). This principle dictates that no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. It protects individuals from the harassment of successive prosecutions for the same crime. Third, it ensures protection against self-incrimination (Article 20(3)). This crucial clause states that no person accused of any offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. It safeguards the accused from being coerced or tortured into making a confession or producing evidence that could lead to their own conviction. These protections form the bedrock of the criminal justice system in India, ensuring that the state's prosecutorial power is exercised within strict constitutional boundaries and that the fundamental human rights of the accused are respected throughout the legal process.

### 5. Give the duties of Attorney General.
The Attorney General for India is the highest law officer in the country, appointed by the President under Article 76 of the Constitution. The duties and functions of the Attorney General are multifaceted and crucial for the legal functioning of the Union Government. The primary duty is to give advice to the Government of India upon such legal matters, and to perform such other duties of a legal character, as may from time to time be referred or assigned to him by the President. This involves offering expert legal counsel on complex constitutional and statutory issues. Secondly, the Attorney General is tasked with discharging functions conferred by or under the Constitution or any other law. A significant part of the role involves representing the Government of India in the Supreme Court in all cases (including suits, appeals, and other proceedings) in which the Government is concerned. Furthermore, the Attorney General has the duty to appear on behalf of the Government of India in any reference made by the President to the Supreme Court under Article 143 of the Constitution. Additionally, the Attorney General may be required to appear in any High Court in any case in which the Government of India is involved. Notably, the Attorney General has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of Parliament or their joint sitting, and any parliamentary committee of which he may be named a member, but without the right to vote. 

### 6. Write five specialities of Supreme court.
The Supreme Court of India is the apex judicial body and boasts several unique specialities that cement its authority and independence. First, it functions as the ultimate interpreter and guardian of the Constitution. It has the power of judicial review, enabling it to strike down legislative acts and executive orders if they are found to be ultra vires or violative of the Constitution. Second, it serves as the guarantor of Fundamental Rights. Under Article 32, any citizen can directly approach the Supreme Court for the enforcement of their fundamental rights, and the Court can issue prerogative writs (like habeas corpus, mandamus, etc.) for this purpose. Third, the Supreme Court has extensive original and exclusive jurisdiction. It is the sole arbiter in disputes between the Government of India and one or more States, or between two or more States, ensuring the smooth functioning of the federal structure. Fourth, it possesses a very broad appellate jurisdiction, acting as the highest court of appeal for civil, criminal, and constitutional matters from High Courts across the country. Through special leave petitions under Article 136, it can grant leave to appeal against any judgment from any court or tribunal in India. Fifth, the Supreme Court is a Court of Record. Its judgments, proceedings, and acts are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony. These records hold evidential value and cannot be questioned when produced before any court. Furthermore, it has the power to punish for its own contempt, safeguarding its dignity and authority.

### 7. Explain Writ Jurisdiction.
Writ jurisdiction in the Indian Constitution is a powerful mechanism designed to enforce fundamental rights and ensure that administrative and judicial authorities act within their legal bounds. This jurisdiction is conferred primarily on two judicial bodies: the Supreme Court under Article 32 and the High Courts under Article 226. A writ is essentially a formal written order issued by the court directing a person, official, or authority to act or abstain from acting in a particular way. There are five main types of writs. 'Habeas Corpus' (to have the body) is issued to secure the release of a person who has been illegally detained. 'Mandamus' (we command) directs a public official, lower court, or government to perform their mandatory legal duties. 'Prohibition' is issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent them from exceeding their jurisdiction. 'Certiorari' (to be certified) is issued to quash an order passed by a lower court or tribunal if it acted without jurisdiction or in violation of natural justice. Finally, 'Quo Warranto' (by what authority) is used to challenge a person's right to hold a public office. While the Supreme Court can only issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights, the High Courts have a broader writ jurisdiction, allowing them to issue writs not only for fundamental rights but also "for any other purpose," such as addressing violations of ordinary legal rights. This makes writ jurisdiction a cornerstone of constitutional remedies in India.

### 8. Explain the role of Governor.
The Governor is the constitutional head of a State in the Indian Union, acting as a crucial link between the Central Government and the State Government. Appointed by the President, the Governor's role is multi-dimensional, encompassing executive, legislative, financial, and judicial powers. Primarily, the Governor is the nominal executive head; all executive actions of the State are taken in their name. They appoint the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, the other ministers. However, the Governor is expected to act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers in most matters. Legislatively, the Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature. They summon, prorogue, and can dissolve the State Legislative Assembly. Every bill passed by the state legislature must receive the Governor's assent to become law; they can give assent, withhold it, or reserve the bill for the President's consideration. Financially, no money bill can be introduced in the state legislature without the Governor's prior recommendation. The Governor also causes the annual state budget to be laid before the legislature. Crucially, the Governor holds certain discretionary powers, such as inviting a party to form the government when no clear majority exists, or reporting to the President about the breakdown of constitutional machinery in the state (Article 356), which can lead to President's Rule. Thus, the Governor serves both as the state's dignified head and as the Centre's representative, ensuring constitutional governance at the provincial level.

### 9. What is the procedure for amending the Constitution?
The procedure for amending the Constitution of India is detailed in Article 368 of Part XX. The framers designed a system that is partly rigid and partly flexible, ensuring stability while allowing for necessary adaptations over time. The amendment process can only be initiated by the introduction of a bill for that purpose in either House of Parliament (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha), and not in the state legislatures. The bill can be introduced either by a minister or by a private member and does not require prior permission of the President. The Constitution provides for three types of amendments based on the nature of the provisions involved. First, certain provisions can be amended by a simple majority in Parliament, similar to ordinary legislative process (e.g., formation of new states, citizenship). Second, a large number of provisions, including Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles, require a special majority of Parliament; this means a majority of the total membership of each House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of each House present and voting. Third, for provisions that affect the federal structure of the Constitution, such as the election of the President, distribution of legislative powers between the Union and States, or Article 368 itself, a special majority of Parliament is required, followed by ratification by the legislatures of at least half of the states by a simple majority. Once passed and ratified (if necessary), the bill is presented to the President, who must give their assent, thereby transforming the bill into an Amendment Act.

### 10. Why administrative tribunals are established?
Administrative tribunals are established in India as specialized adjudicatory bodies to provide a speedy, inexpensive, and specialized mechanism for resolving disputes related to specific administrative and service matters, bypassing the often slow and cumbersome procedures of traditional courts. The constitutional basis for these tribunals was laid by the 42nd Amendment Act of 1976, which inserted Part XIV-A (Articles 323A and 323B) into the Constitution. Article 323A allows Parliament to establish Administrative Tribunals specifically for adjudicating disputes and complaints relating to the recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, States, or local authorities. The primary rationale for their establishment is to reduce the massive backlog of cases in High Courts and the Supreme Court by diverting service-related litigation to these specialized forums. Administrative tribunals are manned by a combination of judicial and administrative members, ensuring that the adjudication process benefits from both legal expertise and practical administrative experience. Furthermore, these tribunals are not bound by the strict procedural rules of the Civil Procedure Code or the Indian Evidence Act; instead, they are guided by the principles of natural justice. This flexibility allows for a more informal, rapid, and pragmatic approach to dispute resolution. By offering an accessible and expert alternative to regular civil courts, administrative tribunals play a crucial role in ensuring efficient administrative justice and addressing the grievances of public servants effectively.
