# Constitution of India (July 2021) Answer Key - Part 1

## PART A (Expanded Answers for 3-mark questions)

**1. Define Constitution. Why is it necessary for a country?**
A constitution can be comprehensively defined as the fundamental law of a nation or state, establishing the character and conception of its government, laying the basic principles to which its internal life is to be conformed, organizing the government, and regulating, distributing, and limiting the functions of its different departments. It prescribes the extent and manner of the exercise of sovereign powers. A constitution is not just a legal document; it is a political and social charter that reflects the aspirations and values of a society. It is the supreme law of the land, meaning any other law or action by the government must align with its principles.
The necessity of a constitution for a country is manifold. Firstly, it provides a set of basic rules that allow for minimal coordination amongst members of a society. This coordination is crucial for maintaining peace and order in any complex, diverse society. Secondly, it specifies who has the power to make decisions in a society. It establishes the framework of the government, detailing the powers and responsibilities of the legislative, executive, and judicial branches. This prevents the arbitrary exercise of power and ensures a system of checks and balances. Thirdly, it sets limits on what a government can impose on its citizens, primarily through the guarantee of fundamental rights. These rights protect the individual from the potential tyranny of the state. Lastly, a constitution enables the government to fulfill the aspirations of a society and create conditions for a just society, acting as an instrument of social change and progress.

**2. Differentiate between termination and deprivation of Indian citizenship.**
Termination and deprivation are two distinct modes by which an individual can lose their Indian citizenship under the Citizenship Act of 1955. 
Termination of citizenship occurs when an Indian citizen voluntarily and consciously acquires the citizenship of another country. The moment a person acquires the citizenship of another sovereign state, their Indian citizenship automatically ceases to exist. This is based on the principle that India does not allow dual citizenship for its nationals. The process is self-executing and happens by the operation of law, provided the person is of full age and capacity. However, there is an exception during times of war: if India is engaged in a war, the termination of citizenship is held in abeyance and does not happen automatically unless the Central Government agrees to it.
Deprivation of citizenship, on the other hand, is a compulsory termination of Indian citizenship by the Central Government. It is not voluntary but is imposed by the state under specific circumstances outlined in the Citizenship Act. Deprivation applies only to those who have acquired Indian citizenship by naturalization or registration. A person can be deprived of their citizenship if it was obtained by fraud, false representation, or the concealment of any material fact. It can also occur if the citizen has shown disloyalty to the Constitution of India, unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy during a war, been imprisoned in any country for two years within five years of registration or naturalization, or has been ordinarily resident out of India for seven continuous years. Deprivation requires an executive order after giving the person a reasonable opportunity to be heard.

**3. How is State defined under Article 12 of Indian Constitution?**
Article 12 of the Indian Constitution provides an inclusive and expansive definition of the term "State" for the purpose of Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (Directive Principles of State Policy). According to Article 12, the "State" includes the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and the Legislature of each of the States, and all local or other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India. 
The inclusion of the "Government and Parliament of India" covers the executive and legislative organs of the Union government. Similarly, "Government and the Legislature of each of the States" covers the executive and legislative organs of the state governments. "Local authorities" include municipalities, panchayats, improvement trusts, and other similar bodies that have the power to make rules and regulations and have autonomous status.
The most widely interpreted part of this definition is "other authorities." Through various judicial pronouncements, the Supreme Court of India has expanded this term to include bodies created by a statute, bodies exercising sovereign functions, and even private bodies or corporations that are financially, functionally, or administratively dominated by or under the pervasive control of the government. This expansion ensures that entities acting as instrumentalities or agencies of the state cannot violate the fundamental rights of citizens, thus providing broader protection to the individuals against arbitrary actions not just by the core government but also by bodies functioning on its behalf.

**4. What is the basic difference between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy?**
The Fundamental Rights (FRs) enshrined in Part III and the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs) contained in Part IV of the Indian Constitution form the core of the constitutional philosophy, yet they differ significantly in their nature, enforceability, and objectives.
The primary difference lies in their justiciability. Fundamental Rights are justiciable in nature, meaning they are legally enforceable by the courts. If a citizen's fundamental right is violated, they can directly approach the High Courts or the Supreme Court for its enforcement. In contrast, Directive Principles are non-justiciable. They are not enforceable by any court of law. A citizen cannot sue the state for not implementing a directive principle.
Secondly, they differ in their overarching objectives. Fundamental Rights are primarily aimed at establishing political democracy. They act as negative obligations on the state, preventing it from doing certain things that would infringe upon individual liberties. They protect individuals against arbitrary state action. Directive Principles, however, are aimed at establishing social and economic democracy. They act as positive obligations on the state, directing it to achieve certain social and economic goals, such as equitable distribution of wealth and provision of adequate livelihood. While Fundamental Rights focus on the welfare of the individual, Directive Principles focus on the welfare of the community as a whole. Despite these differences, they are considered complementary, forming an integrated scheme for the realization of the goals set out in the Preamble.

**5. Explain the procedure for impeachment of the President.**
The President of India can be removed from office before the expiry of their term through a process called impeachment, exclusively on the ground of "violation of the Constitution." The procedure for impeachment is detailed in Article 61 of the Constitution and is a quasi-judicial process.
The impeachment process can be initiated by either house of the Parliament (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha). The charges must be framed in the form of a resolution. For the resolution to be introduced, it must be signed by at least one-fourth of the total members of the house that is framing the charges. Furthermore, a 14-day advance written notice must be given to the President regarding the intention to move such a resolution.
After the notice period, the resolution must be passed by a special majority of not less than two-thirds of the total membership of that house. Once passed by the initiating house, the charges are sent to the other house, which then investigates the charges or appoints a committee to do so. The President has the right to appear and be represented at such an investigation to defend themselves. If, after the investigation, the second house also passes a resolution sustaining the charges by a majority of not less than two-thirds of its total membership, the President stands removed from their office from the date on which such a resolution is passed. This stringent procedure ensures that the highest office is not subjected to frivolous removals.

**6. Mention the powers and Functions of Attorney General of India.**
The Attorney General of India is the highest law officer in the country, appointed by the President under Article 76 of the Constitution. The primary function of the Attorney General is to give advice to the Government of India upon such legal matters, and to perform such other duties of a legal character, as may from time to time be referred or assigned to them by the President.
Key functions include representing the Government of India in the Supreme Court in all cases (including suits, appeals, and other proceedings) in which the Government is concerned. They also represent the Government of India in any reference made by the President to the Supreme Court under Article 143 of the Constitution. Furthermore, the Attorney General has to appear in any High Court in any case in which the Government of India is concerned, if the Government requires them to do so.
In terms of powers, the Attorney General has the right of audience in all courts within the territory of India. Uniquely, the Attorney General has the right to speak in, and otherwise to take part in the proceedings of, either House of Parliament, any joint sitting of the Houses, and any committee of Parliament of which they may be named a member. However, they do not have the right to vote in these proceedings. They enjoy all the privileges and immunities that are available to a Member of Parliament. While acting as the top legal advisor, they are not a full-time counsel for the Government and are not debarred from private legal practice, provided it does not conflict with their duties to the state.

**7. Why do we need to form separate Union Territories?**
Union Territories (UTs) are federal territories in India that are administered directly by the Central Government, unlike the states which have their own elected governments. The formation of separate Union Territories is necessitated by various geographical, cultural, administrative, and political reasons.
Firstly, some territories are geographically isolated or too small to be formed into separate independent states. Examples include the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep. Their remote location and strategic importance in the Indian Ocean make direct central control vital for national security and effective administration. Secondly, cultural distinctiveness is a significant factor. Territories like Puducherry, Daman and Diu, and Dadra and Nagar Haveli have unique cultural heritages influenced by former French or Portuguese colonial rule. Maintaining them as UTs helps in preserving their distinct cultural identity while integrating them into the Indian Union.
Thirdly, political and administrative considerations often demand direct central control. Delhi, being the National Capital, requires special administrative attention and direct central oversight to ensure the smooth functioning of the national government institutions and diplomatic missions located there. Similarly, Chandigarh serves as the joint capital of Punjab and Haryana, and making it a UT was an administrative necessity to resolve the conflict between the two states over its control. Recently, the reorganization of Jammu and Kashmir into two UTs (J&K and Ladakh) was driven by complex political and security considerations. In essence, UTs are formed when an area cannot be merged with neighboring states and is not large or viable enough to become a state of its own, requiring the direct stewardship of the Union government.

**8. Distinguish between an 'Ordinary Bill' and 'Money Bill'.**
In the Indian parliamentary system, legislative proposals are brought before the Parliament in the form of bills. The two most common types are Ordinary Bills and Money Bills, which differ significantly in their subject matter, introduction process, and the powers of the two houses.
A Money Bill is strictly defined under Article 110 of the Constitution. It contains provisions dealing exclusively with matters such as taxation (imposition, abolition, remission, alteration), borrowing of money by the government, the custody of the Consolidated Fund or Contingency Fund of India, or the appropriation of money out of the Consolidated Fund. An Ordinary Bill, on the other hand, deals with any general matter of public importance other than financial subjects and constitutional amendments. 
The procedure for their passage varies considerably. A Money Bill can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha (the lower house) and only on the recommendation of the President. An Ordinary Bill can be introduced in either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha (the upper house) and does not require the President's prior recommendation. Once passed by the Lok Sabha, the Rajya Sabha has very limited powers regarding a Money Bill. It can only make recommendations, which the Lok Sabha may or may not accept, and it must return the bill within 14 days. If it fails to do so, the bill is deemed passed. For an Ordinary Bill, both houses have almost equal powers. If there is a deadlock over an Ordinary Bill, the President can summon a joint sitting of both houses to resolve it. No joint sitting can be summoned for a Money Bill. Furthermore, the President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration, whereas they can do so for an Ordinary Bill.

**9. What are the constitutional provisions relating to freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse?**
The constitutional provisions relating to the freedom of trade, commerce, and intercourse within the territory of India are primarily enshrined in Part XIII of the Constitution, covering Articles 301 to 307. The underlying objective of these provisions is to break down the border barriers between states and create a single economic unit, fostering a sense of national unity and ensuring the unhindered flow of goods and services.
Article 301 establishes the general principle that trade, commerce, and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free. This freedom is not just from internal customs duties but also from any other restrictions that may impede the free movement of goods, thereby aiming to establish India as one unified common market. 
However, this freedom is not absolute and is subject to certain restrictions outlined in subsequent articles. Article 302 empowers the Parliament to impose restrictions on this freedom in the "public interest." For instance, essential commodities can be regulated during shortages. Article 303 prohibits both Parliament and State Legislatures from making laws that discriminate between one state and another, except when Parliament acts to deal with a situation of scarcity of goods in any part of India. Article 304 empowers State Legislatures to impose reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce, or intercourse with or within that state in the public interest, provided that such a bill requires the prior sanction of the President. It also allows states to impose non-discriminatory taxes on goods imported from other states. Lastly, Article 307 provides for the appointment of an authority by Parliament to carry out the purposes of these articles, though no such authority has been constituted to date.

**10. List out the three types of emergencies under Indian Constitution.**
The Constitution of India envisions extraordinary situations where the normal functioning of the democratic and federal structure needs to be suspended to protect the nation's integrity, security, and stability. To handle such unforeseen crises, Part XVIII of the Constitution provides for three specific types of emergencies.
The first type is the National Emergency, provided under Article 352. The President can declare a National Emergency if the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. This declaration shifts the federal structure into a unitary one, giving the Union government sweeping powers over the states, and it can also lead to the suspension of certain Fundamental Rights. It requires parliamentary approval within a month and can be extended indefinitely, subject to periodic approvals.
The second type is the State Emergency, commonly known as President's Rule, provided under Article 356. This is declared when there is a breakdown of constitutional machinery in a specific state. If the President is satisfied, either on the report of the state's Governor or otherwise, that the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution, they can dismiss the state government and assume the executive powers of the state, while the state legislature is suspended or dissolved.
The third type is the Financial Emergency, provided under Article 360. If the President is satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened, they can declare a Financial Emergency. During this period, the executive authority of the Union extends to giving directions to any state to observe specified canons of financial propriety, and it allows for the reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the State or the Union, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
