# Expanded Answers - Part 1 (Questions 1 to 10)

## 1. Explain salient features of Indian constitution.
The Constitution of India is a unique legal document that stands out for several salient features that reflect the historical, social, and political context of the nation. First and foremost, it is the lengthiest written constitution in the world, originally consisting of 395 Articles in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules, which comprehensively covers both fundamental principles and detailed administrative provisions. Another key feature is its blend of rigidity and flexibility; some parts can be amended by a simple parliamentary majority, while others require a special majority and ratification by at least half of the state legislatures. 

Furthermore, the Constitution establishes a federal system with unitary bias, often described as quasi-federal. It divides powers between the Centre and the States but vests strong emergency powers in the Central government. It guarantees Fundamental Rights (Part III) to all citizens, which are justiciable and essential for personal liberty and democratic freedoms. To balance these rights, it incorporates Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), which provide non-justiciable socio-economic guidelines for the government, and Fundamental Duties (Part IVA) for citizens. 

Additionally, India is established as a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic. It provides for a parliamentary form of government at both the Centre and the States, where the executive is responsible to the legislature. The Constitution also features an independent and integrated judiciary, with the Supreme Court at the apex, ensuring the protection of the Constitution and citizens' rights. Single citizenship, universal adult franchise, and special provisions for minorities and marginalized sections further highlight the comprehensive and inclusive nature of the Indian Constitution.

## 2. What do you mean by federal system of government? Give an example.
A federal system of government is a political and administrative framework where power is constitutionally divided and shared between a central national authority and constituent political units, such as states, provinces, or regions. In this system, both levels of government derive their authority directly from a written constitution, which serves as the supreme law of the land. The primary objective of federalism is to balance national unity with regional autonomy, allowing diverse regions to govern local affairs while uniting under a central government for matters of national and international significance.

Key characteristics of a federal system include a dual government structure, a clear division of powers (typically outlined in legislative lists), a written and rigid constitution that cannot be unilaterally altered by one level of government, and an independent judiciary tasked with resolving disputes between the center and the states. Each level of government operates within its own sphere of jurisdiction, meaning the central government handles issues like defense, foreign policy, and currency, while state governments manage local policing, public health, and intra-state commerce.

A classic example of a federal system is the United States of America. In the U.S., the federal government in Washington, D.C., exercises powers specifically enumerated in the U.S. Constitution (like declaring war and regulating interstate commerce). Meanwhile, the individual 50 states retain reserved powers under the Tenth Amendment, allowing them to create their own laws regarding education, criminal justice, and state infrastructure. India also has a federal structure, though it is often termed "quasi-federal" because of its strong centralizing tendencies, especially during emergencies.

## 3. Explain the concept of 'equality before the law'.
The concept of "equality before the law" is a fundamental democratic principle enshrined in Article 14 of the Constitution of India. It forms the bedrock of the Rule of Law, asserting that all individuals, regardless of their background, status, wealth, or position, are subject to the same laws and legal processes. Originating from English common law, this concept dictates the absence of any special privileges in favor of any person. It implies that no one is above the law—from the highest government official to the most ordinary citizen, everyone is equally accountable for their actions in the eyes of the judiciary.

Equality before the law essentially means negative equality; it prohibits arbitrary discrimination by the State. It ensures that the laws of the land apply universally and are administered by ordinary law courts without bias or favoritism. However, this does not mean absolute mathematical equality. The Constitution allows for "equal protection of the laws," which permits the State to classify individuals based on intelligible differentia that have a rational nexus to the objective sought to be achieved. For example, taxing the rich at a higher rate than the poor is a valid classification and does not violate equality before the law.

Furthermore, this principle guarantees that justice should be accessible to all on equal terms. It acts as a safeguard against authoritarianism and arbitrary executive action. By ensuring that the same legal standards govern similar situations, equality before the law promotes fairness, social justice, and trust in the legal and governmental institutions of the country.

## 4. "No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once." Discuss this clause.
The clause "No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once" refers to the legal doctrine of 'Double Jeopardy,' which is explicitly incorporated in Article 20(2) of the Constitution of India as a fundamental right. This principle has deep roots in the common law maxim *nemo debet bis vexari* (a man shall not be brought into danger for one and the same offense more than once). Its primary purpose is to protect individuals from the ongoing harassment, financial drain, and psychological trauma of facing multiple prosecutions and punishments for a single wrongful act by the state.

For the protection under Article 20(2) to be invoked in India, three distinct conditions must be satisfied. First, the person must have been accused of an "offence"—an act or omission made punishable by law. Second, there must have been a previous proceeding or "prosecution" before a court of law or judicial tribunal. Third, the previous prosecution must have resulted in actual "punishment." If a person was prosecuted but acquitted in the first trial, Article 20(2) does not strictly bar a second trial under the Constitution, although the procedural law (Section 300 of the Code of Criminal Procedure) does offer protection against retrial after an acquittal.

Furthermore, this protection only applies to judicial proceedings. It does not prevent a person from facing both criminal prosecution and separate administrative or departmental disciplinary action for the same conduct. For example, a government servant convicted of bribery by a criminal court can also be dismissed from service through a departmental inquiry without violating the rule of Double Jeopardy. 

## 5. Explain the procedure for impeachment of the President of India.
The procedure for the impeachment of the President of India is detailed in Article 61 of the Constitution. Impeachment is a quasi-judicial process used to remove the President from office before the expiration of their term, and the only ground provided in the Constitution for this drastic action is the "violation of the Constitution." Given the dignity and authority of the presidential office, the procedure is designed to be stringent and requires broad parliamentary consensus.

The process can be initiated by either House of Parliament—the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha. To introduce the impeachment charges, a resolution must be moved after giving a 14-day advance written notice. This notice must be signed by at least one-fourth of the total members of the House that is initiating the charge. Once the notice period expires, the resolution must be debated and passed by a special majority of not less than two-thirds of the total membership of that initiating House. 

If passed, the charges are then forwarded to the other House, which acts as the investigating body. This second House can either investigate the charges itself or appoint a tribunal/committee to do so. During this investigation, the President has the fundamental right to appear and be represented to defend themselves against the allegations. If, after the investigation, the second House sustains the charges and passes a resolution by a two-thirds majority of its total membership declaring that the charges have been proved, the President is removed from office with immediate effect from the date the resolution is passed.

## 6. Explain the role of the attorney general for India.
The Attorney General for India, established under Article 76 of the Constitution, is the highest law officer in the country and serves as the primary legal advisor to the Union Government. Appointed by the President of India on the advice of the Union Cabinet, the Attorney General must possess the qualifications required to be a judge of the Supreme Court. The role is pivotal in ensuring that the executive's actions are legally sound and defended robustly in the judicial system. 

The primary duty of the Attorney General is to give advice to the Government of India upon legal matters and to perform other duties of a legal character that are referred or assigned by the President. In practice, this means representing the Government of India in the Supreme Court in all cases (including appeals and petitions) where the government is a party. The Attorney General also represents the government in any reference made by the President to the Supreme Court under Article 143 (advisory jurisdiction). Furthermore, they may be required to appear in any High Court in cases concerning the Government of India.

A unique feature of the Attorney General's role is the constitutional right to speak in, and take part in the proceedings of, both Houses of Parliament or their joint sittings, as well as any parliamentary committee of which they may be named a member, though they do not have the right to vote. While they serve at the pleasure of the President and usually resign when the ruling government changes, they are not considered a full-time government servant and are not debarred from private legal practice, provided they do not advise or hold briefs against the Government of India.

## 7. Explain the procedure for the appointment of chief minister.
The procedure for the appointment of a Chief Minister in a State is governed primarily by Article 164 of the Constitution of India, which simply states that "the Chief Minister shall be appointed by the Governor." However, this constitutional provision is implemented based on established democratic conventions and the principles of the parliamentary system of government. The Governor does not have the arbitrary discretion to appoint anyone; they must appoint a person who commands the confidence of the State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha).

Following a general election to the State Legislative Assembly, the Governor invites the leader of the political party that has secured an absolute majority (more than half the total seats) to form the government and appoints them as the Chief Minister. If no single party achieves a clear majority, a situation known as a 'hung assembly', the Governor's discretionary powers come into play. In such cases, the Governor usually invites the leader of the largest single party or a pre-poll/post-poll coalition that appears most likely to prove its majority on the floor of the House. The appointed Chief Minister is then asked to seek a vote of confidence within a stipulated time frame.

A Chief Minister must be a member of either the Legislative Assembly or the Legislative Council (if the state has a bicameral legislature). If a person who is not a member of the state legislature is appointed as Chief Minister, they must get elected to either House within six months of their appointment; failure to do so results in the automatic termination of their tenure. 

## 8. Explain the duties of advocate general of the state.
The Advocate General of the State is the highest law officer in a state government, equivalent to the Attorney General of India at the central level. The office is established under Article 165 of the Constitution. Appointed by the Governor, the Advocate General must be a person qualified to be appointed as a judge of a High Court. The primary function of the Advocate General is to assist and advise the state government on complex legal matters.

The foremost duty of the Advocate General is to give legal advice to the government of the state on matters referred to them by the Governor. They are tasked with performing other duties of a legal character assigned by the Governor, as well as discharging functions conferred by the Constitution or any other law. In practical terms, the Advocate General represents the State Government in all significant civil and criminal cases before the state's High Court and, occasionally, before the Supreme Court of India if the state is a party to the litigation. 

Additionally, under Article 177, the Advocate General has the right to attend, speak, and take part in the proceedings of the State Legislative Assembly (and Legislative Council, where it exists) or any of its committees to which they may be appointed. This allows them to provide real-time legal expertise during legislative debates, although they do not possess the right to vote. The Advocate General holds office during the pleasure of the Governor and receives remuneration as determined by the Governor.

## 9. Discuss the functions of comptroller and auditor general of India.
The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India, established under Article 148 of the Constitution, is a pivotal constitutional authority serving as the supreme audit institution of the country. The CAG acts as the guardian of the public purse and controls the entire financial system of the country at both the Central and State levels. The primary mandate of the CAG is to ensure accountability of the executive to the Parliament and the State Legislatures regarding the collection and expenditure of public funds.

The core function of the CAG involves conducting rigorous audits of all receipts and expenditures of the Government of India and the state governments. This includes auditing the accounts of the Consolidated Fund of India, the Contingency Fund, and the Public Account. The CAG also audits the accounts of any authority or body substantially financed by government grants, government companies, and other corporations when required by related laws. The audit checks whether the money was legally available, whether it was spent on the intended purpose, and whether the expenditure was incurred with financial prudence and propriety (propriety audit).

After completing the audits, the CAG compiles comprehensive reports. Under Article 151, the reports concerning the accounts of the Union are submitted to the President, who causes them to be laid before both Houses of Parliament. Reports relating to state accounts are submitted to the respective Governors, who place them before the state legislatures. These reports form the basis for detailed scrutiny by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of the Parliament or State Legislature, solidifying the chain of financial accountability in India's democratic setup.

## 10. Explain the distribution of tax revenue with respect to centre-state financial relation.
The distribution of tax revenue between the Centre and the States is a cornerstone of India's federal structure, governed largely by Articles 268 to 279 of the Constitution. To ensure both levels of government have adequate resources to discharge their responsibilities, the Constitution makes a clear bifurcation of taxation powers, largely favoring the Central Government in terms of elastic and high-yield taxes, but mandating a mechanism for revenue sharing to address state deficits.

The Constitution categorizes tax distribution into several mechanisms. First, there are taxes levied by the Centre but collected and appropriated entirely by the States (e.g., stamp duties on bills of exchange under Article 268). Second, there are taxes levied and collected by the Centre but assigned entirely to the States (such as taxes on the sale or purchase of goods in the course of inter-state trade, Article 269). Third, and most importantly, there are taxes levied and collected by the Centre that are mandatorily shared between the Centre and the States (such as Income Tax and Corporation Tax, under Article 270).

The actual formula and percentage of the net proceeds of these shared taxes that go to the States are determined by the President based on the recommendations of the Finance Commission, which is constituted every five years under Article 280. The introduction of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) through the 101st Amendment significantly altered this landscape by introducing concurrent taxation powers. Under GST, both the Centre (CGST) and the States (SGST) simultaneously levy taxes on a common base of the supply of goods and services, while Integrated GST (IGST) on inter-state trade is levied by the Centre but apportioned between the Centre and States, modernizing and streamlining the federal financial architecture.
