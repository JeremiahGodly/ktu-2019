## Q11: Salient features of Indian Constitution & Preamble
**a) Salient Features of the Indian Constitution:**
The Indian Constitution is a unique document, characterized by a synthesis of diverse constitutional principles adapted to the historical, cultural, and political context of India. 
Firstly, it is the lengthiest and most detailed written constitution in the world. When originally adopted in 1949, it contained a Preamble, 395 Articles in 22 Parts, and 8 Schedules. Its voluminous nature is attributed to geographical factors (the vastness of the country), historical factors (the influence of the Government of India Act 1935), and the necessity of accommodating diverse cultures and communities. 
Secondly, it establishes a Federal Structure with a Unitary Bias. While it contains typical federal features like two tiers of government, division of powers, a written constitution, and an independent judiciary, it leans heavily towards a strong center, especially during emergencies, single citizenship, and the appointment of state governors by the center.
Thirdly, it adopted a Parliamentary Democracy modeled on the British system. The executive is responsible to the legislature, and the Prime Minister is the real head of the government, while the President is the nominal head. This system ensures continuous accountability of the executive to the representatives of the people.
Fourthly, Part III of the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights to citizens. These rights are justiciable, meaning they are enforceable by courts. They protect citizens from arbitrary state action and promote political democracy.
Fifthly, Part IV contains the Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP). Though non-justiciable, they are fundamental in the governance of the country, acting as moral guidelines for the state to establish a social and economic democracy.
Furthermore, the Constitution declares India a Secular state, meaning the state does not have an official religion and treats all religions equally. 
It also introduced Universal Adult Franchise, giving every citizen above the age of 18 the right to vote without any discrimination. 
Despite the federal structure, India has Single Citizenship, fostering a sense of unity among citizens. 
An Independent Judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court, acts as the guardian of the Constitution and the protector of fundamental rights. 
The Constitution also includes comprehensive Emergency Provisions to handle extraordinary situations like war or armed rebellion, effectively transforming the federal structure into a unitary one during crises. 
Additionally, it provides Reservation Policies to uplift socially and educationally backward classes. 
Finally, the Amendment Procedure strikes a balance between rigidity and flexibility, allowing the Constitution to evolve while safeguarding its core principles.

**b) Preamble of the Indian Constitution:**
The Preamble to the Indian Constitution is a profound declaration that outlines the foundational principles and the socio-economic and political objectives of the Indian state. It begins with "We, the people of India," emphasizing the concept of popular sovereignty—that the ultimate authority lies with the citizens. 
It declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic. 
'Sovereign' implies that India is an independent nation, internally supreme and externally free from control by any foreign power. 
The term 'Socialist', added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, reflects the commitment to achieving social and economic equality, aiming to end poverty, ignorance, and inequality of opportunity through democratic means. 
'Secular', also added in 1976, guarantees that the state has no official religion, treats all religions with equal respect, and ensures freedom of religion to all individuals. 
'Democratic' indicates that the government derives its authority from the will of the people, established through free and fair periodic elections. 
'Republic' signifies that the head of the state (the President) is elected directly or indirectly by the people and is not a hereditary monarch. 
The Preamble further resolves to secure justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity for all citizens, outlining the moral framework for the nation's governance.

## Q12: Citizenship Methods
**a) Methods of Acquiring Citizenship:**
Citizenship defines the legal relationship between an individual and a state, granting rights and imposing duties. In India, citizenship is governed by the constitutional provisions (Articles 5 to 11) and primarily the Citizenship Act of 1955. This act outlines various methods through which Indian citizenship can be acquired.
1. Citizenship by Birth: A person born in India on or after January 26, 1950, is generally considered a citizen by birth. However, the law has evolved to prevent illegal immigration. Currently, a person born in India is a citizen if both parents are Indian citizens, or if one is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. Citizenship by Descent: This applies to persons born outside India. A person born outside India after January 26, 1950, can acquire citizenship by descent if either of their parents was an Indian citizen at the time of their birth, subject to specific registration requirements at an Indian consulate.
3. Citizenship by Registration: The government can register individuals as citizens upon application if they meet certain criteria. This includes persons of Indian origin who have been ordinarily resident in India for seven years, persons married to Indian citizens, and minor children of Indian citizens.
4. Citizenship by Naturalization: A foreigner can acquire Indian citizenship through naturalization if they have resided in India for a specified period (usually 12 years), renounce their previous citizenship, have a good character, and possess adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution.
5. Citizenship by Incorporation of Territory: If a new territory becomes a part of India, the Government of India can specify the persons from that territory who shall be the citizens of India. For instance, when Puducherry and Goa were incorporated into India, their residents became Indian citizens.
6. Overseas Citizens of India (OCI): While not a full citizenship (India does not allow dual citizenship), the OCI scheme provides a lifelong visa and certain economic and educational privileges to persons of Indian origin residing abroad.
7. Citizenship by Birth on Ships and Aircraft: Under international law principles adopted by India, a child born on an Indian-registered ship or aircraft, regardless of its location, is considered to be born in India and may be eligible for citizenship.
8. Any Other Method: The Parliament retains the power to prescribe any other method or condition for acquiring citizenship as it deems fit through legislative amendments.

## Q13: Features of Right to Equality & Gandhian Ideology
**a) Right to Equality:**
The Right to Equality is a cornerstone of the Indian Constitution, ensuring fairness, justice, and non-discrimination. It encompasses several crucial elements.
1. Equality before the Law (Article 14): This principle states that the law applies equally to all persons, irrespective of their status, wealth, or power. It ensures that the state shall not deny any person equality before the law within the territory of India.
2. Prohibition of Discrimination (Article 15): The state is forbidden from discriminating against citizens on arbitrary grounds like religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. It guarantees equal access to public places like shops, restaurants, and wells.
3. Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment (Article 16): This article guarantees equal opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment under the state. While it prohibits discrimination, it also allows the state to make provisions for the reservation of appointments or posts in favor of any backward class of citizens not adequately represented in state services.
4. Abolition of Untouchability (Article 17): This represents a historic step toward social justice, abolishing the practice of untouchability in all its forms and making its practice a punishable offense.
5. Abolition of Titles (Article 18): To ensure a society based on equality, this article prohibits the state from conferring titles, except military or academic ones, preventing the creation of an aristocratic class.

**b) Gandhian Ideology in Directive Principles:**
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) incorporate several principles inspired by the ideology and programs advocated by Mahatma Gandhi during the national movement. These "Gandhian Principles" aim to realize a socio-economic structure envisioned by Gandhi.
1. Organization of Village Panchayats (Article 40): Gandhi believed that "India lives in its villages." This article directs the state to organize village panchayats and endow them with necessary powers to function as units of self-government, promoting decentralized grassroots democracy.
2. Promotion of Cottage Industries (Article 43): To achieve self-reliance and alleviate rural unemployment, this principle directs the state to promote cottage industries on an individual or co-operative basis in rural areas.
3. Promotion of Educational and Economic Interests (Article 46): Gandhi championed the cause of the marginalized. This article directs the state to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, particularly the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, protecting them from social injustice and exploitation.
4. Prohibition of Intoxicating Drinks and Drugs (Article 47): Reflecting Gandhi's advocacy for prohibition, this principle urges the state to endeavor to bring about prohibition of the consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs that are injurious to health.
5. Prohibition of Cow Slaughter and Improvement of Animal Husbandry (Article 48): Gandhi revered the cow and emphasized rural agriculture. This article directs the state to organize agriculture and animal husbandry on modern lines and specifically take steps to prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves, and other milch and draught cattle.

## Q14: Fundamental Duties & Features of DPSP
**a) Fundamental Duties:**
Added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment in 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee (and later expanded by the 86th Amendment), the Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) serve as a moral obligation on all citizens to help build a strong, united, and civilized nation. Though non-justiciable (not legally enforceable by courts), they are vital for fostering patriotism and civic responsibility.
1. Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem.
2. Cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
3. Uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India.
4. Defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
5. Promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
6. Value and preserve the rich heritage of the country's composite culture.
7. Protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
8. Develop the scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
9. Safeguard public property and abjure violence.
10. Strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavor and achievement.
11. (Added later) Provide opportunities for education to his child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years.

**b) Features of Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP):**
Contained in Part IV of the Constitution, the DPSP are guidelines or instructions for the state to keep in mind while formulating policies and enacting laws.
1. Non-Justiciable: The most prominent feature is that they are not enforceable by any court. If the state fails to implement them, a citizen cannot take the state to court. However, they are fundamental in the governance of the country.
2. Socio-Economic Objectives: Unlike Fundamental Rights, which primarily aim at establishing political democracy, DPSPs aim at establishing social and economic democracy. They embody the concept of a 'Welfare State', moving away from a 'Police State'.
3. Welfare of the People: The overarching goal of the DPSPs is to promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order permeated by justice—social, economic, and political.
4. Balance with Fundamental Rights: The Constitution seeks to maintain a harmonious balance between the rights of the individual (Fundamental Rights) and the needs of the society as envisioned in the DPSPs. Courts often try to interpret laws in a way that harmonizes the two.
5. Broad and Flexible: The principles are broad guidelines rather than rigid rules, allowing successive governments with different political ideologies to interpret and implement them according to changing times and economic conditions.
6. Interpretation by Courts: While non-justiciable, courts use DPSPs as a beacon light while determining the constitutional validity of laws. If a law seeks to implement a directive principle, courts generally tend to view it favorably in terms of public interest.
7. Economic and Social Democracy: By directing the state to minimize inequalities in income, ensure equal pay for equal work, and provide a decent standard of living, the DPSPs lay the foundation for true economic and social democracy.

## Q15: Election & Removal of the President
**a) Election Procedure of the President:**
The President of India is the head of the state and the first citizen of India. Given the parliamentary form of government, the President is elected indirectly to ensure that they remain a nominal head, acting on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, rather than a rival power center to the Prime Minister.
1. Electoral College: The President is elected by an Electoral College. This college consists exclusively of elected members. It includes the elected members of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States (including the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Puducherry). Nominated members do not participate in the election.
2. Eligibility: A candidate must be a citizen of India, at least 35 years of age, and qualified for election as a member of the Lok Sabha. Furthermore, they must not hold any office of profit under the government.
3. System of Voting: The election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. The voting is conducted by secret ballot. This system ensures that the successful candidate secures an absolute majority of votes.
4. Value of Votes: To secure uniformity among states and parity between the states as a whole and the Union, the value of the vote of an MLA is calculated based on the state's population. The total value of votes of all MLAs is equal to the total value of votes of all MPs.
5. Winning Quota: A candidate must secure a specific quota of votes to be declared elected. The quota is determined by dividing the total number of valid votes polled by two and adding one to the quotient.

**b) Removal of the President (Impeachment):**
The President can be removed from office before the expiry of their term only through a rigorous process of impeachment for "violation of the Constitution."
1. Initiation: An impeachment motion can be initiated in either House of Parliament (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha).
2. Requirement for Introduction: The charge must be contained in a resolution. To introduce this resolution, a 14-day advance notice in writing must be given, and the notice must be signed by at least one-fourth of the total number of members of the House initiating the charge.
3. Passage in Initiating House: The resolution must be passed by a special majority, meaning a majority of not less than two-thirds of the total membership of that House.
4. Investigation by the Other House: Once passed by the initiating House, the charge is sent to the other House. The second House must investigate the charge or cause the charge to be investigated. During this investigation, the President has the right to appear and be represented.
5. Final Resolution: If, as a result of the investigation, the second House also passes a resolution by a majority of not less than two-thirds of its total membership declaring that the charge has been sustained, the President is removed from office from the date on which the resolution is passed.

## Q16: Prime Minister & Appeal by Special Leave
**a) Role and Functions of the Prime Minister:**
In the Indian parliamentary democracy, the Prime Minister is the real executive authority and the head of the government. The Prime Minister's office is the most powerful and influential political office in the country.
1. Head of the Government: The Prime Minister leads the government, exercises real executive authority, and is responsible for the overall administration of the country. They are the de facto ruler of the state.
2. Cabinet Leadership: The Prime Minister forms the Council of Ministers. They advise the President on the appointment of other ministers and allocate ministerial portfolios. The Prime Minister can reshuffle portfolios or ask a minister to resign. They preside over cabinet meetings and direct the policy agenda of the government.
3. Legislative Role: As the leader of the majority party in the Lok Sabha, the Prime Minister plays a crucial role in guiding government bills and policies through Parliament. They announce major government policies on the floor of the House and defend government actions during debates.
4. Advisor to the President: The Prime Minister acts as the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers. It is the duty of the Prime Minister to communicate all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of affairs of the Union to the President and to provide any information the President may call for.
5. Coordination: The Prime Minister coordinates the work of various government departments and ministries, ensuring policy coherence and resolving inter-departmental conflicts.
6. Alliance Building: In an era of coalition politics, the Prime Minister is often tasked with managing political coalitions, ensuring the support of allied parties, and maintaining legislative majorities to keep the government stable.

**b) Appeal by Special Leave (Article 136):**
The Supreme Court of India is granted a vast and discretionary appellate jurisdiction under Article 136 of the Constitution, known as the "Special Leave to Appeal."
1. Discretionary Power: Unlike an ordinary appeal which might be a matter of right under certain laws, an appeal by special leave is entirely at the discretion of the Supreme Court. An aggrieved party cannot claim it as a fundamental right.
2. Scope: The Supreme Court may grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination, sentence, or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India. This means it can bypass the regular hierarchy of appeals if it deems fit.
3. Exceptions: The only exception is that it does not apply to judgments, determinations, or orders passed by any court or tribunal constituted by or under any law relating to the Armed Forces.
4. Purpose: This power is exercised to ensure that justice is done and to correct grave miscarriages of justice, especially in certain cases where lower courts or tribunals might have erred significantly in interpreting the law or assessing facts. It serves as an ultimate safety valve in the Indian judicial system.

## Q17: MLA Qualifications & Duties of Advocate General
**a) Qualification and Disqualification of MLA:**
The functioning of a State Legislative Assembly requires capable representatives. The Constitution outlines specific criteria for holding the office of an MLA.
**Qualifications:**
1. Citizenship: The candidate must be a citizen of India.
2. Minimum Age: The candidate must have attained a minimum age, which is 25 years for the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha).
3. Voter Registration: The candidate must be registered as an elector (voter) in any constituency within the state where they are contesting.
4. Mental Soundness: The candidate must be of sound mind and capable of understanding the responsibilities of the office.
**Disqualifications:**
A person can be disqualified from being an MLA under various grounds mentioned in the Constitution and the Representation of the People Act, 1951:
1. Holding an Office of Profit: A person is disqualified if they hold any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of any State, unless that office is exempted by a specific law passed by the state legislature.
2. Undischarged Insolvency: Being an undischarged insolvent (bankrupt) disqualifies a person.
3. Unsound Mind: If declared to be of unsound mind by a competent court.
4. Non-Citizenship: If a person is not a citizen of India, or has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign state, or is under any acknowledgment of allegiance to a foreign state.
5. Criminal Conviction: Conviction for certain specific offenses, such as promoting enmity between different groups, bribery, or heinous crimes, leads to disqualification. Moreover, imprisonment for two or more years generally leads to disqualification for a period of six years from the date of release.
6. Defection (Anti-Defection Law): Under the Tenth Schedule, a member is disqualified if they voluntarily give up the membership of their political party or disobey the party whip (vote against the party directive) in the legislature without prior permission.

**b) Duties and Functions of Advocate General:**
The Advocate General is the highest law officer in a state in India, corresponding to the Attorney General of India at the central level. Appointed by the Governor under Article 165, they play a crucial role in the state's legal affairs.
**Duties:**
1. Legal Advisor: The primary duty is to give advice to the Government of the State upon such legal matters as may be referred to them by the Governor.
2. Representing State Government: They represent the State Government in important cases before the High Court or the Supreme Court where the state is a party.
3. Defending State Interests: They protect and defend the legal and constitutional rights and interests of the state.
4. Providing Legal Opinions: Providing formal legal opinions to various government departments on complex legal issues, draft bills, and policy matters.
5. Legislative Role: They have the right to speak in, and take part in the proceedings of, the State Legislative Assembly (or both Houses if a Legislative Council exists) without the right to vote.
**Functions:**
1. Litigation Management: Overseeing and managing the major litigation involving the state government.
2. Legal Drafting: Assisting in the drafting of important legislation, ordinances, and legal documents on behalf of the state.
3. Court Appearances: Making regular appearances in constitutional and significant legal matters.
4. Protection of State's Rights: Ensuring that the state's legislative and executive powers are not encroached upon unconstitutionally.

## Q18: High Court Jurisdictions & Executive Powers
**a) Original and Writ Jurisdiction of High Courts:**
High Courts are the highest judicial bodies at the state level in India, possessing extensive jurisdictions to administer justice and uphold the Constitution.
**Original Jurisdiction (3 Marks):**
Original jurisdiction refers to the power of a High Court to hear certain cases directly in the first instance, rather than through an appeal from a lower court. While High Courts primarily function as appellate courts, their original jurisdiction is significant. It typically encompasses cases involving disputes regarding the election of Members of Parliament or State Legislatures, matters related to revenue collection, admiralty, contempt of court, and significant civil cases in High Courts like Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras which possess ordinary original civil jurisdiction over their respective metropolitan areas. Most importantly, it involves cases that deal with the enforcement of fundamental rights and substantial questions of constitutional interpretation.
**Writ Jurisdiction (3 Marks):**
Under Article 226 of the Constitution, High Courts have an expansive writ jurisdiction. This is the power to issue prerogative writs to enforce Fundamental Rights and for "any other purpose." This makes the writ jurisdiction of High Courts wider than that of the Supreme Court (which issues writs only for fundamental rights under Article 32). The primary writs are:
1. Habeas Corpus: "To have the body." Issued to protect personal liberty against unlawful detention, directing authorities to present the detained person in court.
2. Mandamus: "We command." Issued to a public authority or government official directing them to perform their statutory or public duty.
3. Certiorari: "To be certified." Issued to a lower court or tribunal to transfer a case to itself or to quash an order passed by the lower authority due to excess of jurisdiction or error of law.
4. Prohibition: Issued to a lower court or tribunal forbidding it from proceeding with a case outside its jurisdiction.
5. Quo Warranto: "By what authority." Issued to inquire into the legality of a person's claim to a public office.

**b) Powers of the Governor (Any 4 points):**
The Governor is the constitutional head of a state, acting similarly to the President at the central level. Their powers are categorized broadly.
1. Executive Powers: All executive actions of the state government are formally taken in the Governor's name. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on the CM's advice, other ministers. They also appoint the Advocate General, members of the State Public Service Commission, and the State Election Commissioner.
2. Legislative Powers: The Governor summons, prorogues, and can dissolve the State Legislative Assembly. They address the first session of the legislature every year. No bill passed by the state legislature can become law without the Governor's assent. The Governor can promulgate Ordinances when the legislature is not in session.
3. Financial Powers: The Annual State Budget (Annual Financial Statement) is laid before the state legislature with the Governor's prior permission. Money bills can be introduced only on the recommendation of the Governor.
4. Veto Powers: When a bill is presented, the Governor can give assent, withhold assent, return the bill (if not a money bill) for reconsideration, or reserve the bill for the consideration of the President.
5. Discretionary Powers: While normally acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers, the Governor has certain discretionary powers, such as selecting a Chief Minister when no party has a clear majority, dismissing a government that has lost confidence, or reporting to the President regarding the breakdown of constitutional machinery in the state.

## Q19: Parliament Legislating on State List & Central Assistance
**a) Parliament's Power to Legislate on State List:**
The Indian Constitution embodies a federal structure with a division of legislative powers between the Union (Parliament) and the States (State Legislatures) detailed in the Seventh Schedule (Union List, State List, and Concurrent List). Normally, Parliament cannot legislate on subjects in the State List. However, to ensure flexibility and address extraordinary situations, the Constitution provides five exceptional circumstances under which Parliament assumes the power to legislate on State List matters.
1. National Interest (Article 249): If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest that Parliament should make laws with respect to a matter in the State List, Parliament becomes empowered to legislate on that subject. Such a resolution remains in force for up to one year.
2. Proclamation of National Emergency (Article 250): While a Proclamation of National Emergency (under Article 352) is in operation, the Parliament acquires the concurrent power to legislate on any matter enumerated in the State List for the whole or any part of the territory of India.
3. Request by States (Article 252): If the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions requesting Parliament to enact a law on a matter in the State List, Parliament can legislate on that subject. However, such a law applies only to the states that passed the resolution and any other state that subsequently adopts it.
4. Implementation of International Agreements (Article 253): To honor international obligations, Parliament has the exclusive power to make any law for the whole or any part of the territory of India for implementing any treaty, agreement, or convention with any other country or international body, even if the subject matter falls within the State List.
5. State Emergency/President's Rule (Article 356): When a state is under President's Rule due to the failure of constitutional machinery, the President can declare that the powers of the State Legislature shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament.

**b) Forms of Central Assistance to States:**
In India's federal financial framework, the states often face a mismatch between their revenue-generating capacity and their expenditure responsibilities. To address this vertical imbalance and promote balanced regional development, the Central Government provides various forms of financial assistance.
1. Central Assistance for State Plans: Historically, the Planning Commission (now NITI Aayog) allocated funds to support the developmental plans formulated by state governments.
2. Support for Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS): These are schemes designed and funded partly by the Central Government but implemented by the State Governments (e.g., MGNREGA, Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana). The center provides a significant portion of the funding.
3. Special Category States: Certain states facing geographical and socio-economic disadvantages (like hilly terrain or strategic international borders) are granted 'Special Category Status'. They receive central assistance on more favorable terms, usually a higher proportion of grants compared to loans.
4. Revenue Deficit Grants: Based on the recommendations of the Finance Commission, the center provides statutory grants (under Article 275) to states that are determined to be in need of assistance to cover their post-devolution revenue deficits.
5. Disaster Relief: The center provides financial assistance to states from the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF) to help them manage and recover from severe natural calamities like floods, cyclones, and earthquakes.

## Q20: Emergencies & Inter-State Council
**a) Emergency Provisions:**
The Indian Constitution contains elaborate emergency provisions in Part XVIII to enable the federal government to acquire unitary powers to handle extraordinary crises threatening the security, stability, or financial integrity of the nation. There are three types of emergencies:
1. National Emergency (Article 352): The President can proclaim a National Emergency if the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. Once declared, the federal structure transforms into a unitary one. The center gains executive control over state administrations, and Parliament is empowered to make laws on any subject in the State List. Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended, and the right to move courts for the enforcement of other fundamental rights (except Articles 20 and 21) can also be suspended by a presidential order.
2. State Emergency or President's Rule (Article 356): If the President, upon receiving a report from the Governor of a state or otherwise, is satisfied that a situation has arisen where the government of the state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution, they can impose President's Rule. The state's elected government is dismissed, the state legislature is either suspended or dissolved, and the Governor administers the state on behalf of the President. Parliament takes over the legislative powers of the state.
3. Financial Emergency (Article 360): If the President is satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened, they can declare a Financial Emergency. During this period, the center can direct states to observe specified canons of financial propriety, reduce the salaries of government officials (including judges), and require all money bills passed by state legislatures to be reserved for the President's consideration.

**b) Functions of the Inter-State Council:**
To promote cooperative federalism and resolve disputes between the Union and the States, or among the States themselves, Article 263 of the Constitution provides for the establishment of an Inter-State Council. The Council is a recommendatory body.
1. Dispute Resolution: One of its primary functions is to inquire into and advise upon disputes which may have arisen between states. It acts as an institutional mechanism to defuse tensions and find amicable solutions outside the judicial process.
2. Deliberations on Common Subjects: It investigates and discusses subjects in which some or all of the states, or the Union and one or more of the states, have a common interest. This facilitates policy coordination on issues with inter-state ramifications, such as river water sharing or internal security.
3. Recommendations on Policy Matters: The Council makes recommendations on any such subject and, in particular, recommendations for the better coordination of policy and action with respect to that subject.
4. Advising on Center-State Relations: It serves as a vital forum for regular consultation between the central government and state governments on broader aspects of center-state relations, helping to build consensus on national policies.
5. Reviewing Work of Commissions and Committees: The Council can also review the implementation of recommendations made by various commissions related to center-state relations, such as the Sarkaria Commission or the Punchhi Commission, ensuring continuous improvement in federal governance.
