# PART B - 14 Mark Questions (Expanded)

**Module - 1**

**Q11 a) Explain in detail the salient features of the Indian constitution. (10 marks)**
**Q11 b) Explain the term partly rigid and partly flexible constitution. (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
The Constitution of India, adopted by the Constituent Assembly on November 26, 1949, and coming into full effect on January 26, 1950, is the supreme law of the land. It possesses several salient features that distinguish it from other constitutions around the world, reflecting the unique socio-cultural, historical, and political context of India.
Firstly, it is the lengthiest written constitution in the world. Originally comprising a Preamble, 395 Articles divided into 22 Parts, and 8 Schedules, it has grown over time due to various amendments. This sheer size is attributed to the vast diversity of the country, the incorporation of fundamental rights, directive principles, detailed administrative provisions, and special provisions for certain classes and regions. It is a comprehensive document that leaves very little to convention, unlike the unwritten British Constitution.
Secondly, it establishes India as a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, Republic. Sovereignty means India is internally supreme and externally free from control. Socialism aims for a welfare state, reducing economic disparities. Secularism implies that the state treats all religions equally and has no official religion. Democracy ensures a government elected by the people, and Republic means the head of state (the President) is elected, not a hereditary monarch.
Thirdly, the Constitution provides for a Parliamentary form of government both at the Centre and in the States, inspired by the British model. In this system, the executive is part of and accountable to the legislature. The President is the nominal executive head, while real executive power vests in the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. This system ensures continuous accountability of the executive to the representatives of the people.
Fourthly, it features a unique blend of federalism with a strong unitary bias. It establishes a dual polity with a division of powers between the Centre and the States, a written constitution, supremacy of the constitution, rigidity, an independent judiciary, and bicameralism—all typical federal features. However, it also contains strong unitary features such as a single citizenship, a unified judiciary, the appointment of state governors by the Centre, and emergency provisions that can transform the federal structure into a unitary one during crises. This has led scholars to describe it as "quasi-federal."
Fifthly, the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights to all citizens, enshrined in Part III. These are justiciable rights, meaning they are enforceable by courts, protecting citizens against arbitrary state action. It also includes Directive Principles of State Policy in Part IV, which are non-justiciable guidelines for the state to promote social and economic welfare. Furthermore, Fundamental Duties were added in Part IVA, reminding citizens of their obligations towards the nation. An independent and integrated judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court, is another crucial feature, acting as the guardian of the Constitution and fundamental rights.
Regarding the term "partly rigid and partly flexible," this refers to the amending process of the Constitution. A rigid constitution requires a special procedure for amendment, like the American Constitution, while a flexible one can be amended by ordinary legislative process, like the British Constitution. The Indian Constitution strikes a balance. Some provisions can be amended by a simple majority in Parliament, indicating flexibility. These include changes to state boundaries or creating legislative councils. Most provisions, however, require a special majority—a majority of the total membership of each House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting. This indicates rigidity. Furthermore, amendments affecting the federal structure require not only a special majority in Parliament but also ratification by the legislatures of at least half of the states, making the process highly rigid. Thus, the Indian Constitution is an exceptional synthesis of rigidity and flexibility, allowing it to adapt to changing times while protecting its core framework.


**Q12 a) Explain the mode of acquisition and termination of citizenship. (10 marks)**
**Q12 b) Explain the term Union and its territory (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
Citizenship in India determines the legal relationship between an individual and the state, conferring specific rights, such as voting and holding public office, along with corresponding duties. The Constitution of India deals with citizenship in Part II (Articles 5 to 11) only at the commencement of the Constitution on January 26, 1950. For matters arising thereafter, Article 11 empowered Parliament to enact laws, resulting in the Citizenship Act, 1955. This Act, amended several times, details the modes of acquisition and termination of Indian citizenship.

**Acquisition of Citizenship:** The Citizenship Act provides five primary ways to acquire Indian citizenship. 
1. **By Birth (Jus Soli):** Any person born in India on or after January 26, 1950, but before July 1, 1987, is a citizen by birth irrespective of their parents' nationality. For those born between July 1, 1987, and December 3, 2004, they are considered citizens if at least one parent is a citizen at the time of birth. For births on or after December 3, 2004, citizenship is granted if both parents are Indian citizens, or one is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant. 
2. **By Descent (Jus Sanguinis):** A person born outside India on or after January 26, 1950, acquires citizenship by descent if their father was a citizen of India at the time of their birth. Later amendments mandated that the birth must be registered at an Indian consulate within one year. From December 3, 2004 onwards, descent-based citizenship requires registration and a declaration that the minor does not hold another country's passport.
3. **By Registration:** The Central Government may, on an application, register as a citizen any person (not being an illegal migrant) if they belong to specific categories. This includes persons of Indian origin residing in India for seven years before applying, persons of Indian origin resident in any country outside undivided India, persons married to an Indian citizen and ordinarily resident in India for seven years, and minor children of Indian citizens.
4. **By Naturalization:** A foreigner (not an illegal migrant) can acquire citizenship by naturalization if they fulfill certain qualifications, such as not belonging to a country where Indians are prevented from becoming naturalized citizens, renouncing their previous citizenship, residing in India for a specified period (typically 11 years out of the previous 14 years), being of good character, having an adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule, and intending to reside in India.
5. **By Incorporation of Territory:** If a new territory becomes a part of India, the Government of India specifies the persons from that territory who shall be citizens of India. For instance, when Puducherry and Goa were incorporated, their residents were granted citizenship.

**Termination of Citizenship:** The Act also specifies three ways Indian citizenship can be lost.
1. **By Renunciation:** Any citizen of India of full age and capacity can make a declaration renouncing their Indian citizenship. Upon registration of that declaration, the person ceases to be a citizen. If made during a war in which India is engaged, its registration is withheld.
2. **By Termination:** If an Indian citizen voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, their Indian citizenship automatically terminates. This provision does not apply during a war in which India is engaged.
3. **By Deprivation:** This is a compulsory termination of citizenship by the Central Government. It applies to citizens who acquired citizenship by naturalization or registration. Deprivation can occur if citizenship was obtained by fraud, false representation, or concealment of material facts, if the citizen has shown disloyalty to the Constitution, if they have unlawfully communicated with an enemy during war, or if they have been sentenced to imprisonment in any country for two years within five years of naturalization.

**Union and its Territory:** The term "Union and its Territory" is described in Part I of the Constitution (Articles 1 to 4). Article 1 states that "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." The term "Union" indicates that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among the states (unlike the USA), and no state has the right to secede from the federation. It signifies indissolubility. The "Territory of India" is a wider expression than the "Union of India." While the "Union of India" includes only the states that share a federal relationship with the Centre and share powers, the "Territory of India" encompasses the entire geographical area over which the sovereignty of India extends. This includes the territories of the States, the Union Territories specified in the First Schedule, and any other territories that may be acquired by India in the future. Articles 2 and 3 empower Parliament to admit new states into the Union, establish new states, and alter the boundaries, areas, or names of existing states, highlighting the indestructible nature of the Union despite the destructible nature of its constituent states.


**Module - 2**

**Q13 a) Explain the meaning, significance and classification of the Directive Principles of State Policy. (10 marks)**
**Q13 b) "The freedom of speech and expression is subject to reasonable restrictions". Explain the statement. (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are enumerated in Part IV of the Indian Constitution, from Articles 36 to 51. They are fundamental guidelines and principles given to the federal and state governments to keep in mind while framing laws and policies.

**Meaning of DPSP:** The term 'Directive Principles of State Policy' implies that these are directives or instructions to the State—which includes both the central and state governments, as well as local authorities—to formulate policies that promote the social and economic welfare of the people. They embody the concept of a 'welfare state' rather than a 'police state'. Unlike Fundamental Rights, which are negative injunctions preventing the state from doing certain things, Directive Principles are positive obligations requiring the state to take active steps to achieve specific socio-economic goals. A crucial characteristic of DPSP, as stated in Article 37, is that they are non-justiciable. This means that they cannot be enforced by any court of law if the government fails to implement them. However, Article 37 also declares that these principles are fundamental in the governance of the country, and it shall be the duty of the state to apply them in making laws.

**Significance of DPSP:** Despite being non-justiciable, the DPSP hold immense significance in the Indian constitutional scheme. Firstly, they lay down the socio-economic foundation of a democratic India. They aim to establish economic and social democracy, which is essential for the success of political democracy guaranteed by Fundamental Rights. They strive to bridge the gap between the rich and the poor, ensuring equitable distribution of resources. Secondly, they serve as a moral compass and a yardstick for evaluating the performance of the government. Citizens and the opposition can use these principles to hold the ruling party accountable during elections. They provide a continuous reminder to the state of its responsibilities towards the marginalized and vulnerable sections of society. Thirdly, they guide the judiciary in interpreting the Constitution. Courts often rely on DPSP to determine the constitutional validity of laws, particularly to understand the underlying philosophy and public interest behind legislation. Over time, the Supreme Court has ruled that a harmonious construction between Fundamental Rights and DPSP must be maintained, as both are integral to the core of the Constitution.

**Classification of DPSP:** While the Constitution does not formally classify the DPSP, they are generally categorized into three broad groups based on their ideological source and objectives:
1. **Socialistic Principles:** These aim to establish a welfare state by promoting social and economic justice. They direct the state to minimize inequalities in income, status, facilities, and opportunities (Article 38). They also call for adequate means of livelihood, equitable distribution of material resources, equal pay for equal work for men and women, protection of the health and strength of workers, and the right to work, education, and public assistance in cases of unemployment or old age (Articles 39, 41, 42, 43).
2. **Gandhian Principles:** These reflect the ideology of Mahatma Gandhi and his vision of rural reconstruction. They direct the state to organize village panchayats as units of self-government (Article 40), promote cottage industries (Article 43), promote the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and other weaker sections (Article 46), prohibit the consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs (Article 47), and prohibit the slaughter of cows and calves (Article 48).
3. **Liberal-Intellectual Principles:** These principles reflect a liberal ideology and modern progressive thinking. They direct the state to secure a Uniform Civil Code for all citizens (Article 44), provide early childhood care and education for all children until they complete the age of six years (Article 45), organize agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines (Article 48), protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife (Article 48A), protect monuments of national importance (Article 49), separate the judiciary from the executive (Article 50), and promote international peace and security (Article 51).

**Freedom of Speech and Reasonable Restrictions:** Article 19(1)(a) of the Indian Constitution guarantees all citizens the right to freedom of speech and expression. This is a vital democratic right, allowing individuals to express their views freely through word of mouth, writing, printing, or any other medium. It is essential for the discovery of truth, self-fulfillment, and participation in decision-making. However, no right can be absolute in a civilized society. Therefore, the statement "The freedom of speech and expression is subject to reasonable restrictions" is deeply embedded in the Constitution itself under Article 19(2). 
The state can impose "reasonable restrictions" on the exercise of this right on specific grounds. These grounds are sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, and incitement to an offense. The term "reasonable" is crucial; it means the restriction must not be arbitrary or excessive and must strike a balance between individual liberty and social control. The judiciary has the final say in determining whether a restriction imposed by the state is reasonable or not, ensuring that the government does not use these grounds to stifle legitimate dissent or criticism. Thus, while citizens have the freedom to speak their minds, they cannot use this freedom to incite violence, harm national security, or defame others.


**Q14 a) Explain in detail the Fundamental Rights of Indian citizens. (10 marks)**
**Q14 b) The Indian constitution is unique in protecting the minorities. Explain? (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
Fundamental Rights are a cornerstone of the Indian Constitution, enshrined in Part III from Articles 12 to 35. These are essential human rights guaranteed to all citizens (and in some cases, non-citizens) to protect them from arbitrary actions by the state. They act as limitations on the power of the legislature and executive, ensuring that the government respects the basic liberties of the individual. They are "fundamental" because they are justiciable; if these rights are violated, an individual can directly approach the Supreme Court (under Article 32) or the High Courts (under Article 226) for their enforcement. The Constitution guarantees six broad categories of Fundamental Rights:

1. **Right to Equality (Articles 14-18):** This forms the bedrock of democracy. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws to all persons within the territory of India, meaning the state cannot treat equals unequally. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, ensuring that the state does not show favoritism. Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity in matters of public employment, with provisions for affirmative action (reservations) for backward classes. Article 17 abolishes "untouchability" and makes its practice in any form a punishable offense. Article 18 abolishes the conferment of titles (like Maharaja or Rai Bahadur) by the state, except for military or academic distinctions, to promote social equality.

2. **Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22):** This cluster protects personal liberties. Article 19 guarantees six basic freedoms to citizens: freedom of speech and expression, assembly without arms, forming associations or unions, free movement throughout India, residing and settling in any part of India, and practicing any profession or carrying on any occupation. However, these are not absolute and are subject to reasonable restrictions. Article 20 provides protection in respect of conviction for offenses, safeguarding against ex-post facto laws, double jeopardy, and self-incrimination. Article 21 is perhaps the most crucial, declaring that no person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. The judiciary has expansively interpreted this to include the right to privacy, a clean environment, and a dignified life. Article 21A guarantees the right to free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14. Article 22 provides protection against arbitrary arrest and detention, laying down procedures that must be followed by authorities.

3. **Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24):** These rights protect the vulnerable. Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, begar (forced labor), and other similar forms of forced labor. Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below the age of 14 years in factories, mines, or any other hazardous employment, aiming to protect children from exploitation.

4. **Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28):** These rights establish India as a secular state. Article 25 guarantees freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practice, and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, and health. Article 26 gives every religious denomination the right to manage its own affairs in matters of religion and own/acquire property. Article 27 ensures that no person shall be compelled to pay taxes for the promotion of any particular religion. Article 28 prohibits religious instruction in educational institutions wholly maintained out of state funds.

5. **Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30):** These are specifically designed to protect the interests of minorities. Article 29 guarantees the right of any section of citizens residing in India having a distinct language, script, or culture of its own to conserve the same. Article 30 grants all minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

6. **Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32):** Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called this the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. It gives individuals the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court has the power to issue directions or orders or writs, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate.

**Protection of Minorities:** The Indian Constitution is indeed unique in its robust mechanisms for protecting minority communities. This uniqueness stems from the recognition of India's immense diversity and the need to assure minorities that their distinct identities will be preserved in a majoritarian democracy. The protection is primarily enshrined in the Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29 and 30). Unlike many constitutions that only provide negative protections against discrimination, the Indian Constitution provides positive rights to minorities. Article 29 allows any section of citizens with a distinct language, script, or culture to actively conserve it. More significantly, Article 30 empowers religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer their own educational institutions. This ensures that minority communities can transmit their culture, religion, and language to future generations through their own institutions. Furthermore, Article 350A directs the state to provide adequate facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage of education to children belonging to linguistic minority groups. A Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities is appointed under Article 350B to investigate matters relating to the safeguards provided for them. This comprehensive framework not only prevents assimilation into the majority culture but actively supports the flourishing of minority identities, making the Indian approach distinct and highly protective.

**Q15 a) What are the qualifications and disqualifications that the Indian Constitution specifies for someone to become a Member of Parliament? (10 marks)**
**Q15 b) Mention any two powers and functions of Indian President. (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
The Parliament of India consists of the President and two Houses: the Rajya Sabha (Council of States) and the Lok Sabha (House of the People). To ensure that the legislature is composed of capable and responsible representatives, the Constitution, along with the Representation of the People Act (RPA), 1951, lays down specific qualifications and disqualifications for membership.

**Qualifications for a Member of Parliament (MP):** Under Article 84 of the Constitution, to be qualified to be chosen to fill a seat in Parliament, a person must fulfill the following fundamental criteria:
1.  **Citizenship:** The person must be a citizen of India and make and subscribe before some person authorized in that behalf by the Election Commission an oath or affirmation according to the form set out for the purpose in the Third Schedule. This establishes a fundamental allegiance to the nation.
2.  **Age Requirement:** There is a distinct age requirement for each house. In the case of a seat in the Rajya Sabha, the candidate must be not less than thirty years of age. In the case of a seat in the Lok Sabha, the candidate must be not less than twenty-five years of age. This ensures a certain level of maturity and experience in the legislators.
3.  **Other Qualifications prescribed by Parliament:** The person must possess such other qualifications as may be prescribed in that behalf by or under any law made by Parliament. Parliament enacted the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which added further statutory qualifications. For instance, a candidate must be registered as an elector for a parliamentary constituency. In the case of a seat reserved for the Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes, the candidate must belong to those categories.

**Disqualifications for a Member of Parliament:** Article 102 of the Constitution details the grounds on which a person shall be disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member of either House of Parliament:
1.  **Office of Profit:** If a person holds any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of any State, other than an office declared by Parliament by law not to disqualify its holder. This principle ensures the independence of legislators from executive influence and prevents a conflict of interest.
2.  **Unsound Mind:** If the person is of unsound mind and stands so declared by a competent court. A sound mental state is essential for undertaking legislative duties.
3.  **Insolvent:** If the person is an undischarged insolvent. Financial stability is considered necessary for a representative.
4.  **Citizenship Issues:** If the person is not a citizen of India, or has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State, or is under any acknowledgment of allegiance or adherence to a foreign State.
5.  **Disqualification under any law made by Parliament:** Parliament has expanded these grounds through the RPA, 1951. Disqualifications under this Act include conviction for certain offenses leading to imprisonment of two years or more, failure to lodge an account of election expenses within the stipulated time, having an interest in government contracts, works, or services, and being dismissed from government service for corruption or disloyalty.
6.  **Defection (Tenth Schedule):** Furthermore, a member can be disqualified on the ground of defection under the Tenth Schedule of the Constitution (Anti-Defection Law). This occurs if a member voluntarily gives up the membership of their political party or votes/abstains from voting contrary to the directives issued by their party. The decision on disqualification under defection is taken by the Chairman (Rajya Sabha) or Speaker (Lok Sabha), while for other grounds, the President decides based on the opinion of the Election Commission.

**Powers and Functions of the Indian President:** The President of India is the head of state and the first citizen of India. While primarily a nominal executive with real powers resting with the Council of Ministers, the President holds significant constitutional authority.
1. **Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the Government of India are formally taken in the President's name. The President appoints the Prime Minister and, on the Prime Minister's advice, other ministers. The President also appoints key constitutional functionaries, including the Attorney General of India, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner, the Chairman and members of the UPSC, and the Governors of states. The President is the Supreme Commander of the defense forces of India.
2. **Legislative Powers:** The President is an integral part of Parliament. The President summons and prorogues the sessions of Parliament and can dissolve the Lok Sabha. The President addresses both Houses assembled together at the commencement of the first session after each general election and the first session of each year. Crucially, a bill passed by Parliament becomes law only after receiving the President's assent. The President can give assent, withhold assent, or return the bill (if it's not a money bill) for reconsideration. Furthermore, when Parliament is not in session, the President can promulgate Ordinances, which have the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament, to deal with urgent matters.

**Q17 a) Explain the powers and functions of Chief Minister and State Legislature in Indian constitution. (10 marks)**
**Q17 b) What are the qualifications needed for governor? (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
The Indian Constitution provides for a parliamentary system of government at the state level, similar to the Centre. The Governor is the constitutional head, while the Chief Minister is the real executive head. The State Legislature constitutes the legislative branch.

**Powers and Functions of the Chief Minister:** The Chief Minister is the head of the state government. Their role is analogous to that of the Prime Minister at the Centre. The powers and functions can be categorized as follows:
1. **In relation to the Council of Ministers:** The Chief Minister is the head of the Council of Ministers. The Governor appoints other ministers only on the advice of the Chief Minister. The CM allocates portfolios among the ministers, shuffles their portfolios, and can ask a minister to resign or advise the Governor to dismiss them. The CM presides over the meetings of the council of ministers and guides, directs, and coordinates the activities of all ministers. The resignation or death of a CM automatically dissolves the council of ministers.
2. **In relation to the Governor:** The CM acts as the primary communication link between the Governor and the council of ministers. Under Article 167, it is the duty of the CM to communicate to the Governor all decisions of the council of ministers relating to the administration of the affairs of the state and proposals for legislation. The CM advises the Governor on the appointment of important officials like the Advocate General, Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission, and the State Election Commissioner.
3. **In relation to the State Legislature:** The CM is the leader of the House (Legislative Assembly). The CM advises the Governor regarding the summoning and proroguing of the sessions of the state legislature. The CM can recommend the dissolution of the Legislative Assembly to the Governor at any time. The CM announces principal government policies on the floor of the House.
4. **Other Powers and Functions:** The CM is the chairman of the State Planning Board and acts as a vice-chairman of the concerned zonal council by rotation. The CM is the chief spokesperson of the state government and plays a vital role in crisis management during emergencies in the state.

**Powers and Functions of the State Legislature:** The State Legislature consists of the Governor and one or two Houses (Legislative Assembly and, in some states, Legislative Council).
1. **Legislative Powers:** The primary function is law-making. The State Legislature has exclusive power to make laws on subjects enumerated in the State List (like police, public health, agriculture) and concurrent power over the Concurrent List (like education, criminal law). However, if there is a conflict between a state law and a central law on a concurrent subject, the central law prevails. 
2. **Financial Powers:** The legislature controls the finances of the state. No tax can be levied or collected, and no expenditure can be incurred without the approval of the State Legislature. The government must present the annual budget (Annual Financial Statement) before the legislature. The Legislative Assembly has dominant financial powers; Money Bills can only be introduced in the Assembly.
3. **Executive Control:** The legislature exercises control over the executive. The council of ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly. The legislature can hold the government accountable through tools like questions, debates, adjournment motions, and calling attention notices. Most importantly, the Assembly can express a lack of confidence in the government by passing a no-confidence motion, forcing the council of ministers to resign.
4. **Electoral and Constituent Powers:** The elected members of the Legislative Assembly participate in the election of the President of India and the members of the Rajya Sabha from the state. Furthermore, some constitutional amendments require ratification by the legislatures of at least half the states before coming into effect.

**Qualifications needed for Governor:** The Governor is the executive head of the state and acts as a representative of the central government. Article 157 and Article 158 of the Constitution lay down the qualifications and conditions for the office of the Governor.
1.  **Citizenship:** The foremost qualification is that the candidate must be a citizen of India. 
2.  **Age:** The candidate must have completed the age of thirty-five years.
These are the only two constitutional qualifications. However, certain conditions of office must be met. The Governor shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of the Legislature of any State specified in the First Schedule. If a member is appointed Governor, they are deemed to have vacated their seat in that House on the date they enter upon the office. Furthermore, the Governor shall not hold any other office of profit. Over time, certain conventions have also developed regarding the appointment of Governors; they are usually eminent personalities from outside the state they are appointed to, ensuring detachment from local politics.

**Q19 a) What are the five extraordinary circumstances on which the Constitution empowers the Parliament to make laws on any matter enumerated in the State list? (10 marks)**
**Q19 b) Explain any two functions of Election Commission of India. (4 marks)**

**Answer:**
The Indian Constitution divides legislative powers between the Union and the States through three lists in the Seventh Schedule: the Union List, the State List, and the Concurrent List. Normally, only State Legislatures have the exclusive power to make laws on subjects enumerated in the State List. However, the Constitution, reflecting its quasi-federal or unitary-biased nature, specifies five extraordinary circumstances under which the Parliament is empowered to legislate on State List subjects.

1.  **When Rajya Sabha passes a resolution (Article 249):** If the Rajya Sabha, the Council of States, declares by a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest that Parliament should make laws with respect to any matter enumerated in the State List specified in the resolution, it becomes lawful for Parliament to make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India with respect to that matter while the resolution remains in force. Such a resolution remains in force for a period not exceeding one year, though it can be renewed. The law made by Parliament ceases to have effect on the expiration of six months after the resolution has ceased to be in force.
2.  **During a National Emergency (Article 250):** While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation (under Article 352), the Parliament acquires the power to make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India with respect to any of the matters enumerated in the State List. The laws made by Parliament under this provision become inoperative on the expiration of six months after the Proclamation of Emergency has ceased to operate. This effectively transforms the federal structure into a unitary one during national crises.
3.  **When States make a request (Article 252):** If the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions to the effect that it is desirable that any of the matters with respect to which Parliament has no power to make laws for the States should be regulated in such States by Parliament by law, it becomes lawful for Parliament to pass an act for regulating that matter accordingly. Any act so passed shall apply to such States and to any other State by which it is adopted afterwards by resolution. Notably, once such a law is passed by Parliament, only Parliament has the power to amend or repeal it, not the state legislatures that originally requested it.
4.  **To implement International Agreements (Article 253):** Parliament has the power to make any law for the whole or any part of the territory of India for implementing any treaty, agreement, or convention with any other country or countries, or any decision made at any international conference, association, or other body. This provision enables the central government to fulfill its international obligations and commitments, even if the subject matter of the treaty falls within the State List. This ensures that the state governments cannot obstruct the implementation of international treaties.
5.  **During President's Rule (Article 356 & 357):** When President's Rule is imposed in a state due to the failure of the constitutional machinery, the President may declare that the powers of the Legislature of the State shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament. Consequently, Parliament makes laws on State List subjects for that specific state. Even after the President's rule ends, laws made by Parliament under this provision continue to be operative until repealed or altered by the state legislature.

**Functions of the Election Commission of India:** The Election Commission of India (ECI) is an autonomous constitutional authority responsible for administering election processes in India at the national, state, and district levels. Article 324 of the Constitution vests the superintendence, direction, and control of elections in the ECI. Two major functions include:
1.  **Conduct of Elections:** The primary function of the ECI is to ensure free and fair elections. It conducts elections to the offices of the President and Vice-President of India, to both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha), and to the State Legislative Assemblies and Councils. This massive logistical exercise involves determining the territorial areas of electoral constituencies throughout the country (delimitation), preparing and periodically revising electoral rolls, registering all eligible voters, notifying the dates and schedules of elections, establishing polling stations, and overseeing the counting of votes and declaration of results.
2.  **Recognition of Political Parties and Model Code of Conduct:** The ECI is responsible for granting recognition to political parties as national or state parties based on their poll performance, and assigning election symbols to them. Crucially, the ECI formulates and enforces the Model Code of Conduct for political parties and candidates to ensure a level playing field and maintain decorum during campaigns. The Code sets guidelines for campaigning, rallies, polling day conduct, and prevents the ruling party from misusing official machinery. The ECI monitors campaigns and takes action against violations to uphold the integrity of the electoral process.
