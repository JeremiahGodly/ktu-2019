# PART A - 3 Mark Questions (Expanded)

**Q1: Define Preamble of Constitution.**
The Preamble is the introductory statement to the Constitution of India, setting out the guiding purpose, principles, and philosophy of the document. It was adopted on 26 November 1949 by the Constituent Assembly and came into effect on 26 January 1950. The Preamble is based on the "Objectives Resolution" drafted and moved by Jawaharlal Nehru. It declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic state. It aims to secure Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and of opportunity), and to promote Fraternity among all citizens, assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation. The words "Socialist", "Secular", and "Integrity" were added by the 42nd Amendment Act in 1976. The Preamble serves as a key to the minds of the makers of the Constitution and helps in interpreting its provisions where the language is ambiguous. It embodies the fundamental values and the philosophy on which the Constitution is based, reflecting the dreams and aspirations of the founding fathers of the nation. Though not enforceable in a court of law, it is a part of the Constitution and forms its basic structure. 

**Q2: List out the sources borrowed while drafting the Constitution of India.**
The Constitution of India is often described as a "bag of borrowings" because its drafters drew inspiration from various constitutions across the world to suit the unique socio-cultural and political context of India. The primary source was the Government of India Act, 1935, which provided the structural framework, including the federal scheme, office of the Governor, judiciary, public service commissions, emergency provisions, and administrative details. From the British Constitution, India adopted the parliamentary system of government, the rule of law, legislative procedures, single citizenship, the cabinet system, prerogative writs, and bicameralism. The US Constitution inspired the fundamental rights, the independence of the judiciary, judicial review, the impeachment of the President, and the removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges, as well as the post of Vice-President. The Irish Constitution provided the Directive Principles of State Policy, the method of presidential election, and the nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha. From the Canadian Constitution, India borrowed the concept of a federation with a strong center, the vesting of residuary powers in the center, and the advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court. The Australian Constitution contributed the Concurrent List, freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse, and the joint sitting of the two houses of Parliament. The Weimar Constitution of Germany influenced the suspension of fundamental rights during an emergency. The Soviet (USSR) Constitution was the source for fundamental duties and the ideals of justice.

**Q3: What is Secularism?**
Secularism in the Indian context refers to the equal treatment of all religions by the state. Unlike the Western concept of secularism, which implies a strict separation between religion and state, Indian secularism is characterized by "Sarva Dharma Samabhava," meaning equal respect for all religions. The state does not have an official religion and maintains a principled distance from religious affairs, ensuring that it neither favors nor discriminates against any particular religion. The word "Secular" was explicitly added to the Preamble of the Indian Constitution by the 42nd Amendment Act of 1976, affirming the nation's commitment to this ideal. The Constitution guarantees religious freedom to all individuals and groups under Articles 25 to 28, allowing them to profess, practice, and propagate their religion subject to public order, morality, and health. Furthermore, Article 15 prohibits discrimination on the grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, ensuring equality before the law. The state also has the authority to intervene in religious matters to eradicate social evils or promote social welfare and reform, such as the abolition of untouchability and the regulation of secular activities associated with religious practices. Indian secularism is essential for maintaining communal harmony, national integration, and democratic values in a highly diverse and pluralistic society. It acts as a unifying force, ensuring that minority communities feel secure and participate equally in the nation-building process.

**Q4: Explain Uniform Civil Code?**
The Uniform Civil Code (UCC) is a proposal to formulate and implement a single set of personal laws that would apply to all citizens of India, regardless of their religion, gender, or sexual orientation. These laws would cover matters such as marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, and succession. Currently, personal laws in India are governed by the religious scriptures and customs of various communities, such as the Hindu Marriage Act, the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, and the Indian Christian Marriage Act. The concept of the UCC is enshrined in Article 44 of the Constitution of India, which is one of the Directive Principles of State Policy. Article 44 states that "The State shall endeavor to secure for the citizens a uniform civil code throughout the territory of India." The primary objective of the UCC is to promote national integration, gender justice, and equality by replacing the fragmented and sometimes discriminatory religious personal laws with a common legal framework. Proponents argue that a UCC would simplify the legal system, ensure equal rights for women who are often disadvantaged under traditional religious laws, and foster a sense of unity among citizens. However, the implementation of the UCC remains a highly debated and sensitive issue. Opponents express concerns that a uniform code might infringe upon the religious freedom guaranteed by Article 25 of the Constitution and could undermine the cultural diversity and distinct identities of minority communities.

**Q5: What qualifications must an individual possess to be appointed as a Supreme Court Judge?**
To be appointed as a Judge of the Supreme Court of India, an individual must fulfill specific qualifications outlined in Article 124(3) of the Constitution. First and foremost, the candidate must be a citizen of India. This is a fundamental prerequisite for holding such a high constitutional office. In addition to citizenship, the candidate must satisfy one of three professional criteria. Firstly, they must have been a judge of a High Court (or of two or more such Courts in succession) for at least five years. Alternatively, the candidate must have been an advocate of a High Court (or of two or more such Courts in succession) for at least ten years. The third and final alternative qualification is that the candidate must be, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist. This provision allows for the appointment of exceptional legal scholars or academicians to the apex court, although in practice, this criterion has rarely been invoked. Furthermore, the Constitution does not prescribe a minimum age for appointment as a Supreme Court judge, but they hold office until they attain the age of 65 years. The appointment is made by the President of India on the recommendation of a collegium headed by the Chief Justice of India and comprising the four senior-most judges of the Supreme Court. These rigorous qualifications ensure that only individuals with extensive legal experience, deep knowledge of the law, and impeccable integrity are elevated to the highest judicial body in the country, maintaining the independence and competence of the judiciary.

**Q6: Describe the constitutional emergency powers granted to the President?**
The Constitution of India vests the President with extraordinary emergency powers to deal with abnormal situations that threaten the security, stability, or financial integrity of the nation. These powers are detailed in Part XVIII of the Constitution and are classified into three types: National Emergency, State Emergency (President's Rule), and Financial Emergency. 
A National Emergency (Article 352) can be declared if the President is satisfied that the security of India or any part of its territory is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. During a National Emergency, the federal structure transforms into a unitary one, and the central government gains sweeping powers over state subjects. Fundamental rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended, and the President can suspend the enforcement of other fundamental rights (except Articles 20 and 21).
A State Emergency or President's Rule (Article 356) is imposed when the President, upon receiving a report from the Governor or otherwise, is satisfied that the government of a state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. This leads to the dismissal of the state government, and the President assumes the executive functions of the state, while the Parliament exercises the legislative powers.
A Financial Emergency (Article 360) can be declared if the President is satisfied that the financial stability or credit of India or any of its parts is threatened. During this emergency, the executive authority of the Union extends to giving financial directions to states.

**Q7: What are the responsibilities carried out by the advocate general of the state?**
The Advocate General is the highest law officer of a state in India, appointed by the Governor under Article 165 of the Constitution. The primary responsibility of the Advocate General is to provide legal advice to the state government on matters referred to them by the Governor. They represent the state government in significant legal proceedings and cases before the High Court and other courts. The Advocate General plays a crucial role in defending the state's policies, laws, and executive actions against legal challenges. They also perform other legal duties assigned by the Governor or the state government, such as drafting important legal documents and reviewing state legislation. Furthermore, the Advocate General has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of the state legislature, though they do not have the right to vote. This allows them to clarify legal positions and provide expert insights during legislative debates. The Advocate General acts as a vital link between the state government and the judiciary, ensuring that the government's actions are legally sound and constitutionally valid. Their responsibilities demand a deep understanding of constitutional and administrative law, as well as the ability to navigate complex legal issues effectively. The office of the Advocate General is analogous to that of the Attorney General of India at the central level, serving as the chief legal advisor and representative of the state.

**Q8: Write short note on Ex-post facto law and No Double Jeopardy.**
Ex-post facto laws and the principle of Double Jeopardy are fundamental safeguards within the Indian Constitution, specifically enshrined under Article 20, which protects individuals from arbitrary legislative and judicial actions. 
An ex-post facto law is a law that imposes penalties retrospectively, meaning it criminalizes acts that were lawful when committed or increases the penalty for a crime after it has been committed. Article 20(1) prohibits the enactment of such laws. It states that no person shall be convicted of any offense except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act, nor subjected to a penalty greater than that which might have been inflicted under the law in force at the time. This protection is limited to criminal laws and does not apply to civil or tax laws, which can be applied retrospectively. 
The principle of "No Double Jeopardy," articulated in Article 20(2), ensures that no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offense more than once. This safeguard prevents the state from using its resources to subject an individual to repeated trials and punishments for a single act, thereby protecting citizens from continuous harassment and ensuring the finality of judicial decisions. For this protection to apply, there must have been a previous prosecution and a resulting punishment by a court of competent jurisdiction. Departmental or disciplinary proceedings do not constitute prosecution in this context, meaning an individual can face both legal prosecution and departmental action for the same misconduct.

**Q9: What are the functions of UPSC?**
The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) is a premier central recruiting agency in India, established as an independent constitutional body under Articles 315 to 323 of the Constitution. Its primary function is to conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the Union, including the All India Services (such as IAS, IPS, and IFS), Central Civil Services, and Defense Services. The UPSC is responsible for ensuring a fair, transparent, and merit-based selection process. 
In addition to recruitment, the UPSC plays a crucial advisory role. It is consulted by the government on various matters relating to personnel management. This includes the methods of recruitment to civil services and civil posts, the principles to be followed in making appointments, promotions, and transfers from one service to another, and the suitability of candidates for such appointments. The UPSC also advises on disciplinary matters affecting civil servants, such as censures, withholding of increments or promotions, and dismissal from service. 
Furthermore, the Commission can be consulted on claims for the reimbursement of legal expenses incurred by a civil servant in defending themselves in legal proceedings related to the execution of their official duties, as well as on claims for the award of pensions in respect of injuries sustained while serving under the government. The UPSC presents an annual report of its work to the President, which is then laid before each House of Parliament, ensuring its accountability and transparency. The independence of the UPSC is vital for maintaining an impartial and efficient civil service.

**Q10: Define National Emergency.**
A National Emergency is a constitutionally mandated state of exception in India, declared under Article 352, which fundamentally alters the normal federal structure and grants extraordinary powers to the central government. The President of India can proclaim a National Emergency if they are satisfied that the security of India or any part of its territory is under grave threat due to war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The term "armed rebellion" replaced the earlier term "internal disturbance" through the 44th Amendment Act of 1978 to prevent the misuse of emergency powers, as occurred in 1975. 
A proclamation of National Emergency must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month of its issuance, by a special majority. Once approved, it remains in force for six months and can be extended indefinitely with parliamentary approval every six months. During a National Emergency, the federal character of the Constitution is suspended, and the state becomes unitary. The Parliament acquires the power to legislate on any subject mentioned in the State List, and the executive power of the Union extends to giving directions to any state regarding the exercise of its executive power. 
Crucially, the declaration of a National Emergency significantly impacts fundamental rights. Under Article 358, the six fundamental rights guaranteed by Article 19 (freedoms of speech, assembly, etc.) are automatically suspended if the emergency is based on war or external aggression. Furthermore, under Article 359, the President can suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of other fundamental rights, with the exception of Articles 20 and 21.
