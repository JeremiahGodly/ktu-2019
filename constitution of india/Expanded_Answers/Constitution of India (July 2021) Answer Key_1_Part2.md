## Module - 1

### 11 a) What is preamble? Explain the importance of preamble in the implementation of constitution. (6 marks)
The Preamble is a brief introductory statement that sets out the guiding purpose, principles, and philosophy of the Constitution of India. Based on the 'Objective Resolution' drafted and moved by Jawaharlal Nehru, it acts as the key to the minds of the makers of the Constitution. It declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic state, and secures to all its citizens Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and of opportunity), and promotes Fraternity (assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation).

The importance of the Preamble in implementing the Constitution cannot be overstated. While it is not enforceable in a court of law (non-justiciable) and does not directly grant substantial power or prohibit power, it holds immense legal and interpretative value. Whenever there is ambiguity or a lack of clarity in any constitutional provision, the judiciary refers to the Preamble to deduce the true intent of the framers. In the landmark Kesavananda Bharati case (1973), the Supreme Court ruled that the Preamble is an integral part of the Constitution and contains the "basic structure" of the document. Therefore, laws and constitutional amendments must align with the foundational values enshrined in the Preamble. It serves as a moral and political compass, guiding the legislature in enacting welfare laws and aiding the executive and judiciary in interpreting and applying those laws in a manner that upholds the spirit of the Constitution.

### 11 b) Explain different ways for acquiring Indian citizenship. (8 marks)
Citizenship in India is governed by Part II of the Constitution (Articles 5 to 11) and, more comprehensively, by the Citizenship Act of 1955. The Act prescribes five distinct ways through which a person can acquire Indian citizenship, reflecting a balance between bloodline descent and territorial belonging. 

1. **By Birth (Jus Soli):** Any person born in India on or after January 26, 1950, is generally considered a citizen by birth. However, due to amendments to curb illegal immigration, the rules have tightened. Currently, a person born in India on or after December 3, 2004, is considered a citizen only if both parents are citizens of India, or if one parent is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. **By Descent (Jus Sanguinis):** A person born outside India can acquire citizenship by descent if either of their parents is a citizen of India at the time of their birth. The birth must be registered at an Indian consulate within one year. 
3. **By Registration:** Persons of Indian origin who have been ordinarily resident in India for seven years, persons married to an Indian citizen who have been resident for seven years, and minor children of Indian citizens can apply for citizenship through registration. 
4. **By Naturalisation:** Foreigners (not illegal migrants) who have resided in India for a specified period (generally 12 years, though exceptions exist) can acquire citizenship by naturalisation. They must fulfill certain conditions, such as having a good character, adequate knowledge of an Eighth Schedule language, and intending to reside in India.
5. **By Incorporation of Territory:** If any new territory becomes part of India, the Government of India specifies by order the individuals of that territory who shall become citizens of India. For instance, when Pondicherry became a part of India, the Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962, was issued to grant citizenship.

### 12 a) Explain the salient features of Indian constitution. (8 marks)
The Constitution of India, adopted on November 26, 1949, and enacted on January 26, 1950, is a monumental legal document characterized by several salient features. 
Firstly, it is the **longest written constitution** in the world. Its sheer volume (originally 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules) arises from the need to govern a vast, diverse nation and includes detailed administrative provisions and special safeguards for minorities and tribal areas. 
Secondly, it is a unique **blend of rigidity and flexibility**. While basic features require a rigid amendment process (two-thirds majority in Parliament plus ratification by half the states), many provisions can be amended by a simple parliamentary majority, allowing the Constitution to adapt to changing times.
Thirdly, it establishes a **Federal System with Unitary Bias**. It divides power between the Centre and States but retains strong unitary features, such as a strong Centre, single citizenship, all-India services, and emergency provisions that can transform the federal structure into a unitary one during crises.
Fourthly, it provides a **Parliamentary Form of Government**, drawn from the British Westminster model, emphasizing the accountability of the executive to the legislature. The President is the nominal head, while real power rests with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers.
Fifthly, the inclusion of **Fundamental Rights (Part III)** guarantees civil liberties against state encroachment, protected by an independent judiciary. 
Complementing these are the **Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV)**, which act as socio-economic goals for the government, and the **Fundamental Duties (Part IVA)**, which outline the moral obligations of citizens. 
Lastly, it established an **Independent and Integrated Judiciary**, with the Supreme Court at the apex, ensuring the uniform application of civil and criminal laws across the country and acting as the guardian of the Constitution.

### 12 b) Write notes on methods of termination of Indian citizenship. (6 marks)
The Citizenship Act of 1955 not only provides methods for acquiring citizenship but also outlines three specific ways by which an individual can lose their Indian citizenship: Renunciation, Termination, and Deprivation.

1. **Renunciation:** An adult Indian citizen of full age and capacity can voluntarily declare their intent to renounce their Indian citizenship. Upon the registration of this declaration by the prescribed authority, that person ceases to be an Indian citizen. Notably, when a male person renounces his citizenship, every minor child of his also ceases to be an Indian citizen. However, such a child can resume Indian citizenship by making a declaration within one year of attaining full age (18 years).
2. **Termination:** India strictly follows the principle of single citizenship. Therefore, if a citizen of India voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country (by naturalization, registration, or any other voluntary means), their Indian citizenship automatically terminates. This provision prevents dual citizenship, which is generally not permitted under the Indian Constitution.
3. **Deprivation:** This is a compulsory termination of citizenship by the Central Government. It applies primarily to those who acquired citizenship through registration or naturalization (not by birth or descent). The government can deprive a person of their citizenship if it is satisfied that the citizenship was obtained by fraud, false representation, or concealment of material facts. It can also be invoked if the citizen has shown disloyalty to the Constitution, has unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy during a war, or has been imprisoned in any country for two years within five years of acquiring Indian citizenship.

### 13 a) Explain the concept of appeal by special leave. (6 marks)
The concept of Appeal by Special Leave is enshrined in Article 136 of the Constitution of India, providing the Supreme Court with an extraordinary and discretionary appellate jurisdiction. Unlike regular appeals, which are brought before the Supreme Court as a matter of right (under Articles 132, 133, and 134) when a High Court issues a certificate, Article 136 allows the Supreme Court to grant special leave to appeal against any judgment, decree, determination, sentence, or order in any cause or matter passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India.

The primary objective of Article 136 is to ensure that justice is not thwarted by procedural technicalities or statutory limitations. The Supreme Court uses this power sparingly and only in exceptional circumstances where a substantial question of law of general public importance is involved, or where there has been a gross miscarriage of justice by a lower court or tribunal. It is important to note that this is a residual power; a party cannot claim special leave as a constitutional right. The Supreme Court has absolute discretion to grant or deny the petition. Furthermore, the provision specifically excludes military tribunals or court-martials from its purview. Through this mechanism, the Supreme Court functions as the ultimate arbiter of justice, stepping in to correct egregious errors regardless of the level of the court where the error originated.

### 13 b) Discuss the classification of Directive Principles of State Policy in detail. (8 marks)
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP), contained in Part IV (Articles 36 to 51) of the Constitution, are non-justiciable guidelines meant to guide the State in policy-making and administration. Their ultimate aim is to establish a welfare state based on social and economic justice. While the Constitution does not formally classify them, scholars typically categorize the DPSPs into three broad groups based on their ideological content and objectives: Socialistic, Gandhian, and Liberal-Intellectual principles.

**1. Socialistic Principles:** These principles reflect the ideology of socialism and aim to lay down the framework of a democratic socialist state. They focus on providing social and economic justice, reducing inequalities, and ensuring an equitable distribution of resources. Key examples include Article 38 (promoting the welfare of the people by minimizing inequalities in income and status), Article 39 (securing the right to an adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, and preventing the concentration of wealth), Article 41 (right to work, education, and public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, or sickness), and Article 43 (securing a living wage and a decent standard of life for workers).

**2. Gandhian Principles:** Based on the ideology of Mahatma Gandhi, these principles represent the program of reconstruction enunciated during the national movement. They focus on empowering rural India, promoting cottage industries, and uplifting marginalized communities. Important articles include Article 40 (organizing village panchayats and endowing them with powers of self-government), Article 43 (promoting cottage industries on an individual or co-operative basis in rural areas), Article 46 (promoting the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and other weaker sections), and Article 47 (prohibiting the consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs that are injurious to health).

**3. Liberal-Intellectual Principles:** These principles reflect the ideology of liberalism and focus on modernity, internationalism, and progressive reforms. Prominent directives in this category include Article 44 (securing a Uniform Civil Code for all citizens throughout the country), Article 45 (providing early childhood care and education for all children until the age of six), Article 50 (separating the judiciary from the executive in the public services of the State), and Article 51 (promoting international peace and security, and maintaining just and honorable relations between nations). Together, these three categories form a comprehensive blueprint for India's socio-economic development.

### 14 a) What do you mean by right against exploitation? Explain. (7 marks)
The Right against Exploitation is a crucial fundamental right enshrined in Articles 23 and 24 of the Constitution of India, aimed at protecting human dignity and preventing the vulnerable from being subjected to abusive practices. In a society historically plagued by severe inequalities and systemic oppression, this right acts as a shield against physical and economic exploitation by both the State and private individuals.

**Article 23** prohibits "traffic in human beings and *begar* and other similar forms of forced labour." Traffic in human beings involves the buying and selling of men, women, and children like commodities, often for immoral purposes such as prostitution or slavery. *Begar* refers to the practice of forcing a person to work without remuneration. The article outlaws all forms of forced labor, ensuring that no individual can be compelled to work against their will, even if some remuneration is offered, as established in various Supreme Court rulings regarding debt bondage. However, an exception exists: the State can impose compulsory service for public purposes (like military conscription or social service) without discrimination based on religion, race, caste, or class.

**Article 24** specifically addresses child labor. It strictly prohibits the employment of children below the age of 14 years in any factory, mine, or other hazardous employment (such as construction work or railways). This provision is vital for protecting the health, safety, and childhood of young individuals, ensuring they have the opportunity for education and proper development rather than being forced into dangerous labor. Subsequent legislation, like the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, has further strengthened these constitutional mandates, creating a legal framework to eradicate exploitation.

### 14 b) Distinguish between fundamental rights and directive principles of state policy. (7 marks)
Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12-35) and Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV, Articles 36-51) are both foundational to the Indian Constitution, yet they differ significantly in their nature, scope, and enforceability. The distinction between them is essential for understanding the balance of power between the citizen and the State.

1. **Enforceability (Justiciability):** This is the most critical distinction. Fundamental Rights are justiciable, meaning a citizen can directly approach the courts (Supreme Court under Article 32 or High Court under Article 226) for their enforcement if they are violated by the State. In contrast, Directive Principles are non-justiciable. They are moral guidelines for the State, and a citizen cannot sue the government in a court of law for failing to implement a DPSP.
2. **Nature of Obligations:** Fundamental Rights are generally negative obligations on the State. They dictate what the State *cannot* do (e.g., the State cannot deny equality, cannot curb freedom of speech without reasonable restriction). They act as limitations on state power to protect individual liberty. Directive Principles, conversely, are positive obligations. They dictate what the State *should* do to promote social and economic welfare (e.g., providing a living wage, free legal aid, protecting the environment).
3. **Primary Objective:** Fundamental Rights aim at establishing political democracy. They protect civil liberties and prevent the establishment of authoritarian rule. Directive Principles aim at establishing social and economic democracy. They seek to create a welfare state by addressing issues of poverty, inequality, and social justice.
4. **Subordination in Conflict:** Historically, courts held that in case of a direct conflict, Fundamental Rights prevail over DPSPs. However, the current settled legal position (as per the Minerva Mills case, 1980) is that the Constitution rests on the "bedrock of balance" between the two. While Fundamental Rights are supreme, courts interpret them harmoniously with DPSPs to achieve the broader goals of the Constitution.

### 15 a) Explain the powers of President of India. (8 marks)
The President of India, as the ceremonial head of state and the first citizen, is vested with extensive powers by the Constitution. Although the President generally acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 74), these powers are vast and categorized into several domains:

**1. Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the Government of India are formally taken in the President's name. The President appoints the Prime Minister and, on their advice, the other Ministers. Crucial appointments, including the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General, Chief Election Commissioner, Chairman and members of the UPSC, and Governors of states, are made by the President. 
**2. Legislative Powers:** The President is an integral part of the Parliament. The President summons and prorogues the sessions of Parliament and can dissolve the Lok Sabha. A bill passed by Parliament becomes law only after receiving the President's assent (who can give assent, withhold it, or return it for reconsideration). Most importantly, under Article 123, the President can promulgate Ordinances when Parliament is not in session, which have the same force as an Act of Parliament.
**3. Judicial and Pardoning Powers:** The President appoints the Chief Justice and other judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Under Article 72, the President has the sovereign power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute sentences in certain cases, notably court-martials and death sentences.
**4. Financial Powers:** Money bills can be introduced in Parliament only with the President's prior recommendation. The President causes the Annual Financial Statement (Budget) to be laid before Parliament and constitutes the Finance Commission every five years.
**5. Emergency Powers:** The President wields extraordinary powers during crises. They can declare a National Emergency (Article 352) due to war or armed rebellion, President's Rule in a state (Article 356) if the constitutional machinery fails, and a Financial Emergency (Article 360) if the country's financial stability is threatened. 

### 15 b) Explain the constitutional position and essential qualifications of Vice-president of India. (6 marks)
The Vice-President of India occupies the second-highest constitutional office in the country, modeled loosely on the American Vice-Presidency. The primary constitutional role of the Vice-President is to act as the *ex-officio* Chairman of the Rajya Sabha (Council of States), performing duties similar to those of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha—presiding over sessions, maintaining order, and deciding points of order.

The most critical constitutional position of the Vice-President arises during a vacancy in the office of the President. Under Article 65, if the President's office falls vacant due to death, resignation, impeachment, or other reasons, the Vice-President acts as the President until a new President is elected (which must happen within six months). Furthermore, if the President is temporarily unable to discharge their duties due to absence or illness, the Vice-President discharges those functions. It is important to note that when acting as President or discharging the President's functions, the Vice-President ceases to perform the duties of the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and is entitled to the salary and privileges of the President.

**Qualifications:** To be eligible for election as Vice-President, Article 66 stipulates that a person must:
1. Be a citizen of India.
2. Have completed the age of thirty-five years.
3. Be qualified for election as a member of the Rajya Sabha (which implies they must possess qualifications prescribed by Parliament).
4. Not hold any office of profit under the Union government, any state government, or any local authority. (Sitting Presidents, Vice-Presidents, Governors, and Ministers are exempt from this condition).

### 16 a) Explain the qualification and disqualification for membership in the house of the people. (6 marks)
The House of the People, or the Lok Sabha, is the lower house of India's bicameral Parliament, representing the citizens directly. The Constitution and the Representation of the People Act, 1951, outline specific criteria for who can serve as a Member of Parliament (MP) in the Lok Sabha.

**Qualifications:** According to Article 84 of the Constitution, to be qualified for membership in the Lok Sabha, a person must:
1. Be a citizen of India.
2. Make and subscribe to an oath or affirmation bearing true faith and allegiance to the Constitution.
3. Be at least 25 years of age.
4. Possess other qualifications prescribed by Parliament. Under the Representation of the People Act, a candidate must be registered as an elector (voter) in any parliamentary constituency in India. To contest from a seat reserved for Scheduled Castes or Scheduled Tribes, they must belong to that specific category.

**Disqualifications:** Article 102 details the conditions under which a person is disqualified from being chosen as, or for being, an MP. These include:
1. Holding any office of profit under the Government of India or a State Government (exceptions apply for ministers and offices exempted by Parliament).
2. Being declared of unsound mind by a competent court.
3. Being an undischarged insolvent.
4. Ceasing to be a citizen of India, or voluntarily acquiring citizenship of a foreign state, or being under an acknowledgment of allegiance to a foreign state.
5. Furthermore, Parliament has enacted statutory disqualifications (e.g., conviction for certain crimes resulting in imprisonment of two years or more, corrupt electoral practices). Additionally, the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law) disqualifies members if they voluntarily give up membership of their political party or vote contrary to party whips.

### 16 b) Explain various kinds of jurisdiction of Supreme Court. (8 marks)
The Supreme Court of India is one of the most powerful apex courts in the world, endowed with a broad and multifaceted jurisdiction by the Constitution to act as the ultimate interpreter of the law and the guarantor of fundamental rights. Its jurisdiction can be broadly classified into Original, Appellate, Advisory, and Writ jurisdictions.

**1. Original Jurisdiction (Article 131):** The Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction to hear disputes of a federal nature. This means cases that arise directly between the Government of India and one or more States, or between two or more States, must be filed directly in the Supreme Court. This ensures the court acts as the federal umpire.

**2. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32):** This is a critical component of its original jurisdiction, specifically for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Any citizen whose fundamental rights are violated can directly approach the Supreme Court, bypassing lower courts. The Court can issue writs like *Habeas Corpus*, *Mandamus*, *Prohibition*, *Certiorari*, and *Quo Warranto* to restore these rights.

**3. Appellate Jurisdiction:** The Supreme Court is the highest court of appeal in the country. It hears appeals against the judgments of High Courts in three main categories:
*   **Constitutional Matters (Article 132):** If a High Court certifies that a case involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution.
*   **Civil Matters (Article 133):** If the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance that needs the Supreme Court's decision.
*   **Criminal Matters (Article 134):** An appeal lies if the High Court, on appeal, reversed an acquittal and sentenced the accused to death, or if it withdrew a case from a subordinate court and sentenced the accused to death.
Additionally, **Article 136** grants the Court discretionary power to grant Special Leave to Appeal against any judgment from any court or tribunal in India (except military tribunals).

**4. Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143):** The President of India can seek the Supreme Court's opinion on any question of law or fact of public importance. While the Court's opinion is advisory and not binding on the President, it carries immense legal weight.

### 17 a) Explain the powers and functions of the Governor of Kerala state. (8 marks)
The Governor is the constitutional head of the State of Kerala (as in other Indian states) and represents the President of India at the state level. While operating within a parliamentary framework where real executive power lies with the Chief Minister and the Council of Ministers, the Governor possesses significant executive, legislative, financial, and discretionary powers.

**1. Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the state government are taken in the name of the Governor. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister (usually the leader of the majority party in the Legislative Assembly) and other ministers on the Chief Minister's advice. The Governor also appoints the Advocate General of the State, the Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission, and the State Election Commissioner. The Governor can seek information from the Chief Minister relating to the administration of the state.

**2. Legislative Powers:** The Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature. They have the power to summon, prorogue the State Legislative Assembly, and can dissolve it. The Governor addresses the first session of the legislature every year and the first session after a general election. Crucially, no bill passed by the state legislature becomes an Act without the Governor's assent. The Governor can give assent, withhold it, return the bill (if it's not a money bill) for reconsideration, or reserve it for the consideration of the President. The Governor can also promulgate Ordinances when the legislature is not in session.

**3. Financial Powers:** A Money Bill can only be introduced in the state legislature with the prior recommendation of the Governor. The Governor ensures that the Annual State Budget is laid before the legislature and constitutes the State Finance Commission every five years.

**4. Discretionary Powers:** Unlike the President, the Constitution explicitly grants some discretionary powers to the Governor. These are exercised without the advice of the Council of Ministers. They include recommending the imposition of President's Rule (Article 356) if the state's constitutional machinery breaks down, reserving certain bills for the President, and appointing a Chief Minister in the event of a hung assembly or sudden demise of a ruling CM without an obvious successor.

### 17 b) Explain the composition and duration of state legislative council. (6 marks)
The State Legislative Council, or Vidhan Parishad, is the upper house in states with a bicameral legislature. Unlike the Legislative Assembly, which is mandatory, the existence of the Legislative Council is optional. Article 169 empowers Parliament to create or abolish a Legislative Council in a state if the Legislative Assembly of that state passes a resolution to that effect by a special majority. 

**Composition:** The composition of the Legislative Council is designed to ensure representation from various sectors of society, making it a heterogeneous body. Article 171 dictates that the total number of members in a Legislative Council cannot exceed one-third of the total number of members in the state's Legislative Assembly, but it must not be less than 40 members. The election and nomination process is indirect and divided as follows:
*   1/3rd of the members are elected by local bodies like municipalities and district boards.
*   1/12th are elected by graduates of three years' standing residing in the state.
*   1/12th are elected by teachers of three years' standing in educational institutions not lower in standard than a secondary school.
*   1/3rd are elected by the members of the State Legislative Assembly from amongst persons who are not members of the Assembly.
*   The remainder (approximately 1/6th) are nominated by the Governor from individuals possessing special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, cooperative movement, and social service.

**Duration:** The Legislative Council is a permanent body; it is not subject to dissolution. However, akin to the Rajya Sabha at the Centre, one-third of its members retire every second year. Consequently, a member of the Legislative Council enjoys a continuous term of six years.

### 18 a) Explain the qualification and disqualification for membership of the state legislature. (7 marks)
The State Legislature consists of the Governor and the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha), and in some states, a Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad). The Constitution sets clear parameters for who can become a member of these legislative bodies.

**Qualifications:** Under Article 173, to be qualified for membership of the state legislature, a person must:
1. Be a citizen of India.
2. Make and subscribe to an oath or affirmation of allegiance to the Constitution before an authority authorized by the Election Commission.
3. Age criterion: Be at least 25 years old to contest for the Legislative Assembly, and at least 30 years old for the Legislative Council.
4. Possess any other qualifications prescribed by Parliament. For instance, the Representation of the People Act requires the candidate to be an elector for an assembly constituency in that state.

**Disqualifications:** Article 191 outlines the grounds for disqualification from being a member of the State Legislative Assembly or Legislative Council. A person is disqualified if they:
1. Hold any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of any State, unless that office is exempted by a state law.
2. Are declared to be of unsound mind by a competent court.
3. Are an undischarged insolvent.
4. Are not a citizen of India, or have voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign state, or owe allegiance to a foreign state.
5. Are disqualified under any law made by Parliament. This includes disqualifications for electoral offenses, corruption, or criminal convictions under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
Furthermore, the Tenth Schedule of the Constitution mandates disqualification on grounds of defection—if a member voluntarily gives up the membership of their political party or disobeys party directives (whips) during voting in the house.

### 18 b) Explain the constitution of High court. What are the essential qualifications required for the appointment of High court Judge? (7 marks)
The High Court is the highest judicial authority in a state, playing a pivotal role in the integrated judicial system of India. Governed by Part VI, Chapter V of the Constitution, High Courts have wide-ranging jurisdiction, including original, appellate, and writ jurisdictions, enabling them to protect fundamental rights and exercise supervisory control over all subordinate courts and tribunals within their territorial limits.

**Constitution of the High Court:** Article 214 stipulates that there shall be a High Court for each state. However, Parliament has the power (under Article 231) to establish a common High Court for two or more states or for two or more states and a Union Territory. Structurally, a High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other number of Judges as the President of India may deem necessary to appoint from time to time. Unlike the Supreme Court, the Constitution does not fix the maximum number of judges for a High Court; it varies depending on the volume of litigation and the size of the state.

**Qualifications for Appointment:** To ensure the judiciary's competence and independence, Article 217 lays down stringent qualifications for the appointment of a High Court judge. To be eligible, a person must:
1. Be a citizen of India.
2. Have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years.
   *OR*
3. Have been an advocate of a High Court (or of two or more such Courts in succession) for at least ten years. 
The judges of the High Court are appointed by the President. The Chief Justice of the High Court is appointed after consultation with the Chief Justice of India and the Governor of the state concerned. For appointing other judges, the Chief Justice of that specific High Court is also consulted.

### 19 a) Explain parliamentary legislation in the state field. (6 marks)
The Indian Constitution fundamentally divides legislative powers between the Centre and the States through the Seventh Schedule (Union List, State List, Concurrent List). Normally, Parliament legislates on Union and Concurrent List subjects, while State Legislatures have exclusive power over State List subjects. However, the Constitution allows for "Parliamentary legislation in the State field" under certain extraordinary circumstances to ensure national interest and uniformity. 

Parliament can make laws on subjects enumerated in the State List in five specific situations:
1. **National Interest (Article 249):** If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by a special majority (two-thirds of members present and voting) declaring that it is necessary in the national interest, Parliament gains the power to legislate on the specified State List subject for one year.
2. **National Emergency (Article 250):** During the operation of a National Emergency under Article 352, Parliament assumes the power to legislate on any subject in the State List for the entire country or part of it.
3. **State Consent (Article 252):** If the legislatures of two or more states pass resolutions requesting Parliament to enact a law on a State List subject, Parliament can do so. Such a law applies only to those consenting states (and any other state that adopts it later).
4. **International Agreements (Article 253):** Parliament has overriding power to make laws for the whole or any part of India to implement any treaty, agreement, or convention with foreign countries, even if the subject falls under the State List.
5. **President's Rule (Article 356/357):** When a state is under President's Rule due to the failure of constitutional machinery, Parliament is empowered to make laws for that state concerning any subject, including those in the State List.

### 19 b) Discuss the effects of national and financial emergencies. (8 marks)
Emergency provisions in the Indian Constitution are designed to convert the normal federal structure into a unitary one temporarily, enabling the central government to deal effectively with severe crises. The effects of National and Financial Emergencies are profound and impact executive, legislative, and financial relations.

**Effects of National Emergency (Article 352):** A National Emergency can be declared on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. 
1. **Executive and Legislative:** The federal balance shifts dramatically. The Centre gains the power to give executive directions to states on any matter. Parliament acquires the power to legislate on any subject in the State List (Article 250). State governments are not suspended, but they come under the complete control of the Centre.
2. **Financial:** The President can modify the constitutional distribution of revenues between the Centre and the States, suspending or reducing the financial transfers to states to retain funds for national defense.
3. **Fundamental Rights:** Article 358 automatically suspends the six fundamental freedoms under Article 19 as soon as an emergency is declared on grounds of war or external aggression (not armed rebellion). Under Article 359, the President can issue an order suspending the right to move courts for the enforcement of other Fundamental Rights, except those under Articles 20 (protection in respect of conviction) and 21 (protection of life and personal liberty). 

**Effects of Financial Emergency (Article 360):** Declared when the financial stability or credit of India is threatened.
1. The executive authority of the Centre expands to give directions to states to observe financial propriety.
2. The Centre can order the reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the State, and even those serving the Union, including Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
3. The President can direct that all money bills or other financial bills passed by the state legislatures be reserved for their consideration. This severely curtails the financial autonomy of the states.

### 20 a) Explain the procedure for amendment of the constitution. (6 marks)
The procedure for amending the Constitution of India is laid down in Article 368. The framers intentionally designed a mechanism that is neither as easy as enacting ordinary laws (which would make the constitution too unstable) nor as difficult as the American amendment process (which would make it too rigid to adapt to changing times).

The procedure under Article 368 involves several steps:
1. **Initiation:** An amendment can be initiated only by the introduction of a bill for that purpose in either House of Parliament (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha). It cannot be initiated in the state legislatures. The bill can be introduced by a minister or a private member and does not require the prior permission of the President.
2. **Special Majority:** The bill must be passed in each House by a "special majority," which means a majority of the total membership of that House AND a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.
3. **State Ratification (Conditional):** If the amendment seeks to alter the federal provisions of the Constitution (such as the election of the President, distribution of legislative powers, or representation of states in Parliament), the bill must also be ratified by the legislatures of at least one-half of the states by a simple majority before it is presented to the President.
4. **Presidential Assent:** After being passed by both Houses (and ratified by states, if necessary), the bill is presented to the President. Following the 24th Amendment Act, the President is bound to give their assent to a constitutional amendment bill; they cannot withhold assent or return it for reconsideration. Upon receiving the assent, the Constitution stands amended.

### 20 b) What is the need for administrative tribunals? Explain the functions of state administrative tribunals. (8 marks)
Administrative tribunals are quasi-judicial bodies established by the government to resolve disputes related to administrative matters, taxation, and service conditions of public servants. The growing complexity of modern governance, the expansion of state functions into socio-economic spheres, and the resulting increase in litigation led to a massive backlog of cases in traditional courts. Ordinary courts follow strict, rigid procedural laws (like the Civil Procedure Code) which are time-consuming and expensive. Therefore, administrative tribunals were needed to provide a mechanism that is speedy, inexpensive, and accessible. Furthermore, technical disputes (like environmental issues or taxation) require specialized knowledge that ordinary judges might lack; tribunals are often staffed with both legal experts and subject-matter specialists, ensuring more informed adjudication.

**State Administrative Tribunals (SATs):** Recognizing the need to address grievances of state government employees efficiently, the 42nd Amendment Act of 1976 introduced Part XIV-A to the Constitution. Article 323A empowered Parliament to establish administrative tribunals for the Union and the States. Subsequently, Parliament enacted the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, which authorized the Central Government to establish SATs upon the specific request of the concerned State Government.

**Functions of SATs:**
1. **Adjudication of Service Disputes:** The primary function of a SAT is to adjudicate disputes and complaints regarding the recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the state. This includes matters like promotions, transfers, disciplinary proceedings, pensions, and dismissals.
2. **Alternative Forum:** Once a SAT is established, it assumes original jurisdiction over all service matters of state employees, effectively barring the jurisdiction of all other courts (except the Supreme Court under Article 136, and later High Courts under Articles 226/227 as ruled in the *L. Chandra Kumar* case). 
3. **Procedural Flexibility:** SATs are not bound by the strict rules of the Code of Civil Procedure or the Indian Evidence Act. They are guided by the principles of natural justice, which allows them to adopt flexible, rapid procedures, drastically reducing the time taken to resolve disputes compared to High Courts.
