### 11 a) List out the salient features of Indian Constitution
The Indian Constitution, adopted on November 26, 1949, and effective from January 26, 1950, is a unique document possessing several salient features that distinguish it from other constitutions of the world. Firstly, it is the lengthiest written constitution globally, originally containing 395 Articles and 8 Schedules, which has since expanded through numerous amendments. This bulkiness is due to the vast diversity of the country, the inclusion of fundamental rights and directive principles, and detailed administrative provisions. Secondly, it is drawn from various sources; the framers extensively borrowed principles from the Government of India Act 1935, and constitutions of the UK (parliamentary system, rule of law), USA (fundamental rights, judicial review), Ireland (directive principles), and Canada (federal system with a strong centre). 

Thirdly, it exhibits a unique blend of rigidity and flexibility in its amendment procedure. While some provisions can be amended by a simple parliamentary majority, others require a special majority, and entrenched provisions need ratification by half of the state legislatures. Fourthly, it establishes a federal system with a strong unitary bias. Although it has federal features like dual government, division of powers, and an independent judiciary, it contains strong centralizing tendencies, especially during emergencies where the structure transforms into a unitary one. Fifthly, it provides for a Parliamentary form of government at both the Centre and the states, characterized by the presence of nominal and real executives, majority party rule, and collective responsibility of the executive to the legislature.

Furthermore, the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights (Part III) to its citizens, ensuring basic liberties and protecting them against state action. It also enumerates Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV), which serve as guidelines for the government to establish a welfare state. Additionally, it incorporates Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A) reminding citizens of their obligations. Other notable features include a secular state model ensuring equal respect for all religions, a single citizenship to foster national unity, an independent integrated judiciary with the Supreme Court at the apex, and the provision for Universal Adult Franchise, which grants voting rights to all citizens above 18 years without discrimination, thus democratizing the political process.

### 11 b) Discuss the various aspects in the preamble of Indian Constitution
The Preamble to the Indian Constitution is a profound introductory statement that encapsulates the philosophy, ideals, and objectives of the framers. It serves as a key to unravel the minds of the makers of the Constitution and outlines the fundamental values on which the nation is built. The Preamble begins with the phrase "We, the people of India," which indicates the ultimate source of authority of the Constitution. It signifies that the power resides with the citizens, establishing the principle of popular sovereignty. 

The Preamble declares the nature of the Indian State through five distinct terms: Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic. 'Sovereign' implies that India is internally supreme and externally free from control by any foreign power. 'Socialist', added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, reflects the commitment to a democratic socialism aimed at ending poverty, ignorance, disease, and inequality of opportunity. 'Secular', also added by the 42nd Amendment, means the State has no official religion and treats all religions equally, ensuring religious freedom for all. 'Democratic' indicates that the government draws its authority from the people through regular elections, emphasizing a system where the executive is responsible to the legislature. 'Republic' signifies that the head of the state (the President) is an elected representative, not a hereditary monarch, and public offices are open to all without discrimination.

Beyond defining the nature of the state, the Preamble outlines its primary objectives. It seeks to secure Justice in three dimensions: social (eliminating inequalities based on caste, class, or gender), economic (reducing wealth disparities), and political (ensuring equal political rights). It guarantees Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship, essential for the development of individuals. It aims to provide Equality of status and opportunity, striving to remove artificial barriers and discrimination. Finally, it promotes Fraternity, aiming to foster a sense of brotherhood among citizens to ensure the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation (the word 'integrity' was also added in 1976). While the Preamble is not directly enforceable in courts, it acts as a guiding light for judicial interpretation of constitutional provisions.

### 12 a) Discuss the various methods to acquire Indian citizenship
The acquisition of Indian citizenship is governed by the Citizenship Act of 1955, read with Part II of the Indian Constitution (Articles 5 to 11). The Act provides for five primary methods through which an individual can acquire Indian citizenship. The first method is Citizenship by Birth (Jus Soli). Under this provision, a person born in India on or after January 26, 1950, but before July 1, 1987, is a citizen by birth irrespective of their parents' nationality. For those born between July 1, 1987, and the commencement of the 2003 amendment, citizenship is granted if at least one parent is a citizen at the time of birth. For those born on or after the 2003 amendment, citizenship is acquired if both parents are citizens, or one is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant.

The second method is Citizenship by Descent (Jus Sanguinis). A person born outside India on or after January 26, 1950, but before December 10, 1992, is a citizen by descent if their father was an Indian citizen at the time of birth. Post-December 1992, the requirement was modified so that citizenship by descent is granted if either parent is an Indian citizen. Subsequent amendments require that the birth must be registered at an Indian consulate within a year.

The third method is Citizenship by Registration. This is applicable to specific categories of persons, such as persons of Indian origin who have been ordinarily resident in India for seven years before applying, persons of Indian origin residing in any country outside undivided India, persons married to an Indian citizen and ordinarily resident in India for seven years, and minor children of Indian citizens. 

The fourth method is Citizenship by Naturalization. A foreigner (not being an illegal migrant) can acquire citizenship by naturalization if they fulfill certain stringent conditions. These include renouncing their previous citizenship, being ordinarily resident in India or serving the Government of India for a specified period (generally 11 of the previous 14 years, and continuously for the preceding 12 months), being of good character, and possessing adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule.

The fifth method is Citizenship by Incorporation of Territory. If a new territory becomes a part of India, the Government of India may, by order, specify the persons of that territory who shall become citizens of India, detailing the date and conditions of such acquisition.

### 12 b) Explain any three citizenship amendment act
The Citizenship Act of 1955 has been amended several times to address evolving geopolitical realities, migration issues, and policy shifts. Three significant amendments include those passed in 1986, 2003, and the highly debated amendment of 2019. 

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1986, was introduced primarily to curb the influx of illegal immigrants, particularly from neighboring countries like Bangladesh into border states like Assam. Prior to this amendment, the principle of 'jus soli' (citizenship by birth) was broadly applied, meaning anyone born in India acquired citizenship. The 1986 amendment restricted this by stipulating that for a person born in India on or after July 1, 1987, to acquire citizenship by birth, it was necessary that at least one of their parents be an Indian citizen at the time of birth. This was a critical shift from unconditional birthright citizenship to a more restricted model aimed at addressing demographic concerns in border regions.

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003, further tightened the provisions regarding citizenship by birth and descent and introduced the concept of an 'illegal migrant' explicitly into the legal framework. It mandated that for persons born on or after its commencement, citizenship by birth requires both parents to be Indian citizens, or one parent to be a citizen and the other not to be an illegal migrant. It also introduced provisions for the Overseas Citizen of India (OCI) scheme, aimed at providing certain benefits to Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) in specified countries, stopping short of dual citizenship. Importantly, it also laid the groundwork for the creation of a National Register of Indian Citizens (NRIC) and the issuance of national identity cards.

The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (CAA), is perhaps the most consequential and controversial amendment. It sought to grant Indian citizenship to persecuted religious minorities—specifically Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians—who fled from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan and entered India on or before December 31, 2014. The amendment significantly reduced the residency requirement for naturalization for these specific groups from 11 years to 5 years. It was highly contentious because it excluded Muslims from its purview, leading to widespread protests over concerns that it violated the secular ethos of the Constitution, particularly Article 14 (Right to Equality), by linking citizenship to religious identity.

### 13 a) What are the features of fundamental rights? Explain any two types of fundamental right.
Fundamental Rights, enshrined in Part III (Articles 12 to 35) of the Indian Constitution, are the basic human rights guaranteed to citizens (and in some cases, non-citizens) to ensure their holistic development and protect them against arbitrary state action. A key feature of these rights is that they are justiciable, meaning they are legally enforceable in courts of law; any individual whose rights are violated can directly approach the Supreme Court (under Article 32) or High Courts (under Article 226) for remedies. However, they are not absolute. They are subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by the State on grounds of sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the state, public order, decency, or morality. Furthermore, Fundamental Rights can be suspended during a National Emergency (except Articles 20 and 21). While most rights are available against arbitrary actions of the State, some (like the abolition of untouchability) are enforceable against private individuals as well.

The Constitution categorizes Fundamental Rights into six broad types. Two significant types are the Right to Equality and the Right to Freedom. 

The Right to Equality (Articles 14-18) is the cornerstone of democratic governance, aiming to establish an egalitarian society. Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of laws, meaning all persons are subject to the same laws without discrimination. Article 15 prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, particularly regarding access to public spaces. Article 16 ensures equality of opportunity in public employment. Crucially, Article 17 abolishes the practice of "untouchability" in any form, making it a punishable offense. Article 18 abolishes titles (except military and academic) to prevent artificial social hierarchies. Together, these articles promote social and economic equality and empower the state to implement affirmative action for weaker sections.

The Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22) protects core civil liberties. Article 19 is pivotal, guaranteeing six freedoms to citizens: speech and expression, peaceful assembly without arms, forming associations or unions, moving freely throughout India, residing and settling in any part of the country, and practicing any profession or carrying on any occupation. However, these are subject to reasonable restrictions. Article 20 provides protection against arbitrary and excessive punishment in criminal matters. Article 21, often considered the most vital, guarantees the protection of life and personal liberty, establishing that no person shall be deprived of their life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law (which has been broadly interpreted to mean fair, just, and reasonable procedure). Article 21A guarantees the right to elementary education, and Article 22 provides protection against arbitrary arrest and preventive detention.

### 13 b) List out the Gandhian ideology included in directive principle
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP), contained in Part IV of the Constitution, serve as guidelines for the State to establish a just social and economic order. While they are classified broadly into socialist, Gandhian, and liberal-intellectual principles, the Gandhian principles specifically reflect the program of reconstruction enunciated by Mahatma Gandhi during the national movement. These principles aim to realize Gandhi's vision of a decentralized, self-reliant, and morally upright society. 

Key Gandhian ideologies incorporated as Directive Principles include:

1. **Organization of Village Panchayats (Article 40):** This directs the State to organize village panchayats and endow them with necessary powers and authority to function as units of self-government. This embodies Gandhi's vision of 'Gram Swaraj' or village self-rule, emphasizing decentralized democracy at the grassroots level.
2. **Promotion of Cottage Industries (Article 43):** The State is directed to promote cottage industries on an individual or co-operative basis in rural areas. This aligns with Gandhi's focus on rural employment, self-reliance (Swadeshi), and opposition to large-scale, dehumanizing industrialization.
3. **Promotion of Educational and Economic Interests of Weaker Sections (Article 46):** The State shall promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people, particularly the Scheduled Castes (SCs), Scheduled Tribes (STs), and protect them from social injustice and all forms of exploitation. This reflects Gandhi's lifelong campaign for the upliftment of the marginalized and eradication of untouchability.
4. **Prohibition of Intoxicating Drinks and Drugs (Article 47):** This principle directs the State to endeavor to bring about prohibition of the consumption (except for medicinal purposes) of intoxicating drinks and drugs which are injurious to health. This stems directly from Gandhi's strong advocacy for prohibition to improve moral and physical health, particularly among the poor.
5. **Prohibition of Cow Slaughter (Article 48):** The State is directed to take steps for preserving and improving the breeds, and prohibiting the slaughter, of cows and calves and other milch and draught cattle. This reflects traditional Indian reverence for cattle, championed by Gandhi for both economic (agriculture) and cultural reasons.

### 14 a) What are the duties of Indian Citizens according to Constitution?
The Fundamental Duties of Indian citizens were added to the Constitution by the 42nd Amendment Act in 1976, upon the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Enshrined in Part IV-A under a single Article 51A, these duties serve as a reminder to citizens that while they enjoy fundamental rights, they also bear corresponding responsibilities towards the nation and society. Originally, there were ten duties, and an eleventh was added by the 86th Amendment in 2002. Unlike Fundamental Rights, these duties are non-justiciable; they cannot be legally enforced by a court, meaning there is no direct legal penalty for their violation, although Parliament can enforce them through specific legislation.

The eleven Fundamental Duties mandate that every citizen must:
1. **Abide by the Constitution** and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem.
2. **Cherish and follow the noble ideals** that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
3. **Uphold and protect the sovereignty**, unity, and integrity of India.
4. **Defend the country** and render national service when called upon to do so.
5. **Promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood** amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities, and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
6. **Value and preserve the rich heritage** of the country’s composite culture.
7. **Protect and improve the natural environment** including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
8. **Develop the scientific temper**, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
9. **Safeguard public property** and to abjure violence.
10. **Strive towards excellence** in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavor and achievement.
11. **Provide opportunities for education** to their child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years (added in 2002).

These duties aim to foster a sense of civic responsibility, promote national unity, and create an enlightened citizenry essential for the success of democracy.

### 14 b) List out the features of directive principles of state
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP), contained in Part IV of the Indian Constitution (Articles 36 to 51), represent the socio-economic philosophy of the Constitution and outline the ideals the state should strive to achieve. A primary feature of the DPSP is their non-justiciable nature; they are not legally enforceable by any court. If the state fails to implement a directive principle, a citizen cannot sue the government in court. However, Article 37 explicitly states that these principles are "fundamental in the governance of the country," and it is the moral and political duty of the State to apply them in making laws. 

The DPSP aim to establish a "welfare state" in India, characterized by social and economic justice, as opposed to a mere "police state" focused solely on maintaining law and order. They are designed to address the inequalities and poverty prevalent in society. The principles can be broadly classified into three ideological categories: socialist principles (e.g., securing adequate means of livelihood, equitable distribution of resources, equal pay for equal work), Gandhian principles (e.g., organizing village panchayats, promoting cottage industries, prohibiting intoxicating drinks), and liberal-intellectual principles (e.g., securing a uniform civil code, providing free and compulsory education, protecting the environment). 

Furthermore, the DPSP act as a yardstick to evaluate the performance of the government. Since they embody the collective will and aspirations of the people, political parties are judged by the electorate based on their commitment to implementing these directives. They also aid the judiciary in interpreting the Constitution; courts often rely on the DPSP to determine the constitutional validity of laws, using them to give a broad and progressive interpretation to fundamental rights, harmonizing the two parts to achieve the overarching goals of justice and equality.

### 15 a) Describe the procedure for election and removal of the president of India
The President of India is the head of the State, and the procedures for their election and removal are specifically designed to ensure the office remains dignified, impartial, and representative of the federal structure.

**Election Procedure:** The President is elected not directly by the people, but indirectly by an Electoral College, as per Article 54. The Electoral College consists exclusively of elected members; nominated members are excluded. It comprises the elected members of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha), the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States, and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the Union Territories of Delhi and Puducherry. The election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, and voting is by secret ballot. To ensure parity among states and between the states as a whole and the Union, a specific formula calculates the value of the vote of each MLA and MP based on population. A candidate must secure a predetermined quota of votes to be declared elected.

**Removal Procedure (Impeachment):** The President holds office for a term of five years but can be removed before the term expires through a process called impeachment, detailed in Article 61. The sole ground for impeachment is a "violation of the Constitution." The impeachment charges can be initiated in either House of Parliament (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha). The resolution to initiate charges must be signed by at least one-fourth of the total members of the House and requires a 14-day advance notice given to the President. If the resolution is passed by a special majority (two-thirds of the total membership of that House), it is sent to the other House, which acts as the investigating body. The President has the right to appear and be represented at such an investigation. If the investigating House sustains the charges and passes the impeachment resolution with a special majority (two-thirds of its total membership), the President stands removed from office from the date on which the resolution is passed. This stringent procedure ensures that removal is not based on frivolous political grounds.

### 15 b) Explain any three functions of Parliament
The Parliament of India, comprising the President, the Lok Sabha (House of the People), and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States), is the supreme legislative body and the fulcrum of the democratic system. Its functions are multifaceted, ranging from law-making to ensuring executive accountability.

**1. Legislative Functions:** The primary and most evident function of Parliament is making laws for the governance of the country. Parliament exercises supreme legislative power over the subjects enumerated in the Union List and Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule. During an emergency, it can also legislate on matters in the State List. The legislative process involves introducing a bill (ordinary, money, or constitutional amendment), navigating it through various readings and committee scrutiny, and passing it by both Houses before it receives Presidential assent to become an Act. Parliament is responsible for enacting broad socio-economic legislation, amending existing laws, and shaping the legal framework of the nation.

**2. Executive Accountability and Control:** In a parliamentary democracy, the executive (the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister) is drawn from the legislature and remains in power only as long as it enjoys the confidence of the Lok Sabha. Parliament exercises continuous control over the executive through various mechanisms. Members can hold ministers accountable during the Question Hour, raise issues of urgent public importance through Adjournment Motions, and initiate debates on government policies. The most potent tool is the No-Confidence Motion; if passed in the Lok Sabha, the entire Council of Ministers must resign, demonstrating Parliament's ultimate authority over the executive's existence.

**3. Financial Functions:** Parliament holds the "power of the purse," meaning the government cannot levy or collect any tax, nor spend any public money, without parliamentary authorization. This function is exercised primarily through the presentation and passage of the Annual Financial Statement (the Budget). Parliament scrutinizes the government's estimated receipts and expenditure, votes on demands for grants, and passes the Finance Bill and Appropriation Bill. Furthermore, parliamentary committees like the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) and Estimates Committee meticulously examine government spending to ensure funds are utilized legally, efficiently, and effectively, thereby maintaining strict financial discipline.

### 16 a) Explain the powers and functions of the Prime Minister
The Prime Minister of India is the real executive head of the state, serving as the head of the government. While the President is the nominal executive, the Constitution vests the actual administrative authority in the Prime Minister and their Council of Ministers. The Prime Minister's powers and functions are vast, spanning across executive, legislative, and administrative domains, making the office the most powerful in the Indian political system.

**Relation to the Council of Ministers:** The Prime Minister is the keystone of the cabinet arch. Following their appointment by the President, the Prime Minister recommends other persons to be appointed as ministers. The Prime Minister has full discretion to allocate portfolios among ministers, reshuffle them, or demand the resignation of any minister. The Prime Minister presides over cabinet meetings, directs discussions, and ensures that decisions are cohesive. Importantly, the resignation or death of the Prime Minister automatically dissolves the entire Council of Ministers, highlighting their central role.

**Relation to the President:** The Prime Minister serves as the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers (Article 78). It is the Prime Minister's duty to communicate all decisions of the Council relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation to the President. Furthermore, the Prime Minister advises the President on critical appointments, including the Attorney General of India, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner, Chairman and members of UPSC, and Governors of states. 

**Relation to Parliament:** As the leader of the lower house (usually the Lok Sabha) and the leader of the majority party, the Prime Minister plays a dominant role in the legislature. They advise the President regarding the summoning and proroguing of the sessions of Parliament and can recommend the dissolution of the Lok Sabha at any time. The Prime Minister announces major government policies on the floor of the House and intervenes in important debates to clarify the government's stance.

Beyond these, the Prime Minister is the chairman of key bodies like the NITI Aayog (formerly Planning Commission), the National Development Council, and the Inter-State Council. They play a crucial role in shaping foreign policy, represent the country in international forums, and serve as the chief crisis manager during national emergencies.

### 16 b) Supreme Court may in its discretion to grant to special leave to appeal. Examine the situation
Article 136 of the Indian Constitution grants the Supreme Court a unique and expansive discretionary power: the authority to grant Special Leave to Appeal. This power allows the Supreme Court to hear an appeal against any judgment, decree, determination, sentence, or order passed or made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India (except those constituted under laws relating to the Armed Forces). 

The significance of Article 136 lies in its exceptional nature. Unlike regular appeals where a litigant has a statutory right to approach the higher court, the Special Leave Petition (SLP) is not a right; it is a privilege granted entirely at the discretion of the Supreme Court. The primary rationale behind this provision is to ensure that justice is not thwarted by procedural technicalities or rigid jurisdictional limits. It acts as a residual, overarching mechanism to allow the highest court to intervene whenever it perceives that substantial injustice has occurred.

The Supreme Court exercises this discretion sparingly and generally only in exceptional circumstances. Situations that may prompt the Court to grant special leave include cases where a substantial question of law of general public importance is involved, where there has been a gross miscarriage of justice due to a lower court acting without jurisdiction or exceeding it, or where a lower court's decision is perverse, arbitrary, or violates the principles of natural justice. The Court uses this power to correct manifest errors that shock the judicial conscience, ensuring that the finality of lower court decisions does not stand in the way of ultimate justice.

### 17 a) Discuss the qualification and disqualification of the membership of state legislature
The membership of a State Legislature—whether the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) or the Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad)—is governed by constitutional provisions detailing qualifications to ensure competent representation, and disqualifications to maintain the integrity of the legislative process.

**Qualifications (Article 173):** To be qualified to be chosen to fill a seat in the legislature of a State, a person must meet foundational criteria. Firstly, they must be a citizen of India and make and subscribe an oath or affirmation before an authority authorized by the Election Commission. Secondly, there is an age requirement: a candidate must be not less than 25 years of age in the case of a seat in the Legislative Assembly, and not less than 30 years of age in the case of a seat in the Legislative Council. Thirdly, the candidate must possess any other qualifications prescribed in that behalf by or under any law made by Parliament (such as being an elector for an assembly constituency in that state, as stipulated by the Representation of the People Act, 1951).

**Disqualifications (Article 191):** The Constitution lays down specific grounds on which a person shall be disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member of the Legislative Assembly or Legislative Council of a State. These include:
1. **Office of Profit:** Holding any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of any State, other than an office declared by the legislature not to disqualify its holder. This prevents a conflict of interest between legislative duties and executive employment.
2. **Unsound Mind:** Being declared of unsound mind by a competent court.
3. **Insolvency:** Being an undischarged insolvent.
4. **Citizenship:** Not being a citizen of India, or voluntarily acquiring the citizenship of a foreign State, or being under any acknowledgment of allegiance to a foreign State.
5. **Parliamentary Law:** Being disqualified by or under any law made by Parliament. Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, additional disqualifications include conviction for certain offenses leading to imprisonment of two or more years, failure to lodge an account of election expenses, or being found guilty of corrupt election practices.

Additionally, under the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law), a member is disqualified if they voluntarily give up the membership of their political party or vote against the party whip, subject to certain exceptions for party splits or mergers.

### 17 b) What are the duties and functions of Advocate general of the state
The Advocate General is the highest law officer of a State, appointed by the Governor under Article 165 of the Indian Constitution. The office is parallel to that of the Attorney General of India at the national level. To be appointed, a person must be qualified to be appointed a Judge of a High Court (i.e., a citizen of India who has held a judicial office for ten years or has been a High Court advocate for ten years).

The primary duty of the Advocate General is to provide legal advice to the Government of the State upon such legal matters, and to perform such other duties of a legal character, as may from time to time be referred or assigned to them by the Governor. They represent the state government in significant legal proceedings before the High Court or any other court within the state. Additionally, they discharge functions conferred on them by or under the Constitution or any other law. 

Crucially, under Article 177, the Advocate General has the right to speak in, and otherwise take part in the proceedings of, the Legislative Assembly of the State (and the Legislative Council, if one exists), as well as any committee of the Legislature of which they may be named a member. However, they do not possess the right to vote in these legislative proceedings. They hold office during the pleasure of the Governor and receive remuneration as determined by the Governor.

### 18 a) List out the different jurisdiction and powers enjoyed by the High Court and explain original and writ jurisdiction in detail
High Courts stand at the apex of the judicial administration in a state and possess extensive jurisdiction and diverse powers under the Indian Constitution. The various jurisdictions of a High Court generally include Original Jurisdiction, Writ Jurisdiction, Appellate Jurisdiction, Supervisory Jurisdiction, control over subordinate courts, and acting as a Court of Record. 

**Original Jurisdiction:** Original jurisdiction refers to the power of a High Court to hear cases in the first instance, rather than on appeal from lower courts. Unlike the Supreme Court, whose original jurisdiction is largely confined to federal disputes, the original jurisdiction of High Courts is more varied. Primarily, it extends to matters of admiralty, probate, matrimonial causes, and contempt of court. It also covers election disputes pertaining to Members of Parliament and Members of the State Legislature. In civil matters, some of the older High Courts (like Bombay, Madras, and Calcutta) possess original civil jurisdiction for cases involving higher monetary values arising within their respective presidency towns. However, for most other High Courts, original jurisdiction in civil and criminal matters is limited, as the foundational tiers of the subordinate judiciary handle these in the first instance.

**Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226):** The writ jurisdiction of a High Court is one of its most potent constitutional powers, crucial for protecting the rights of citizens and ensuring the rule of law. Article 226 empowers every High Court to issue orders, directions, or writs—including habeas corpus (to produce a detained person), mandamus (to command a public authority to perform a duty), prohibition (to prevent a lower court from exceeding jurisdiction), quo warranto (to inquire into the legality of a claim to public office), and certiorari (to quash the order of a lower court)—to any person or authority within its territorial jurisdiction. 

Significantly, the writ jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226 is broader than that of the Supreme Court under Article 32. While the Supreme Court can issue writs only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights (Part III), a High Court can issue writs not only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights but also "for any other purpose." This phrase implies that High Courts can intervene to enforce ordinary legal rights, statutory rights, or administrative justice where a substantial legal injury has occurred and no adequate alternative remedy exists, making the High Court a powerful sentinel of citizen rights against state overreach at the provincial level.

### 18 b) What are the powers enjoyed by the Governor
The Governor is the constitutional head of the State, acting as the representative of the Central Government while functioning as the nominal executive of the state. The powers enjoyed by the Governor are broadly classified into Executive, Legislative, Financial, and Judicial powers.

**Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the state government are formally taken in the Governor's name. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on their advice, appoints other ministers. Crucially, the Governor appoints key state officials like the Advocate General, the Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission, and the State Election Commissioner. The Governor can seek information from the Chief Minister regarding administrative affairs and proposed legislation. 
**Legislative Powers:** The Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature. They have the power to summon, prorogue the legislature, and dissolve the Legislative Assembly. The Governor addresses the first session of the legislature each year. Most importantly, a bill passed by the state legislature becomes a law only after receiving the Governor's assent; the Governor can assent to the bill, withhold assent, return it for reconsideration (if it's not a money bill), or reserve it for the President's consideration. The Governor can also promulgate Ordinances when the legislature is not in session.
**Financial Powers:** A Money Bill can be introduced in the state assembly only with the prior recommendation of the Governor. They ensure the Annual Financial Statement (State Budget) is laid before the legislature. The Governor also controls the state Contingency Fund to meet unforeseen expenditures.
**Judicial Powers:** Under Article 161, the Governor has the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit, or commute sentences for any person convicted of an offense against a state law. The Governor is also consulted by the President in the appointment of judges to the state High Court.

### 19 a) What are the five extraordinary circumstances on which the Constitution empowers the Parliament to make laws on any matter enumerated in the State list?
The Indian Constitution embodies a federal structure with a clear division of legislative powers between the Union (Parliament) and the States (State Legislatures) through the Seventh Schedule (Union, State, and Concurrent Lists). Generally, Parliament cannot legislate on subjects enumerated in the State List. However, to ensure flexibility and address national imperatives, the Constitution specifies five extraordinary circumstances under which Parliament acquires the power to make laws on subjects in the State List:

1. **Resolution by the Rajya Sabha (Article 249):** If the Rajya Sabha (Council of States) passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the "national interest" that Parliament should make laws with respect to a matter enumerated in the State List, Parliament becomes empowered to legislate on that matter. Such a resolution remains in force for up to one year, but can be renewed indefinitely for one year at a time.
2. **During a National Emergency (Article 250):** While a Proclamation of National Emergency (under Article 352) is in operation, the federal distribution of powers is effectively suspended. Parliament acquires the concurrent power to legislate on any subject mentioned in the State List for the whole or any part of the territory of India. Laws made under this provision become inoperative six months after the emergency ceases.
3. **By Agreement/Consent of States (Article 252):** If the legislatures of two or more States pass resolutions requesting Parliament to enact laws on a matter in the State List, Parliament can legislate on that matter. However, such a law applies only to those states that passed the resolution; any other state may adopt it subsequently by passing a similar resolution. Once Parliament legislates under this provision, the amending or repealing power also vests solely with Parliament, not the states.
4. **To Implement International Agreements (Article 253):** To fulfill international obligations, Parliament has the power to make any law for the whole or any part of India for implementing any treaty, agreement, or convention with any other country or countries, or any decision made at any international conference. This power allows Parliament to override the State List to ensure the country complies with its international commitments.
5. **During President's Rule (Article 356 read with Article 357):** When a proclamation of failure of constitutional machinery in a State (President's Rule) is issued, the President can declare that the powers of the State Legislature shall be exercisable by or under the authority of Parliament. Consequently, Parliament can make laws on State List subjects for that specific state. Even after President's rule is lifted, laws made by Parliament during this period continue to be operative until repealed or amended by the state legislature.

### 19 b) Briefly explain the grants – in – aid the state
Grants-in-aid are a crucial mechanism in federal finance, designed to transfer resources from the Central Government to the State Governments. While the Constitution assigns specific revenue-generating powers (like taxes) to both tiers, the Centre generally has more buoyant sources of revenue, whereas the states have extensive expenditure responsibilities for social and economic development. Grants-in-aid are used to bridge this vertical fiscal imbalance and to address horizontal imbalances (disparities among different states).

The Constitution provides for two main types of grants-in-aid: Statutory Grants and Discretionary Grants. 
**Statutory Grants (Article 275):** These grants are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India every year and are given to specific states that are in need of financial assistance, not to every state. The amount of the grant and the states to receive them are determined by Parliament on the recommendation of the Finance Commission. These are often used to cover revenue deficits in state budgets or to fund specific schemes promoting the welfare of Scheduled Tribes or raising the level of administration in Scheduled Areas.
**Discretionary Grants (Article 282):** Both the Centre and the States can make grants for any public purpose, even if the purpose is not within their respective legislative competence. Under this article, the Centre provides substantial financial assistance to states for plan expenditures and specific central sector or centrally sponsored schemes. Since these are given at the discretion of the government (often based on the erstwhile Planning Commission, now NITI Aayog guidelines), they form a significant, flexible tool for directing state development priorities aligned with national objectives.

### 20 a) Explain three types of emergencies under Indian Constitution
Part XVIII of the Indian Constitution (Articles 352 to 360) empowers the President to tackle abnormal situations effectively through provisions for three distinct types of emergencies. These provisions are designed to protect the sovereignty, unity, integrity, and security of the country, as well as the democratic political system, though they alter the federal structure to a unitary one during their operation.

**1. National Emergency (Article 352):** A National Emergency can be proclaimed by the President when the security of India or a part of it is threatened by "war or external aggression or armed rebellion." (The term 'internal disturbance' was replaced by 'armed rebellion' by the 44th Amendment in 1978 to prevent misuse). The President can declare this emergency only upon receiving a written recommendation from the Cabinet. Once proclaimed, it must be approved by both Houses of Parliament by a special majority within one month. During a National Emergency, the Centre becomes all-powerful; Parliament can legislate on State List subjects, the Centre can give executive directions to states on any matter, and the distribution of financial resources between the Centre and states can be altered. Crucially, Fundamental Rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended (except in case of armed rebellion), and the right to move courts for enforcement of other Fundamental Rights (except Articles 20 and 21) can be suspended by a presidential order.

**2. State Emergency or President's Rule (Article 356):** This is declared when there is a "failure of constitutional machinery in a State." The President can impose this rule based on a report from the Governor of the state or otherwise, if satisfied that the state government cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. It must be approved by Parliament by a simple majority within two months. Under President's Rule, the elected state government is dismissed, the State Legislative Assembly is either suspended or dissolved, and the Governor administers the state on behalf of the President. The legislative powers of the state are assumed by Parliament. This emergency does not affect Fundamental Rights.

**3. Financial Emergency (Article 360):** The President can proclaim a Financial Emergency if satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India, or any part thereof, is threatened. This proclamation must be approved by Parliament by a simple majority within two months. Once declared, the executive authority of the Centre expands to give directions to any state to observe specified canons of financial propriety. The Centre can direct the reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the State, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Furthermore, all money bills passed by the state legislatures can be reserved for the President's consideration.

### 20 b) What are the functions of interstate council
The Inter-State Council is a constitutional body established under Article 263 of the Indian Constitution to promote cooperative federalism by facilitating coordination between the Union and the States, and among the States themselves. While the Constitution permitted its creation, it was formally established only in 1990 via a Presidential order, based on the recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission. 

The primary function of the Inter-State Council is to act as an advisory, recommendatory, and consultative body on matters of common interest. Specifically, Article 263 outlines its duties as:
1.  **Inquiring into and advising upon disputes** which may have arisen between States. While the Supreme Court has original jurisdiction over inter-state legal disputes, the Council provides a political and non-judicial forum for dialogue and consensus-building to resolve issues before they escalate.
2.  **Investigating and discussing subjects** in which some or all of the States, or the Union and one or more of the States, have a common interest. This encompasses a wide range of policy issues impacting the federal structure.
3.  **Making recommendations** upon any such subject, particularly recommendations for the better coordination of policy and action with respect to that subject. 

By bringing together the Prime Minister, Chief Ministers of all States, and key Union Cabinet Ministers on a single platform, the Council serves as the supreme institutional mechanism for harmonizing policies, addressing grievances, and ensuring smooth functioning of the federal polity through debate and cooperation rather than litigation or central imposition.
