## 11. a) Explain the different commission for reorganisation of states in India. (10 marks)
## 11. b) Explain about the policy of Indian constitution about acquisition of foreign territory. (4 marks)

**a) Commissions for Reorganisation of States in India**
The reorganization of states in India after independence was a complex and sensitive issue, primarily driven by demands for linguistic provinces. To address this, the government appointed several commissions. The first was the Dhar Commission (Linguistic Provinces Commission) in 1948, headed by S.K. Dhar. This commission recommended the reorganization of states based on administrative convenience rather than linguistic considerations, arguing that linguistic reorganization might threaten national unity. This recommendation caused widespread disappointment, leading to the formation of the JVP Committee in 1948, consisting of Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, and Pattabhi Sitaramayya. The JVP Committee also formally rejected language as the sole basis for state reorganization, emphasizing security, unity, and economic prosperity. 
However, the tragic death of Potti Sreeramulu after a prolonged fast demanding a separate Telugu-speaking state forced the government to concede, leading to the creation of Andhra State in 1953. This event triggered similar demands across the country. Consequently, the Fazl Ali Commission (States Reorganisation Commission) was appointed in 1953, comprising Fazl Ali, K.M. Panikkar, and H.N. Kunzru. Submitting its report in 1955, the commission broadly accepted language as the basis for reorganization but rejected the theory of 'one language, one state'. It emphasized four major factors: preservation and strengthening of the unity and security of the country; linguistic and cultural homogeneity; financial, economic, and administrative considerations; and successful working of national development plans. Based on its recommendations, the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 was passed, which significantly altered the political map of India, creating 14 states and 6 Union Territories. Since then, further reorganizations have occurred, often based on linguistic, ethnic, or administrative grounds, leading to the current structure of Indian states. These commissions played a pivotal role in shaping the internal political boundaries of modern India, balancing regional aspirations with the overriding need for national integration.

**b) Policy on Acquisition of Foreign Territory**
The Indian Constitution, under Article 1, defines the territory of India, which includes the territories of the states, union territories, and "such other territories as may be acquired." This provision explicitly recognizes the sovereign right of the Indian State to acquire foreign territory. International law recognizes several modes of acquiring territory, such as cession (following a treaty or agreement), occupation (of territory hitherto unoccupied by a recognized sovereign), conquest, or subjugation. The Constitution implicitly adopts these principles of international law. The power to acquire new territory is an inherent attribute of sovereignty and does not require a specific constitutional amendment for the act of acquisition itself. However, once a foreign territory is acquired, Parliament has the power under Article 2 to admit it into the Union or establish it as a new state on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. Alternatively, it can be administered as a Union Territory under Article 239. The process of integrating acquired territories into the constitutional framework highlights India's sovereign capabilities while ensuring that new territories are governed according to democratic and constitutional principles. Notable examples of acquired territories include Goa, Daman and Diu, Puducherry, and Sikkim.

## 12. a) Explain in detail about modes of acquiring and Loss of Indian Citizenship. (10 marks)
## 12. b) Explain the important Indian constitutional history. (4 marks)

**a) Modes of Acquiring and Loss of Indian Citizenship**
Citizenship signifies the legal relationship between an individual and a state, granting rights and imposing duties. The Indian Constitution, in Articles 5 to 11 (Part II), deals with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution, but empowers Parliament to regulate citizenship matters subsequently. Consequently, Parliament enacted the Citizenship Act, 1955, which details the modes of acquiring and losing Indian citizenship.
There are five primary modes of acquiring Indian citizenship:
1. **By Birth:** A person born in India is considered a citizen by birth. The specific conditions regarding the citizenship of parents have evolved. Currently, a person born in India is a citizen if both parents are Indian citizens, or if one is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. **By Descent:** A person born outside India can acquire citizenship by descent if either of their parents is an Indian citizen at the time of birth, subject to registration requirements at an Indian consulate.
3. **By Registration:** Persons of Indian origin (PIOs) ordinarily resident in India for a specified period (usually 7 years), persons married to Indian citizens, and minor children of Indian citizens can apply for citizenship by registration.
4. **By Naturalization:** Foreigners (not illegal migrants) who have resided in India for a specified period (typically 11 years, though exceptions exist) and meet other qualifications like good character and knowledge of an Eighth Schedule language can acquire citizenship by naturalization.
5. **By Incorporation of Territory:** If any new territory becomes part of India, the Government of India specifies the persons from that territory who shall be citizens of India.

The Citizenship Act, 1955 also provides three ways in which Indian citizenship can be lost:
1. **Renunciation:** Any adult citizen of full capacity can voluntarily declare their intent to renounce Indian citizenship. Upon registration of such a declaration, they cease to be a citizen. If made during a war, the registration may be withheld by the government.
2. **Termination:** If an Indian citizen voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, their Indian citizenship automatically terminates. This is because India does not permit dual citizenship.
3. **Deprivation:** The Government of India can compulsorily deprive a person of their citizenship if it was acquired by fraud, false representation, or concealment of material facts; if the person has shown disloyalty to the Constitution; if they have unlawfully communicated with an enemy during a war; or if a naturalized citizen has been imprisoned in any country for two years within five years of naturalization.

**b) Important Indian Constitutional History**
The Indian constitutional history is deeply rooted in the various acts passed by the British Parliament to administer India. Key milestones include the Regulating Act of 1773, which first attempted to centralize administration; the Pitt’s India Act of 1784; and the Charter Acts (1813, 1833, 1853), which gradually shifted the East India Company's role from commercial to administrative. The Government of India Act 1858 marked the direct assumption of power by the British Crown. Subsequent acts like the Indian Councils Acts (1861, 1892, 1909 - Morley-Minto Reforms) slowly introduced representative elements. The Government of India Act 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms) introduced diarchy in provinces. The most crucial predecessor to the current Constitution was the Government of India Act 1935, which proposed a federal structure and provincial autonomy, and provided much of the structural framework for the 1950 Constitution. The Constituent Assembly, formed in 1946 based on the Cabinet Mission Plan, finally drafted the Constitution, culminating in its adoption on November 26, 1949, and commencement on January 26, 1950.

## 13. a) Explain in detail about the fundamental Rights of Indian constitution. (10 marks)
## 13. b) Distinguish between fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of Indian constitution. (4 marks)

**a) Fundamental Rights of the Indian Constitution**
Enshrined in Part III (Articles 12 to 35) of the Constitution, Fundamental Rights are the cornerstone of Indian democracy, guaranteeing essential liberties to all citizens and, in some cases, to foreigners. These rights are justiciable, meaning they can be enforced by the courts, and they act as limitations on the arbitrary power of the State. The Constitution originally provided for seven fundamental rights, but the Right to Property was removed and made a legal right by the 44th Amendment Act in 1978. The remaining six categories are:
1. **Right to Equality (Articles 14-18):** This encompasses equality before the law and equal protection of laws (Art. 14); prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (Art. 15); equality of opportunity in matters of public employment (Art. 16); abolition of untouchability (Art. 17); and abolition of titles (Art. 18).
2. **Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22):** This crucial section guarantees six specific freedoms to citizens (Art. 19): speech and expression, peaceful assembly, forming associations/unions, moving freely throughout India, residing and settling in any part of India, and practicing any profession/carrying on any occupation. These are subject to reasonable restrictions. It also provides protection in respect of conviction for offenses (Art. 20, covering ex post facto laws, double jeopardy, and self-incrimination); protection of life and personal liberty (Art. 21, broadly interpreted to include the right to privacy, dignity, and a clean environment); the right to education (Art. 21A); and protection against arrest and detention in certain cases (Art. 22).
3. **Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24):** This prohibits traffic in human beings and forced labor (begar) (Art. 23) and prohibits the employment of children below the age of 14 years in factories, mines, or other hazardous employment (Art. 24).
4. **Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28):** This guarantees the freedom of conscience and free profession, practice, and propagation of religion (Art. 25); freedom to manage religious affairs (Art. 26); freedom from payment of taxes for promotion of any particular religion (Art. 27); and freedom from attending religious instruction in certain educational institutions (Art. 28).
5. **Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30):** These rights protect the interests of minorities by allowing any section of citizens to conserve their distinct language, script, or culture (Art. 29) and granting all religious and linguistic minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice (Art. 30).
6. **Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32):** Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called this the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. It empowers individuals to move the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of their Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court can issue various writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto) to protect these rights.

**b) Fundamental Rights vs. Directive Principles**
Fundamental Rights (FRs, Part III) and Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs, Part IV) both aim to create an egalitarian society but differ fundamentally in their nature and enforceability.
Firstly, FRs are largely negative obligations on the State; they prohibit the State from doing certain things (e.g., discriminating, denying liberty). DPSPs, conversely, are positive obligations; they direct the State to undertake specific actions to improve socio-economic conditions (e.g., providing equal pay, securing a living wage).
Secondly, and most importantly, FRs are justiciable; a citizen can approach the courts if they are violated. DPSPs are non-justiciable; they are not enforceable by any court, though they are fundamental in the governance of the country.
Thirdly, FRs aim at establishing political democracy by guaranteeing civil liberties, while DPSPs aim at establishing social and economic democracy by outlining welfare goals.
Despite these differences, the Supreme Court has often held that they are complementary; the Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between FRs and DPSPs, and together they form the core of the Constitution's social philosophy.

## 14. a) Explain in detail about the fundamental Duties of Indian constitution. (10 marks)
## 14. b) Explain the classification of Directive Principles of state policy. (4 marks)

**a) Fundamental Duties of the Indian Constitution**
The Fundamental Duties were not part of the original Constitution drafted in 1950. They were added to the Constitution by the 42nd Amendment Act in 1976, during the Internal Emergency, based on the recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee. Inspired by the Constitution of the erstwhile Soviet Union, these duties are enshrined in Part IV-A, comprising a single Article, 51-A. Originally ten in number, an eleventh duty was added by the 86th Amendment Act in 2002.
The rationale behind Fundamental Duties is to remind citizens that while the Constitution grants them fundamental rights, these rights are not absolute and come with reciprocal obligations towards the nation, society, and fellow citizens. They serve as a moral compass and a code of conduct for citizens. The eleven Fundamental Duties require citizens to:
1. Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem.
2. Cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
3. Uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India.
4. Defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
5. Promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all people of India, transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities, and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
6. Value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture.
7. Protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife, and have compassion for living creatures.
8. Develop the scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
9. Safeguard public property and abjure violence.
10. Strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavor and achievement.
11. (Added in 2002) Provide opportunities for education to his child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years.
Unlike Fundamental Rights, Fundamental Duties are non-justiciable; there is no direct legal provision for their enforcement or sanction for their violation in the Constitution itself. However, Parliament is free to enforce them through suitable legislation (e.g., laws protecting the national flag or the environment). Furthermore, the judiciary often uses Fundamental Duties to interpret the Constitution, holding that any law seeking to give effect to a duty is considered 'reasonable' in relation to the restrictions on fundamental rights.

**b) Classification of Directive Principles of State Policy**
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSPs), contained in Part IV of the Constitution, are guidelines or principles that the State must keep in mind while formulating policies and enacting laws. While the Constitution itself does not formally classify them, scholars generally group them into three broad categories based on their ideological content and objectives:
1. **Socialist Principles:** These principles aim at providing social and economic justice and set the path towards a welfare state. Examples include Article 38 (promoting the welfare of the people), Article 39 (securing adequate means of livelihood, equitable distribution of resources, equal pay for equal work), Article 41 (right to work, education, and public assistance in cases of unemployment, old age, sickness), and Article 43 (securing a living wage and decent standard of life for workers).
2. **Gandhian Principles:** Based on Gandhian ideology, these represent the program of reconstruction enunciated by Mahatma Gandhi during the national movement. Examples include Article 40 (organizing village panchayats), Article 43 (promoting cottage industries), Article 46 (promoting educational and economic interests of SCs, STs, and other weaker sections), Article 47 (prohibiting intoxicating drinks and drugs), and Article 48 (prohibiting the slaughter of cows and calves).
3. **Liberal-Intellectual Principles:** These principles reflect the ideology of liberalism. Examples include Article 44 (securing a Uniform Civil Code for all citizens), Article 45 (provision for early childhood care and education), Article 48 (organizing agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines), Article 49 (protecting monuments of national importance), Article 50 (separating the judiciary from the executive), and Article 51 (promoting international peace and security).

## 15. a) Explain about Vice President of India, functions and related Articles. (10 marks)
## 15. b) Explain about the Union Executive of India. (4 marks)

**a) Vice President of India, Functions, and Related Articles**
The Vice President of India occupies the second-highest constitutional office in the country, next only to the President. The office is modeled on the lines of the American Vice President. The relevant constitutional provisions concerning the Vice President are found in Articles 63 to 71.
Article 63 mandates that "There shall be a Vice-President of India." According to Article 66, the Vice President is elected not directly by the people but by the method of indirect election. The electoral college consists of the members of both Houses of Parliament (elected and nominated members both participate, unlike the Presidential election where only elected members vote, and state legislators do not participate). The election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote. The Vice President holds office for a term of five years from the date they enter office (Article 67) but can resign earlier or be removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Rajya Sabha and agreed to by the Lok Sabha. To be eligible, a person must be a citizen of India, have completed 35 years of age, and be qualified for election as a member of the Rajya Sabha.
The functions of the Vice President are twofold:
1. **Ex-officio Chairman of Rajya Sabha (Article 64):** The Vice President's primary and most regular function is to preside over the sessions of the Rajya Sabha (the upper house of Parliament). In this capacity, their powers and functions are similar to those of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha. They maintain order in the house, allow members to speak, and decide points of order. They do not have a regular vote but can cast a deciding vote in case of a tie.
2. **Acting President (Article 65):** The Vice President acts as the President in the event of a vacancy in the office of the President occurring due to death, resignation, removal, or otherwise. They also discharge the functions of the President when the President is unable to discharge their duties due to absence, illness, or any other cause, until the President resumes their duties. While acting as President, the Vice President does not perform the duties of the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and is entitled to the salary and allowances of the President.

**b) The Union Executive of India**
The Union Executive in India represents the machinery of the central government responsible for executing and administering laws and policies. Enshrined in Part V of the Constitution (Articles 52 to 78), the Union Executive consists of the President, the Vice-President, the Prime Minister, the Council of Ministers, and the Attorney General of India. 
India follows a parliamentary system of government, which is characterized by a dual executive structure: a nominal head and a real head. The President is the nominal or titular head of the state (de jure executive). All executive actions are formally taken in the President's name. However, the real executive power (de facto executive) lies with the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. The Constitution mandates that the President shall exercise their functions in accordance with the aid and advice tendered by the Council of Ministers (Article 74). The Prime Minister is the head of government and the most powerful political figure in the country. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha, meaning they remain in power only as long as they enjoy the confidence of the lower house. The Attorney General serves as the highest legal advisor to the Union Executive.

## 16. a) Explain about Prime Minister of India, functions and related Articles. (10 marks)
## 16. b) Explain about the Council of Minister. (4 marks)

**a) Prime Minister of India, Functions, and Related Articles**
In the parliamentary system established by the Indian Constitution, the Prime Minister is the real executive authority (de facto head), the head of the government, and the most pivotal figure in the country's political administration. While the President is the head of the State, the Prime Minister exercises the actual executive powers.
The Constitution does not contain any specific procedure for the selection and appointment of the Prime Minister. Article 75 merely states that "the Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President." By convention, the President appoints the leader of the majority party (or a coalition of parties) in the Lok Sabha as the Prime Minister. The Prime Minister's term is not fixed; they hold office during the pleasure of the President, which in practice means as long as they enjoy the majority support in the Lok Sabha.
The functions and powers of the Prime Minister are vast and multifaceted:
1. **In relation to the Council of Ministers:** The Prime Minister recommends persons to be appointed as ministers by the President. They allocate and reshuffle various portfolios among the ministers, preside over meetings of the Council of Ministers and influence its decisions, and can ask a minister to resign or advise the President to dismiss them. The resignation or death of the Prime Minister automatically dissolves the Council of Ministers.
2. **In relation to the President (Article 78):** The Prime Minister serves as the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers. It is the duty of the Prime Minister to communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to administration and legislation, and to furnish information requested by the President. The Prime Minister advises the President regarding the appointment of important officials like the Attorney General, CAG, UPSC Chairman, etc.
3. **In relation to Parliament:** The Prime Minister is the leader of the lower house. They advise the President with regard to summoning and proroguing the sessions of Parliament, can recommend the dissolution of the Lok Sabha to the President at any time, and announce government policies on the floor of the House.
4. **Other roles:** The Prime Minister is the chairman of important bodies like the NITI Aayog (formerly Planning Commission), National Integration Council, and Inter-State Council. They play a significant role in shaping foreign policy and serve as the chief spokesperson of the Union government.

**b) The Council of Ministers**
The Council of Ministers is the core executive body in the Indian parliamentary system, headed by the Prime Minister. Article 74 states that there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President, who shall act in accordance with such advice. Article 75 stipulates that ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister, and that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This principle of collective responsibility means that the entire Council acts as a team; if a vote of no-confidence is passed in the Lok Sabha against the government, all ministers must resign. 
The Council comprises three categories of ministers: Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, and Deputy Ministers. Cabinet Ministers hold key portfolios (like Home, Defense, Finance) and constitute the Cabinet, the inner decision-making circle. Ministers of State can either have independent charge of ministries/departments or be attached to Cabinet Ministers. Deputy Ministers are attached to Cabinet Ministers or Ministers of State to assist them. The total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, cannot exceed 15% of the total strength of the Lok Sabha (as per the 91st Amendment Act, 2003).

## 17. a) Explain the powers and functions of Governor of Indian constitution. (10 marks)
## 17. b) What are the qualifications needed for Governor? (4 marks)

**a) Powers and Functions of the Governor**
The Governor is the constitutional head of a State in India, appointed by the President. While functioning largely as a titular head analogous to the President at the center, the Governor also acts as an agent of the central government, thereby holding a dual role. The Governor’s powers and functions are extensive, though primarily exercised on the aid and advice of the State Council of Ministers.
1. **Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the state government are formally taken in the Governor's name. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and, on the Chief Minister's advice, appoints other ministers. The Governor also appoints the Advocate General, the Chairman and members of the State Public Service Commission, and the State Election Commissioner. The Governor can seek information from the Chief Minister regarding administrative and legislative matters. During President's Rule (Article 356), the Governor assumes extensive executive powers and administers the state on behalf of the President.
2. **Legislative Powers:** The Governor is an integral part of the State Legislature. They summon and prorogue the sessions of the state legislature and can dissolve the legislative assembly. The Governor addresses the legislature at the commencement of the first session each year. Crucially, a bill passed by the state legislature becomes law only after receiving the Governor's assent. The Governor can give assent, withhold assent, return the bill (if not a money bill) for reconsideration, or reserve the bill for the consideration of the President (especially if it derogates the powers of the High Court or is against national interest). The Governor also has the power to promulgate ordinances when the legislature is not in session, which have the same force as a law passed by the legislature.
3. **Financial Powers:** The Governor causes the annual state budget to be laid before the legislature. No money bill can be introduced in the legislative assembly without the Governor's prior recommendation. The Governor also constitutes the State Finance Commission every five years.
4. **Judicial Powers:** The Governor has the power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute the sentence of any person convicted of any offense against any law relating to a matter to which the executive power of the state extends. The Governor is also consulted by the President in the appointment of judges to the state High Court.
5. **Discretionary Powers:** Unlike the President, the Governor possesses some constitutional discretionary powers where they are not bound by ministerial advice. These include reserving a bill for the President, recommending President's Rule, and seeking information from the Chief Minister.

**b) Qualifications for Governor**
The Constitution prescribes very simple qualifications for a person to be appointed as a Governor. According to Article 157 and Article 158:
1. The person must be a citizen of India.
2. The person must have completed 35 years of age.
Furthermore, the Constitution lays down certain conditions for the Governor's office: the Governor shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or a House of the State Legislature. If such a member is appointed as Governor, they are deemed to have vacated their seat in that House on the date they enter the office of the Governor. Additionally, the Governor must not hold any other office of profit. Beyond these constitutional requirements, a couple of conventions have developed over time: the person is usually an outsider (not belonging to the state they are appointed to) to ensure political detachment, and the President generally consults the Chief Minister of the state before appointing the Governor.

## 18. a) Explain the powers and functions of Chief Minister and State Legislature of Indian constitution. (10 marks)
## 18. b) Explain about advocate general of the state. (4 marks)

**a) Powers and Functions of Chief Minister and State Legislature**
**Chief Minister:** Analogous to the Prime Minister at the Union level, the Chief Minister (CM) is the real executive head at the state level. Appointed by the Governor (typically the leader of the majority party in the Legislative Assembly), the CM’s powers are substantial.
1. **Head of the Council of Ministers:** The CM forms the ministry, advising the Governor on who should be appointed as ministers. The CM allocates portfolios, can reshuffle the cabinet, and presides over cabinet meetings, driving the policy agenda of the state government. The resignation or dismissal of the CM brings down the entire Council of Ministers.
2. **Advisor to the Governor:** The CM is the primary communication channel between the Governor and the Council of Ministers. It is the CM's constitutional duty (Article 167) to inform the Governor of all cabinet decisions regarding administration and legislation. The CM advises the Governor on the appointment of key state officials like the Advocate General and members of the State Public Service Commission.
3. **Leader of the Legislature:** The CM is the leader of the lower house (Legislative Assembly) and plays a key role in steering the legislative business. The CM advises the Governor on summoning, proroguing, and dissolving the Assembly.

**State Legislature:** The State Legislature is the primary law-making body in a state. Some states have a bicameral legislature (Legislative Assembly and Legislative Council), while most have a unicameral one (only the Legislative Assembly).
1. **Legislative Powers:** Its main function is to enact laws on subjects listed in the State List and Concurrent List of the Seventh Schedule. The Assembly has the final say in ordinary bills.
2. **Financial Powers:** The legislature controls the state purse. The government cannot levy taxes or spend money without the legislature's approval. The Legislative Assembly has exclusive power over Money Bills.
3. **Executive Control:** The legislature exercises control over the executive. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly. The legislature can hold the government accountable through questions, debates, adjournment motions, and ultimately, by passing a vote of no-confidence, which forces the government to resign.
4. **Electoral Functions:** Elected members of the Legislative Assembly participate in the election of the President of India and the members of the Rajya Sabha from that state.

**b) Advocate General of the State**
The Advocate General is the highest law officer in a state, corresponding to the Attorney General at the Union level. The office is established by Article 165 of the Constitution. The Advocate General is appointed by the Governor of the state and holds office during the pleasure of the Governor. To be appointed, a person must be qualified to be appointed as a judge of a High Court (i.e., must be a citizen of India and must have held a judicial office in India for 10 years or been an advocate of a High Court for 10 years).
The primary function of the Advocate General is to give advice to the government of the state upon legal matters and to perform other duties of a legal character assigned by the Governor. The Advocate General represents the state government in legal proceedings before the High Court or any other court. Crucially, the Advocate General has the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of the state legislature and any committee to which they may be named a member, though they do not have the right to vote. This allows them to provide legal counsel during the legislative process.

## 19. a) Explain in detail about Indian Public Services. (10 marks)
## 19. b) What is the role of administrative tribunals in India? (4 marks)

**a) Indian Public Services**
The Indian public administration system relies heavily on a structured and professional civil service to implement government policies and ensure continuity in governance, irrespective of changes in political leadership. The Constitution of India provides a detailed framework for the public services under Part XIV (Articles 308 to 323). The public services in India are broadly classified into three categories: All-India Services, Central Civil Services, and State Civil Services.
1. **All-India Services (AIS):** These are unique to the Indian federal system. Members of these services are recruited and trained by the Central Government but are allotted to different State cadres. They serve both the Center and the States, acting as a crucial link and fostering national integration. Currently, there are three All-India Services: the Indian Administrative Service (IAS), the Indian Police Service (IPS), and the Indian Forest Service (IFoS). Article 312 empowers the Parliament to create new All-India Services if the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting declaring that it is necessary in the national interest. The officers of AIS are controlled jointly by the Central Government (which holds the ultimate disciplinary power) and the respective State Government (which has immediate control).
2. **Central Civil Services:** These services are exclusively under the jurisdiction of the Central Government. The personnel are responsible for the administration of Union subjects (like defense, foreign affairs, income tax, railways). Important Central Services include the Indian Foreign Service (IFS), Indian Revenue Service (IRS), Indian Audit and Accounts Service, and Indian Postal Service. These are classified into Group A, B, C, and D based on seniority and responsibility.
3. **State Civil Services:** These services function exclusively under the jurisdiction of the respective State Governments to administer subjects in the State List. They manage the administrative machinery at the district and lower levels. Examples include state administrative services (like deputy collectors), state police services (like deputy superintendents of police), and state educational services.
The Constitution provides crucial safeguards for civil servants to ensure their independence and political neutrality. Article 311 stipulates that no civil servant shall be dismissed or removed by an authority subordinate to that by which they were appointed, and they cannot be dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank except after an inquiry informing them of the charges and giving them a reasonable opportunity to be heard.

**b) Role of Administrative Tribunals in India**
Administrative tribunals are specialized adjudicatory bodies established to resolve disputes arising from administrative actions and service matters of public servants. They were incorporated into the Constitution by the 42nd Amendment Act in 1976, which added Part XIV-A (Articles 323A and 323B).
The primary role of these tribunals is to provide a speedy, inexpensive, and specialized mechanism for dispute resolution, reducing the massive burden on the regular judiciary (High Courts and the Supreme Court). 
Article 323A empowers Parliament to establish Administrative Tribunals specifically for adjudicating disputes and complaints regarding recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union or any State or local authority. Under this, the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 was passed, leading to the creation of the Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) for central government employees and State Administrative Tribunals (SATs) for state employees. 
These tribunals possess the characteristics of a court but are not bound by the strict procedural rules of the Civil Procedure Code or the Indian Evidence Act; they are guided by the principles of natural justice. They play a vital role in ensuring that civil servants have accessible redressal mechanisms for grievances related to promotions, disciplinary actions, and other service conditions.

## 20. a) Explain the duties and powers of comptroller and Auditor general of India. (10 marks)
## 20. b) What is the procedure for Amendment in Indian constitution? (4 marks)

**a) Duties and Powers of Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India**
The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India is an independent constitutional authority, described by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as arguably the most important officer under the Constitution. Established under Article 148, the CAG is the supreme audit institution of India, acting as the guardian of the public purse and ensuring accountability of the government (both central and state) to the legislature. The CAG is appointed by the President and can only be removed on grounds of proven misbehavior or incapacity, following a stringent procedure similar to that of a Supreme Court judge, ensuring profound independence.
The duties and powers of the CAG are broadly defined in Article 149 and detailed in the CAG's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971.
1. **Auditing Accounts:** The core function of the CAG is auditing. The CAG audits all receipts and expenditures from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and the Consolidated Fund of each Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly. 
2. **Contingency and Public Accounts:** The CAG also audits all transactions relating to the Contingency Fund and the Public Account of both the Union and the States.
3. **Auditing Government Entities:** The CAG audits the receipts and expenditures of bodies and authorities substantially financed from Union or State revenues, government companies, and other corporations and bodies when required by related laws.
4. **Advisory Role:** Under Article 150, the CAG advises the President regarding the form in which the accounts of the Union and the States should be maintained.
5. **Reporting:** The CAG compiles comprehensive audit reports relating to the accounts of the Union and submits them to the President, who, in turn, causes them to be laid before both Houses of Parliament (Article 151). Similarly, reports concerning state accounts are submitted to the Governor to be laid before the State Legislature. These reports form the basis for scrutiny by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of the Parliament or State Legislature, completing the cycle of financial accountability. Through meticulous auditing, the CAG brings to light financial irregularities, inefficiencies, and instances of unauthorized expenditure, playing a critical role in upholding democratic financial governance.

**b) Procedure for Amendment in the Indian Constitution**
The framers of the Indian Constitution sought to balance stability with flexibility, creating a document that could adapt to changing times without being too easily altered by transient political majorities. The procedure for amending the Constitution is laid down in Article 368 (Part XX). The Constitution provides for three different ways of amending its provisions, although only two are formally considered under Article 368:
1. **Simple Majority in Parliament:** Certain provisions can be amended by a simple majority of the two Houses of Parliament (i.e., a majority of members present and voting). This process is similar to the passage of an ordinary law. Examples include the admission or establishment of new states, alteration of state boundaries, and abolition or creation of legislative councils in states. These amendments are notably not deemed to be amendments under Article 368.
2. **Special Majority in Parliament (Article 368):** Most constitutional provisions require a special majority for amendment. A bill must be passed in each House of Parliament by a majority of the total membership of that House AND by a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles fall into this category.
3. **Special Majority plus Ratification by States (Article 368):** For amendments that affect the federal structure of the Constitution, a more stringent process is required. After being passed by a special majority in both Houses of Parliament, the amendment bill must also be ratified by the legislatures of not less than one-half of the States by a simple majority. Provisions requiring this include the election of the President, the extent of the executive power of the Union and States, the distribution of legislative powers (Seventh Schedule), and representation of states in Parliament. 
An amendment bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament (not in state legislatures) by a minister or a private member. It does not require the prior permission of the President. Once passed according to the required procedure, the President must give their assent to the bill; they cannot withhold assent or return the bill for reconsideration.
