# Constitution of India (June 2022) Answer Key - Part 2

## PART B (14-mark questions expanded)

### 11. a) Define Constitution of India with comparison with other countries. b) Discuss the term Union and its Territory.
**a) Definition and Comparison of the Indian Constitution:**
The Constitution of India is the supreme law of the land, providing a comprehensive framework that defines the fundamental political principles, establishes the structure, procedures, powers, and duties of government institutions, and sets out fundamental rights, directive principles, and the duties of citizens. It was adopted on November 26, 1949, and came into effect on January 26, 1950. It acts as a living document that guides the governance of the world's largest democracy.
When compared to other constitutions, the Indian Constitution is distinct in several ways. Firstly, it is the lengthiest written constitution globally. Unlike the American Constitution, which is remarkably brief with only 7 original articles focusing primarily on the federal structure, the Indian Constitution goes into granular detail regarding administration, both at the federal and state levels. 
Furthermore, the Indian Constitution borrows heavily from various global sources but adapts them to the Indian context. From the UK, it adopted the parliamentary system of government, the rule of law, legislative procedures, single citizenship, and the cabinet system. However, unlike the UK, where parliament is supreme and the constitution is unwritten and highly flexible, India has a written constitution with a supreme judiciary capable of judicial review.
From the USA, India borrowed the concept of Fundamental Rights, the independence of the judiciary, and judicial review. Yet, the Indian judiciary is an integrated system, unlike the dual court system in the US. India's federal structure with a strong unitary bias is akin to the Canadian model, rather than the true federalism of the USA. The Directive Principles of State Policy were inspired by the Irish Constitution, a feature not found in many Western democratic constitutions. The amendment process is a blend of the rigidity of the American constitution and the flexibility of the British system. Thus, while it is a mosaic of borrowed features, the Indian Constitution is uniquely tailored to the diverse and complex socio-political reality of India.

**b) The Union and its Territory:**
Part I of the Indian Constitution (Articles 1 to 4) deals with the Union and its Territory. Article 1 declares that "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." The term 'Union' implies that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among independent states (like the USA) and that no state has the right to secede from the federation. The country is one integral whole and is divided into states only for the convenience of administration.
The territory of India comprises:
1. The territories of the States.
2. The Union Territories specified in the First Schedule.
3. Such other territories as may be acquired.
Article 2 grants the Parliament the power to admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. This relates to territories that are currently not part of the Indian Union.
Article 3 is particularly significant as it empowers Parliament to form a new state by separation of territory from any state, or by uniting two or more states. Parliament can also increase or diminish the area of any state, and alter the boundaries or name of any state. This shows that the states in India do not have territorial integrity; the map of India can be redrawn by the Parliament. However, a bill for this purpose can be introduced only on the prior recommendation of the President, and the President must refer the bill to the state legislature concerned for expressing its views, although these views are not binding on Parliament.
Article 4 states that laws made for admission or establishment of new states (under Article 2) and formation of new states (under Article 3) are not to be considered as amendments of the Constitution under Article 368. They can be passed by a simple majority.

### 12. a) Explain the term citizenship and its types. b) How Indian Citizenship can be acquired?
**a) Citizenship and its Types:**
Citizenship refers to the legal status of an individual as a recognized member of a sovereign state or a nation. It denotes a relationship between the individual and the state, wherein the individual owes allegiance to the state, and in turn, the state is obligated to provide protection and guarantee certain fundamental rights to the individual. Citizens enjoy full civil and political rights, such as the right to vote, the right to hold public office, and the right to freedom of speech, which are often denied to non-citizens or aliens living within the state's territory.
Broadly, there are two primary principles or types of citizenship recognized globally:
1. Jus Soli (Right of the Soil): Under this principle, citizenship is determined by the place of birth. Anyone born within the territory of the state automatically acquires citizenship, regardless of the nationality of their parents. The United States is a prominent example of a country that heavily relies on jus soli.
2. Jus Sanguinis (Right of Blood): This principle determines citizenship based on descent or ancestry. A child acquires the citizenship of their parents, irrespective of where they are born.
The Indian Constitution, in Part II (Articles 5 to 11), deals with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution on January 26, 1950. However, the comprehensive law governing citizenship in India is the Citizenship Act, 1955, which has been amended several times. India primarily follows a mixed model, incorporating elements of both jus soli and jus sanguinis, though recent amendments have leaned more towards jus sanguinis to prevent illegal immigration. Notably, India provides for 'single citizenship' for the entire country, unlike countries with federal systems like the USA, where citizens have dual citizenship (national and state).

**b) Acquisition of Indian Citizenship:**
The Citizenship Act, 1955, prescribes five ways to acquire Indian citizenship:
1. By Birth (Jus Soli): A person born in India is considered an Indian citizen. The rules have evolved over time. Currently, a person born in India on or after December 3, 2004, is considered a citizen by birth only if both parents are citizens of India, or one parent is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. By Descent (Jus Sanguinis): A person born outside India can become a citizen by descent. Currently, for someone born outside India on or after December 3, 2004, they acquire citizenship by descent only if their birth is registered at an Indian consulate within one year of its occurrence.
3. By Registration: An individual can apply to the government to be registered as an Indian citizen. This category includes persons of Indian origin who have been resident in India for seven years before applying, persons of Indian origin resident in any country outside undivided India, persons married to an Indian citizen who are ordinarily resident in India for seven years, and minor children of Indian citizens.
4. By Naturalization: A foreigner can acquire Indian citizenship through naturalization. The conditions include residing in India or serving the Government of India for a continuous period of 12 months before the application, and additionally, for an aggregate period of 11 years in the preceding 14 years. They must also be of good character and possess an adequate knowledge of an Indian language specified in the Eighth Schedule.
5. By Incorporation of Territory: If any new territory becomes a part of India, the Government of India specifies the persons of that territory who shall be the citizens of India. For example, when Pondicherry became part of India, its residents were granted Indian citizenship.

### 13. a) Explain the term fundamental rights and its classification. b) Explain right against exploitation and right to constitutional remedies.
**a) Fundamental Rights and their Classification:**
Fundamental Rights are those essential rights which are enshrined in Part III (Articles 12 to 35) of the Indian Constitution. They are termed 'fundamental' because they are guaranteed and protected by the Constitution, which is the fundamental law of the land, and they are essential for the all-round development (material, intellectual, moral, and spiritual) of individuals. These rights protect the liberties and freedoms of citizens against any invasion by the state, thereby establishing a "government of laws and not of men." They are justiciable in nature, meaning that in case of a violation, a person can approach the courts for their enforcement.
The Constitution originally provided for seven fundamental rights, but the Right to Property was deleted from the list of Fundamental Rights by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978. Now, there are six categories of Fundamental Rights:
1. Right to Equality (Articles 14-18): Ensures equality before the law, prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, provides equality of opportunity in public employment, abolishes untouchability, and abolishes titles.
2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22): Guarantees six essential freedoms including speech and expression, peaceful assembly, forming associations, free movement, residence, and profession. It also includes protection in respect of conviction for offenses, protection of life and personal liberty, and protection against arrest and detention.
3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24): Prohibits human trafficking, forced labor, and the employment of children in hazardous jobs.
4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28): Guarantees the freedom of conscience, the right to profess, practice, and propagate religion, freedom to manage religious affairs, and freedom from attending religious instructions in certain educational institutions.
5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30): Protects the interests of minorities, allowing them to conserve their distinct language, script, or culture, and establishes their right to administer educational institutions.
6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32): Empowers citizens to move the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of these fundamental rights.

**b) Right against Exploitation and Right to Constitutional Remedies:**
*Right against Exploitation:* 
This right, covered under Articles 23 and 24, aims to protect the dignity of the individual and prevent exploitation of the vulnerable sections of society. 
Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, begar (forced labor without payment), and other similar forms of forced labor. Any contravention of this provision is an offense punishable in accordance with the law. This right protects individuals not only against state actions but also against actions by private individuals.
Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below the age of 14 years in any factory, mine, or other hazardous activities (like construction work or railways). This is a crucial right aimed at protecting the health and childhood of young ones and ensuring they have the opportunity for education.

*Right to Constitutional Remedies:*
Enshrined in Article 32, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar called this article "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it." The mere declaration of fundamental rights is meaningless unless there is an effective machinery for their enforcement. Article 32 provides this machinery. 
It guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Under this article, the Supreme Court has the power to issue directions, orders, or writs—including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari—whichever may be appropriate for the enforcement of the rights. This right itself is a fundamental right, making the Supreme Court the defender and guarantor of all fundamental rights. The High Courts also possess similar writ jurisdiction under Article 226, though Article 32 specifically secures the right to approach the apex court.

### 14. a) What do you mean by Directive principles of state policy? b) What are the fundamental duties of an Indian citizen?
**a) Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP):**
Contained in Part IV (Articles 36 to 51) of the Indian Constitution, the Directive Principles of State Policy are guidelines or instructions given to the central and state governments. The framers borrowed this concept from the Irish Constitution. The fundamental idea behind DPSPs is to create a "welfare state" that promotes the social and economic well-being of the people, moving beyond a mere "police state" that only maintains law and order. 
Unlike Fundamental Rights, which are negative obligations on the state (restricting the state from acting in certain ways) and are justiciable, DPSPs are positive obligations. They are non-justiciable; this means that if the government fails to implement them, a citizen cannot take the government to court to compel their enforcement. However, Article 37 explicitly states that these principles are "nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws."
DPSPs embody the concept of socio-economic democracy. They cover a wide range of subjects and can be broadly classified into three categories:
1. Socialistic Principles: These aim at providing social and economic justice. Examples include securing adequate means of livelihood, equal pay for equal work, equitable distribution of material resources, and the right to work, education, and public assistance.
2. Gandhian Principles: Based on Mahatma Gandhi's ideology, these include organizing village panchayats, promoting cottage industries, prohibiting intoxicating drinks and drugs, and organizing agriculture and animal husbandry on modern lines, including preventing the slaughter of cows.
3. Liberal-Intellectual Principles: These include securing a uniform civil code for all citizens, providing free and compulsory education for children (now partially a fundamental right), separating the judiciary from the executive, and promoting international peace and security.
While not legally enforceable, DPSPs act as a moral and political compass for the government, and their implementation is judged by the electorate during elections.

**b) Fundamental Duties of an Indian Citizen:**
The Fundamental Duties were not part of the original Constitution drafted in 1950. They were added to Part IV-A (Article 51A) by the 42nd Amendment Act in 1976, during the Emergency, upon the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Later, an 11th duty was added by the 86th Amendment Act in 2002. 
The rationale behind including Fundamental Duties was to remind citizens that while enjoying their fundamental rights, they must also be conscious of their duties towards the nation, society, and fellow citizens. Like DPSPs, Fundamental Duties are non-justiciable; there is no direct legal enforcement for their violation. However, the courts can consider them while interpreting laws.
The 11 Fundamental Duties expected of every citizen of India are:
1. To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem.
2. To cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
3. To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India.
4. To defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
5. To promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
6. To value and preserve the rich heritage of the country's composite culture.
7. To protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
8. To develop the scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
9. To safeguard public property and to abjure violence.
10. To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavor and achievement.
11. To provide opportunities for education to one's child or ward between the age of six and fourteen years. (Added in 2002).

### 15. a) Explain how Union Executive is elected and formed. b) What are the different functions of Parliament?
**a) Formation and Election of the Union Executive:**
The Union Executive, detailed in Part V of the Constitution, consists of the President, the Vice-President, the Prime Minister, the Council of Ministers, and the Attorney General of India. 
The President is the nominal head of the state. The President is not directly elected by the people but through an Electoral College consisting of the elected members of both Houses of Parliament (Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha) and the elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States (including the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Puducherry). The election is held in accordance with the system of proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, and voting is by secret ballot. The President holds office for a term of five years.
The Vice-President is elected by an Electoral College consisting of the members of both Houses of Parliament (both elected and nominated members). Unlike the President's election, state legislatures do not participate in the Vice-President's election. The Vice-President acts as the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
The real executive power vests in the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers. Following a general election for the Lok Sabha, the President invites the leader of the political party, or an alliance of parties, which commands a majority in the Lok Sabha, to form the government. The leader is appointed as the Prime Minister. 
The other ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister. The Council of Ministers consists of three categories of ministers: Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, and Deputy Ministers. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the House of the People (Lok Sabha). This means that they must retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha; if a vote of no-confidence is passed, the entire ministry must resign.

**b) Functions of Parliament:**
The Parliament is the supreme legislative body of India, consisting of the President, the Rajya Sabha (Council of States), and the Lok Sabha (House of the People). Its functions are multifaceted and crucial for the democratic process.
1. Legislative Functions: The primary function of Parliament is to make laws for the country. It has exclusive power to legislate on subjects mentioned in the Union List and concurrent power to legislate on subjects in the Concurrent List. In certain exceptional circumstances (like emergencies or state request), it can also legislate on State List subjects. A bill must be passed by both Houses and receive the President's assent to become a law.
2. Executive Control: Parliament exercises control over the executive branch. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Parliament exercises this control through various mechanisms such as question hour, zero hour, calling attention motions, adjournment motions, and most importantly, the vote of no-confidence. Members can ask questions to hold ministers accountable for their departments' functioning.
3. Financial Functions: Parliament holds the purse strings of the nation. No tax can be levied or collected, and no expenditure can be incurred by the government except under the authority of an Act of Parliament. The government presents the annual budget (Annual Financial Statement) to Parliament for its approval.
4. Constituent Functions: Parliament is vested with the power to amend the Constitution. Certain parts can be amended by a simple majority, while others require a special majority (two-thirds of members present and voting and a majority of total membership).
5. Judicial Functions: Parliament has the power to impeach the President for the violation of the Constitution. It can also remove judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, the Chief Election Commissioner, and the Comptroller and Auditor General through a process of impeachment.
6. Electoral Functions: Parliament participates in the election of the President and the Vice-President. The Lok Sabha elects its Speaker and Deputy Speaker, and the Rajya Sabha elects its Deputy Chairman.

### 16. a) Differentiate Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha with five points. b) How can a citizen be qualified and disqualified as an MP?
**a) Differences between Rajya Sabha and Lok Sabha:**
1. Nature of the House: The Lok Sabha (House of the People) is the lower house and represents the people of India as a whole. The Rajya Sabha (Council of States) is the upper house and represents the states and union territories of the Indian federation.
2. Election and Tenure: Members of the Lok Sabha are directly elected by the citizens of India through universal adult suffrage from territorial constituencies. The Lok Sabha has a normal term of five years and can be dissolved earlier by the President. In contrast, members of the Rajya Sabha are indirectly elected by the elected members of state legislative assemblies. The Rajya Sabha is a permanent body and cannot be dissolved; however, one-third of its members retire every two years, giving each member a term of six years.
3. Size and Composition: The maximum strength of the Lok Sabha is fixed at 552, though currently, it is lower. The maximum strength of the Rajya Sabha is 250, out of which 238 are representatives of the states and union territories, and 12 are nominated by the President for their expertise in fields like literature, science, art, and social service.
4. Financial Powers: The Lok Sabha holds superior financial powers. A Money Bill can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha, not in the Rajya Sabha. The Rajya Sabha can only delay a Money Bill for a maximum of 14 days and make recommendations, which the Lok Sabha is free to accept or reject. The Lok Sabha also holds the exclusive power to vote on demands for grants.
5. Executive Accountability: The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible only to the Lok Sabha. A motion of no-confidence can only be introduced and passed in the Lok Sabha. If the Lok Sabha passes a no-confidence motion, the government must resign. The Rajya Sabha can criticize the government but cannot bring it down.

**b) Qualifications and Disqualifications of an MP:**
*Qualifications:*
To be elected as a Member of Parliament (MP), a citizen must fulfill certain criteria specified in the Constitution and the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
1. The person must be a citizen of India.
2. They must make and subscribe to an oath or affirmation before a person authorized by the Election Commission.
3. For the Lok Sabha, the candidate must be not less than 25 years of age. For the Rajya Sabha, the candidate must be not less than 30 years of age.
4. They must possess other qualifications as prescribed by Parliament. Under the RPA, 1951, a person must be registered as an elector for a parliamentary constituency. To contest a seat reserved for SC/STs, the candidate must belong to a Scheduled Caste or Scheduled Tribe in any state.

*Disqualifications:*
The Constitution and the RPA, 1951, list several grounds for disqualification of an MP.
1. If the person holds any office of profit under the Union or state government (except those exempted by Parliament).
2. If the person is of unsound mind and stands so declared by a competent court.
3. If the person is an undischarged insolvent.
4. If the person is not a citizen of India, or has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign state, or is under any acknowledgment of allegiance to a foreign state.
5. Disqualification on grounds of Defection (Tenth Schedule): An MP is disqualified if they voluntarily give up membership of their political party or vote against the party whip.
6. Under the RPA, 1951, disqualifications include conviction for certain election offenses or corrupt practices, conviction for any offense resulting in imprisonment for two or more years, failure to lodge an account of election expenses within the time, and having interest in government contracts or being a director in a government corporation.

### 17. a) Differentiate State Government and Union Territory. b) Explain State Legislative Assembly in detail.
**a) State Government vs. Union Territory:**
The Indian Republic is composed of States and Union Territories (UTs). Their governance structures differ significantly based on the degree of autonomy they possess.
1. Constitutional Status and Autonomy: States hold a federal relationship with the Central Government. They share sovereign power with the Centre as per the distribution of powers in the Seventh Schedule. They possess a high degree of autonomy in matters listed in the State List. In contrast, Union Territories have a unitary relationship with the Centre. They are administered directly by the Central Government and do not share power in the same federal sense.
2. Executive Head: The executive head of a State is the Governor, who is the constitutional head appointed by the President, but acts on the advice of the elected Chief Minister. The executive head of a UT is an administrator appointed by the President. They are known by various designations such as Lieutenant Governor (e.g., Delhi, Puducherry, J&K), Chief Commissioner, or Administrator. The administrator acts as an agent of the President, not as a constitutional head.
3. Legislature and Governance: Every state possesses its own legislature (either unicameral or bicameral) and an elected Council of Ministers headed by a Chief Minister. The state legislature has the power to make laws on subjects in the State List. Most UTs do not have their own legislature. The Parliament has the power to make laws on any subject for the UTs. However, exceptions exist: Puducherry, Delhi, and Jammu & Kashmir have been provided with legislative assemblies and a Council of Ministers, but their powers are restricted compared to full states (e.g., Delhi lacks control over land, police, and public order).
4. Administrative Machinery: The administrative setup in states is uniform, adhering to the constitutional provisions detailed in Part VI. The administrative machinery in UTs varies from one to another, determined by the President as per Part VIII of the Constitution.

**b) State Legislative Assembly in Detail:**
The State Legislative Assembly, or Vidhan Sabha, is the popular and lower house of the state legislature, analogous to the Lok Sabha at the national level. 
*Composition:* It consists of representatives directly elected by the people based on universal adult franchise from territorial constituencies within the state. Its maximum strength is fixed at 500, and minimum strength at 60. However, exceptions have been made for smaller states like Sikkim, Goa, and Mizoram, which have fewer than 60 members. 
*Reservation:* There is a constitutional provision for the reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Assembly based on their population ratios. Previously, the Governor could nominate one member from the Anglo-Indian community if they felt the community was inadequately represented, but this provision was discontinued by the 104th Amendment Act in 2019.
*Term:* The normal term of the Legislative Assembly is five years from the date of its first meeting. However, the Governor has the power to dissolve it at any time, usually on the advice of the Chief Minister or during President's Rule. During a national emergency, Parliament can extend the term of the Assembly by one year at a time, but not beyond six months after the emergency ends.
*Presiding Officers:* The Assembly elects its own Speaker and Deputy Speaker from among its members. The Speaker presides over the meetings, maintains order, interprets rules, and casts a deciding vote in case of a tie. 
*Powers and Functions:* The Assembly wields substantial legislative and financial powers. It can make laws on subjects in the State List and Concurrent List. Financially, it has paramount control over the state's finances; no tax can be levied and no money can be spent without its approval. It passes the annual state budget and money bills can only be introduced here. The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly, which exercises control over the executive through questions, debates, and no-confidence motions. It also participates in the election of the President of India and members of the Rajya Sabha from that state.

### 18. a) Discuss about Jurisdiction of High court. b) Explain State Legislative Council in detail
**a) Jurisdiction of the High Court:**
The High Court is the highest judicial body within a state's jurisdiction. Under the Indian Constitution, the jurisdiction of a High Court is wide-ranging, encompassing original, appellate, and writ powers.
1. Original Jurisdiction: This refers to the power of the High Court to hear cases directly in the first instance, rather than on appeal. Its original jurisdiction includes matters of admiralty, probate, matrimonial, and contempt of court cases. Crucially, disputes relating to the election of Members of Parliament and State Legislatures fall under its original jurisdiction. It also has original jurisdiction regarding revenue matters or acts ordered in revenue collection.
2. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226): This is arguably the most potent power of the High Court. It is empowered to issue prerogative writs—habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari. Notably, the writ jurisdiction of the High Court is wider than that of the Supreme Court (Article 32). While the Supreme Court can issue writs solely for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights, the High Court can issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights as well as "for any other purpose," implying ordinary legal rights.
3. Appellate Jurisdiction: The High Court acts as the primary court of appeal for both civil and criminal cases arising from the subordinate courts within the state. In civil matters, it hears first appeals against decisions of district courts on points of law and fact, and second appeals on points of law. In criminal matters, it hears appeals against judgments of sessions courts, especially where the sentence exceeds seven years of imprisonment or involves the death penalty (which requires High Court confirmation before execution).
4. Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227): The High Court has the power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals (except military tribunals) functioning within its territorial jurisdiction. It can call for returns from them, make general rules regarding their practice and proceedings, and prescribe forms for maintaining books and accounts.
5. Court of Record: Like the Supreme Court, the High Court is a court of record. Its judgments are recorded for perpetual memory and act as binding precedents for subordinate courts. It has the inherent power to punish for its own contempt.

**b) State Legislative Council in Detail:**
The State Legislative Council, or Vidhan Parishad, is the upper house in states that possess a bicameral legislature. Only a few states in India (currently six: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh) have a Legislative Council.
*Creation and Abolition:* Article 169 of the Constitution empowers the Parliament to create or abolish a Legislative Council in a state. This requires the Legislative Assembly of that state to pass a resolution by a special majority (majority of total membership and a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting). 
*Composition:* The size of the Legislative Council is much smaller than the Assembly. Its total membership cannot exceed one-third of the total membership of the state Legislative Assembly, but it cannot be less than 40. 
Unlike the Assembly, members of the Council are indirectly elected and nominated, representing various specific constituencies to ensure diverse expertise. 
- 1/3rd of the members are elected by local bodies like municipalities, district boards, etc.
- 1/12th are elected by graduates of three years' standing residing in the state.
- 1/12th are elected by teachers of three years' standing in educational institutions not lower than secondary schools.
- 1/3rd are elected by the members of the Legislative Assembly from amongst persons who are not members of the Assembly.
- The remaining 1/6th are nominated by the Governor from individuals possessing special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art, cooperative movement, and social service.
*Term:* Like the Rajya Sabha, the Legislative Council is a continuing chamber and is not subject to dissolution. The term of a member is six years, with one-third of the members retiring every two years.
*Presiding Officers:* The Council elects its Chairman and Deputy Chairman from among its members to preside over its meetings.
*Powers:* The Legislative Council is a comparatively weak house. In legislative matters, it can only delay ordinary bills for a maximum period of four months (three months in the first instance and one month if the Assembly passes it again). It cannot reject or amend a bill contrary to the Assembly's wishes. In financial matters, it has virtually no power; money bills cannot be introduced in the Council, and it can only delay a money bill for 14 days. The executive is responsible only to the Assembly, not the Council. Thus, it functions primarily as an advisory or revising chamber.

### 19. a) How is Central and State Government related on economic basis? b) Explain how the constitution handles an emergency situation in the country.
**a) Central and State Government Economic Relations:**
The financial relations between the Centre and the States are detailed in Part XII of the Constitution. Given the federal structure, financial independence is necessary for both tiers of government to function effectively. However, the distribution is heavily skewed in favor of the Centre.
1. Allocation of Taxing Powers: The Constitution clearly demarcates the taxing powers. The Centre has the exclusive power to levy taxes on subjects in the Union List (e.g., income tax, corporate tax, customs duties). The States have the power to levy taxes on subjects in the State List (e.g., land revenue, state excise, tolls, taxes on agricultural income). The Concurrent List does not contain major tax subjects. 
2. Distribution of Tax Revenues: Recognizing that states' revenue sources might be insufficient for their massive developmental responsibilities, the Constitution provides for the sharing of central taxes. 
   - Some taxes are levied by the Centre but collected and appropriated by the States (e.g., stamp duties on bills of exchange).
   - Some taxes are levied and collected by the Centre but assigned to the States (e.g., taxes on sale of goods in interstate trade).
   - The major chunk of central taxes (like income tax and corporate tax) are levied and collected by the Centre but distributed between the Centre and the States. The share of states is determined on the recommendation of the Finance Commission.
3. Grants-in-Aid: Apart from tax sharing, the Centre provides grants-in-aid to states to assist them financially.
   - Statutory Grants (Article 275): Given to states in need of financial assistance, as recommended by the Finance Commission.
   - Discretionary Grants (Article 282): Both Centre and States can make public grants for purposes not strictly within their legislative competence. Historically, the Centre heavily utilized these grants (often guided by the erstwhile Planning Commission) to influence state policies.
4. Borrowing Powers: The Centre can borrow both domestically and internationally. States can only borrow domestically, and if a state has an outstanding loan to the Centre, it requires the Centre's consent to raise fresh loans, giving the Centre significant financial control.
5. Finance Commission: A constitutional body appointed by the President every five years to recommend the distribution of tax revenues between the Centre and States, and the principles governing grants-in-aid, ensuring a systematic approach to fiscal federalism.

**b) Emergency Provisions in the Constitution:**
Part XVIII of the Constitution contains the Emergency Provisions, designed to enable the Central government to deal effectively with extraordinary situations that threaten the security, stability, or financial integrity of the nation. During an emergency, the federal structure transforms into a unitary one without formal constitutional amendment. There are three types of emergencies:
1. National Emergency (Article 352): The President can declare a National Emergency if the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. 
   - The declaration must be approved by both Houses of Parliament by a special majority within one month.
   - Effects: The Centre gains the power to direct states on any executive matter. Parliament can legislate on state subjects. Financial relations can be modified. Crucially, fundamental rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended, and the President can suspend the right to move courts for enforcement of other fundamental rights (except Articles 20 and 21).
2. State Emergency / President's Rule (Article 356): Proclaimed when the President is satisfied (usually on a report from the Governor) that the government of a state cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution.
   - Must be approved by Parliament by a simple majority within two months.
   - Effects: The state government is dismissed, and the state legislature is suspended or dissolved. The President assumes the executive powers of the state, usually exercised through the Governor. Parliament assumes the legislative powers of the state. Fundamental rights are not affected.
3. Financial Emergency (Article 360): The President can proclaim a Financial Emergency if the financial stability or credit of India or any part of it is threatened.
   - Must be approved by Parliament within two months by a simple majority.
   - Effects: The Centre can direct states to observe specific financial proprieties. It allows for the reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving the State or the Union, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. All money bills passed by state legislatures can be reserved for the President's consideration.

### 20. a) Which are the functions of Comptroller and Auditor General of India? b) Explain the role of Public Service Commission.
**a) Functions of the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India:**
The CAG, established under Article 148, is an independent constitutional authority who serves as the supreme audit institution of India. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar considered the CAG to be the most important officer under the Constitution, tasked with guarding the public purse and ensuring that executive expenditure complies with parliamentary authorization.
The CAG's functions, derived from the Constitution and the CAG (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971, include:
1. Auditing Expenditure: The primary function is to audit all expenditures from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and the Consolidated Fund of Union Territories having legislative assemblies. The CAG ensures that funds were legally available, disbursed by a competent authority, and spent for the purpose intended.
2. Auditing Contingency Funds and Public Accounts: The CAG audits all expenditures from the Contingency Fund of India and the Public Account of India, as well as the corresponding funds at the state level.
3. Auditing Government Companies and Corporations: The CAG audits the receipts and expenditures of all bodies and authorities substantially financed from Union or State revenues, government companies, and other corporations as required by related laws.
4. Auditing Receipts: The CAG audits the receipts of the Union and State governments to ensure that rules and procedures are in place to ensure an effective check on the assessment, collection, and proper allocation of revenue.
5. Advisory Role: The CAG advises the President regarding the prescription of the form in which the accounts of the Centre and the States shall be kept (Article 150).
6. Reporting: The CAG submits audit reports relating to the accounts of the Union to the President, who lays them before both Houses of Parliament. Similarly, audit reports for the States are submitted to the respective Governors, who place them before the state legislatures. These reports are thoroughly examined by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of the Parliament/State Legislature, establishing executive accountability. 

**b) Role of the Public Service Commission:**
Articles 315 to 323 in Part XIV of the Constitution provide for a Public Service Commission for the Union (UPSC) and for each State (SPSC), along with the provision for Joint State Public Service Commissions (JSPSC). These commissions act as the central recruiting agencies and watchdogs of the merit system in the country.
Their core roles and functions include:
1. Recruitment/Examinations: Their most visible and crucial role is to conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the Union (like IAS, IPS, Central Services) and the respective States (State Civil Services, Police Services). This ensures that recruitment is based purely on merit, conducted impartially, free from political patronage.
2. Advisory Functions on Personnel Management: The government is constitutionally mandated to consult the UPSC/SPSC on all matters relating to methods of recruitment to civil services and civil posts. 
3. Advice on Promotions and Transfers: They advise the government on the principles to be followed in making appointments, promotions, and transfers from one service to another, and assess the suitability of candidates for such promotions and transfers.
4. Disciplinary Matters: They are consulted on all disciplinary matters affecting a person serving under the Government in a civil capacity, including memorials or petitions relating to such matters. The advice provides a safeguard to civil servants against arbitrary punitive actions.
5. Claims and Pensions: They advise on any claim for the award of a pension in respect of injuries sustained while serving the government in a civil capacity, or any claim for reimbursement of legal expenses incurred by a civil servant in defending legal proceedings instituted against them for acts done in the execution of their duty.
6. Submitting Annual Reports: The UPSC presents an annual report on its work to the President (and SPSCs to the Governor). This report, along with a memorandum explaining cases where the Commission's advice was not accepted and the reasons for such non-acceptance, is laid before the Parliament/State Legislature. This mechanism ensures transparency and acts as a check against the government arbitrarily ignoring the Commission's expert advice.
Through these functions, the Public Service Commissions play a pivotal role in maintaining the integrity, efficiency, and political neutrality of the civil services in India.
