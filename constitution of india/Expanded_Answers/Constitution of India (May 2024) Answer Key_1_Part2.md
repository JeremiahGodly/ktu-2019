### 11. a) Discuss the significance of Preamble to the Constitution of India and its role in reflecting the ideals of the Constitution. (9 marks) b) Explain the historical background that led to the framing of Constitution of India. (5 marks)

**a) Significance of the Preamble and its Role in Reflecting Constitutional Ideals**
The Preamble to the Constitution of India serves as a brief introductory statement that sets out the guiding purpose, principles, and philosophy of the Indian Constitution. It is often referred to as the "soul of the Constitution," the "key to the Constitution," and the "identity card of the Constitution." The significance of the Preamble lies in the fact that it embodies the fundamental values and the philosophy on which the Constitution is based, and the aims and objectives which the founding fathers enjoined the polity to strive to achieve. 

The Preamble indicates the source from which the document derives its authority, namely, the people of India ("We, the people of India"). This emphasizes the democratic principle that the ultimate power rests with the citizens. It declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic nation. 'Sovereign' implies India is an independent nation, not subject to any external control. 'Socialist' (added by the 42nd Amendment) reflects the goal of ending poverty, ignorance, disease, and inequality of opportunity. 'Secular' (also added by the 42nd Amendment) means the state has no official religion and treats all religions equally. 'Democratic' indicates that the government draws its authority from the people through elections, and 'Republic' signifies that the head of the state is elected, not a hereditary monarch.

Furthermore, the Preamble secures to all its citizens Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and of opportunity), and promotes Fraternity (assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation). In legal interpretation, the Supreme Court in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) established that the Preamble is a part of the Constitution and can be used to interpret ambiguous provisions of the Constitution. It acts as a guiding light for the judiciary and the legislature, ensuring that laws and judgments align with the core philosophy of the Indian state.

**b) Historical Background of the Framing of the Constitution**
The framing of the Indian Constitution was not an overnight event but the culmination of a long historical process, heavily influenced by the British colonial administration and the Indian independence movement. The conceptual roots can be traced back to various British acts, starting from the Regulating Act of 1973, which initiated the centralization of administration in India, to the Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935. The Government of India Act 1935, in particular, provided a significant blueprint, establishing a federal structure, provincial autonomy, and a bicameral legislature, many features of which were later adapted into the final Constitution.

The formal demand for a Constituent Assembly to frame the Constitution was first raised by M.N. Roy in 1934 and later officially adopted by the Indian National Congress in 1935. The British government accepted this demand in principle through the 'August Offer' of 1940. However, it was the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 that laid down a workable scheme for the formation of the Constituent Assembly. The Assembly met for the first time on December 9, 1946. Despite the immense challenges posed by partition, communal violence, and the integration of princely states, the Assembly, under the chairmanship of Dr. Rajendra Prasad and with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar heading the Drafting Committee, worked tirelessly for 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days. The Objectives Resolution moved by Jawaharlal Nehru in 1946 provided the underlying philosophy. Drawing inspiration from various global constitutions while remaining rooted in the Indian context, the Assembly adopted the final draft on November 26, 1949, which came into full effect on January 26, 1950.

### 12. a) Discuss different ways in which the citizenship is acquired and lost in India, according to Constitutional provisions. (9 marks) b) Why Indian Constitution is sometimes called a “borrowed” constitution. (5 marks)

**a) Acquisition and Loss of Citizenship in India**
Citizenship in India is a complex legal subject, primarily governed by Part II of the Constitution (Articles 5 to 11) and elaborated by the Citizenship Act of 1955. The Constitution itself only provided for citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution (January 26, 1950) and empowered Parliament under Article 11 to enact laws regarding the acquisition and termination of citizenship subsequently. Accordingly, Parliament passed the Citizenship Act, 1955.

Under the Citizenship Act, citizenship can be acquired in five principal ways:
1. **By Birth (Jus Soli):** Every person born in India on or after January 26, 1950, is generally a citizen by birth. However, due to amendments (like the 1986 and 2003 amendments), the criteria have become stricter, now requiring at least one parent to be an Indian citizen and the other not being an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. **By Descent (Jus Sanguinis):** A person born outside India can acquire citizenship by descent if either of their parents is an Indian citizen at the time of their birth. Strict registration rules at an Indian consulate apply for births occurring after December 1992.
3. **By Registration:** Persons of Indian origin who have been resident in India for a specified period (usually 7 years), persons married to Indian citizens, and minor children of Indian citizens can apply for citizenship through registration.
4. **By Naturalization:** A foreigner can acquire Indian citizenship through naturalization if they have resided in India for a prolonged period (generally 11 of the last 14 years, plus the preceding 12 months), possess good character, and have an adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule.
5. **By Incorporation of Territory:** If any new territory becomes part of India, the Government of India can specify the persons from that territory who shall become citizens of India.

Conversely, the Act provides three ways for the loss of citizenship:
1. **Renunciation:** Any citizen of India of full age and capacity can voluntarily renounce their Indian citizenship by making a declaration. When a male person ceases to be a citizen, his minor children also lose their citizenship.
2. **Termination:** If a citizen of India voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, their Indian citizenship automatically terminates. This is because India does not permit dual citizenship.
3. **Deprivation:** This is a compulsory termination by the Central Government. It applies mainly to citizens who acquired citizenship by naturalization or registration. Grounds for deprivation include obtaining citizenship by fraud, showing disloyalty to the Constitution, unlawfully communicating with an enemy during war, or being imprisoned in any country for two years within five years of naturalization.

**b) The "Borrowed" Constitution**
The Indian Constitution is sometimes referred to as a "borrowed" constitution or a "bag of borrowings" because its framers extensively studied and incorporated provisions from the constitutions of various other countries. The founders were not aiming to invent entirely new political principles but to adopt the best features of existing constitutions and adapt them to suit the unique historical, cultural, and socio-economic conditions of India.

For instance, the Parliamentary system of government, the rule of law, legislative procedures, and the concept of single citizenship were heavily inspired by the British Constitution. The concept of Fundamental Rights, the independence of the judiciary, judicial review, and the impeachment of the President were borrowed from the Constitution of the United States. The Directive Principles of State Policy, which provide socio-economic guidelines to the state, were taken from the Irish Constitution. The idea of a federation with a strong centre and the vesting of residuary powers in the centre were derived from the Canadian Constitution. Furthermore, the provision for the concurrent list and joint sitting of Parliament came from Australia, the suspension of fundamental rights during an emergency from the Weimar Constitution of Germany, and the procedure for constitutional amendment from South Africa. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar proudly stated that there is nothing to be ashamed of in borrowing, as nobody holds any patent rights in the fundamental ideas of a Constitution. The Indian Constitution is not a blind copy but a thoughtful amalgamation tailored to India's specific needs.

### 13. a) Explain the basic features of Fundamental Rights. (5 marks) b) Discuss different types of Fundamental rights outlined in the Constitution. (9 marks)

**a) Basic Features of Fundamental Rights**
Fundamental Rights are enshrined in Part III of the Indian Constitution, spanning from Articles 12 to 35. These rights are fundamental because they are essential for the holistic development—material, intellectual, moral, and spiritual—of individuals. They form the bedrock of democratic life in India, protecting citizens against the arbitrary exercise of power by the state. 

Several key features characterize these rights. Firstly, they are **justiciable**, meaning that if they are violated, an individual can approach the courts (Supreme Court under Article 32 or High Courts under Article 226) for their enforcement. Secondly, they are **not absolute but subject to reasonable restrictions**. The state can impose limitations on grounds of state security, public order, decency, morality, and friendly relations with foreign states. This balances individual liberty with social control. Thirdly, **some rights are available only to citizens** (e.g., Article 15, 16, 19, 29, 30), while **others are available to all persons**, including foreigners and legal persons like corporations (e.g., Article 14, 21). Fourthly, they are **not sacrosanct or permanent**. Parliament can curtail or repeal them through constitutional amendments, provided they do not alter the 'basic structure' of the Constitution (as established in the Kesavananda Bharati case). Finally, these rights can be **suspended during a National Emergency**, except for the rights guaranteed by Articles 20 (protection in respect of conviction for offences) and 21 (protection of life and personal liberty).

**b) Types of Fundamental Rights in the Constitution**
The Indian Constitution initially provided for seven Fundamental Rights, but the Right to Property (Article 31) was deleted from this list by the 44th Amendment Act in 1978 and made a legal right. Currently, there are six categories of Fundamental Rights:

1. **Right to Equality (Articles 14-18):** This encompasses equality before the law and equal protection of laws (Article 14). It prohibits the state from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (Article 15). It ensures equality of opportunity in matters of public employment (Article 16). Furthermore, it abolishes 'Untouchability' and forbids its practice in any form (Article 17) and abolishes titles, except military and academic ones (Article 18).
2. **Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22):** This is a cluster of vital liberties. Article 19 guarantees six basic freedoms to citizens: speech and expression, peaceful assembly, forming associations, free movement throughout India, residence in any part, and practicing any profession. Article 20 provides protection against arbitrary conviction. Article 21, perhaps the most expansive right, ensures that no person shall be deprived of their life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law. Article 21A guarantees the right to elementary education. Article 22 provides protection against arbitrary arrest and preventive detention.
3. **Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24):** This right aims to prevent human trafficking, forced labor (begar), and other similar forms of exploitation (Article 23). It also strictly prohibits the employment of children below the age of 14 years in factories, mines, or any other hazardous employment (Article 24), protecting vulnerable sections of society.
4. **Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28):** Securing the secular fabric of the nation, Article 25 guarantees the freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practice, and propagate religion. Article 26 gives every religious denomination the right to manage its own religious affairs. Article 27 ensures freedom from paying taxes for the promotion of any particular religion, and Article 28 provides freedom from attending religious instructions in certain educational institutions.
5. **Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30):** These rights protect the interests of minorities. Article 29 guarantees the right of any section of citizens residing in India to conserve their distinct language, script, or culture. Article 30 grants all minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
6. **Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32):** This right is the mechanism for enforcing all the other fundamental rights. It empowers a citizen to approach the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of these rights through the issuance of various writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto).

### 14. a) Discuss the different categories under which Directive Principles of State Policy can be categorized. (7 marks) b) What are the Fundamental duties of an Indian citizen? Explain. (7 marks)

**a) Categories of Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)**
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are enumerated in Part IV of the Constitution (Articles 36 to 51). While the Constitution itself does not formally classify the Directive Principles into distinct categories based on their underlying philosophy, political scientists and constitutional experts generally categorize them into three broad ideological groups for better understanding: Socialistic Principles, Gandhian Principles, and Liberal-Intellectual Principles.

1. **Socialistic Principles:** These principles reflect the ideology of socialism. They aim to establish a welfare state in India by minimizing inequalities in income, status, and opportunities, and by promoting social and economic justice. Key articles include:
   - **Article 38:** Directs the state to promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order permeated by justice—social, economic, and political.
   - **Article 39:** Mandates the state to direct its policy towards securing adequate means of livelihood, equitable distribution of material resources, prevention of concentration of wealth, equal pay for equal work for men and women, and protection of the health and strength of workers and children.
   - **Article 41, 42, & 43:** Direct the state to secure the right to work, education, and public assistance in cases of unemployment or old age (Art 41), provide for just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief (Art 42), and secure a living wage and a decent standard of life for all workers (Art 43).

2. **Gandhian Principles:** These principles are based on the socio-economic program reconstructed by Mahatma Gandhi during the national movement. They represent his vision for rural development and decentralization. Key articles include:
   - **Article 40:** Requires the state to organize village panchayats and endow them with necessary powers to function as units of self-government.
   - **Article 43:** Promotes cottage industries on an individual or co-operative basis in rural areas.
   - **Article 46:** Directs the state to promote the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and protect them from social injustice.
   - **Article 47 & 48:** Urge the state to prohibit the consumption of intoxicating drinks and drugs (Art 47) and to prohibit the slaughter of cows, calves, and other milch and draught cattle (Art 48).

3. **Liberal-Intellectual Principles:** These principles reflect the ideology of liberalism and modern, progressive thought. Key articles include:
   - **Article 44:** Directs the state to secure a Uniform Civil Code for all citizens throughout the territory of India.
   - **Article 45:** Originally mandated free and compulsory education for all children until age 14 (now modified after Right to Education).
   - **Article 48A & 49:** Require the state to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife (Art 48A) and to protect monuments and places of national or historical importance (Art 49).
   - **Article 50 & 51:** Direct the state to separate the judiciary from the executive in public services (Art 50) and to promote international peace and security, and maintain just and honorable relations between nations (Art 51).

**b) Fundamental Duties of an Indian Citizen**
Fundamental Duties are enshrined in Part IV-A (Article 51A) of the Constitution. They were added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act of 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Originally ten in number, an eleventh duty was added by the 86th Amendment in 2002. These duties are essentially moral and civic obligations that remind citizens that rights and duties are correlative. While not legally enforceable by courts through writs, they serve as a guide for citizens' behavior and can be used by courts to determine the constitutional validity of a law.

The eleven Fundamental Duties mandate that every citizen of India shall:
1. Abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag, and the National Anthem.
2. Cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
3. Uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India.
4. Defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
5. Promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood amongst all the people of India transcending religious, linguistic, and regional or sectional diversities; and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
6. Value and preserve the rich heritage of the country’s composite culture.
7. Protect and improve the natural environment including forests, lakes, rivers, and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
8. Develop the scientific temper, humanism, and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
9. Safeguard public property and to abjure violence.
10. Strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity so that the nation constantly rises to higher levels of endeavor and achievement.
11. (Added later) Who is a parent or guardian, to provide opportunities for education to his child or, as the case may be, ward between the age of six and fourteen years.
These duties act as a warning against anti-national and anti-social activities, promoting a sense of discipline and commitment towards the nation's progress.

### 15. a) Explain the powers and functions of President of India as outlined in the Constitution. (7 marks) b) Discuss the Constitutional provisions related to the office of the Prime Minister in India. (7 marks)

**a) Powers and Functions of the President of India**
The President of India is the head of the Indian State, the first citizen of India, and the supreme commander of the defense forces. Although the President is a nominal or titular executive head, with the real executive power resting with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, the office possesses a vast array of formal powers categorized broadly into executive, legislative, financial, judicial, diplomatic, military, and emergency powers.

- **Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the Government of India are formally taken in the name of the President. The President appoints the Prime Minister and, on the PM's advice, other ministers. The President also appoints high constitutional functionaries like the Attorney General, Comptroller and Auditor General, Chief Election Commissioner, Chairman and members of the UPSC, and Governors of states. Furthermore, the President can seek any information relating to the administration of affairs of the Union from the Prime Minister.
- **Legislative Powers:** The President is an integral part of Parliament. They have the power to summon or prorogue Parliament and dissolve the Lok Sabha. The President addresses both Houses at the commencement of the first session after each general election and the first session of each year. Crucially, a bill passed by Parliament becomes an act only after receiving the President's assent. The President can withhold assent or return ordinary bills for reconsideration (suspensive veto). When Parliament is not in session, the President can promulgate ordinances (Article 123) which have the same force as acts of Parliament.
- **Financial and Judicial Powers:** No money bill can be introduced in Parliament without the President's prior recommendation. The President causes the Union Budget to be laid before Parliament and constitutes the Finance Commission every five years. Judicially, the President appoints the Chief Justice and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Under Article 72, the President holds the pardoning power, which includes granting pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment or to suspend, remit, or commute sentences, particularly in cases involving capital punishment or court-martials.
- **Emergency Powers:** The Constitution vests extraordinary powers in the President to deal with crises. The President can declare a National Emergency (Article 352) due to war, external aggression, or armed rebellion; President's Rule (Article 356) due to the failure of constitutional machinery in a state; and a Financial Emergency (Article 360) if the nation's financial stability is threatened.

**b) Constitutional Provisions related to the Office of the Prime Minister**
In the parliamentary system of government established by the Constitution, the Prime Minister is the real executive authority (de facto executive), while the President is the nominal executive. The Prime Minister is the head of the government and plays the most crucial role in the political administration of the country.

The Constitution does not contain a specific procedure for the selection and appointment of the Prime Minister. However, Article 75 simply states that "the Prime Minister shall be appointed by the President." By convention, the President invites the leader of the majority party (or coalition) in the Lok Sabha to form the government and appoints them as the Prime Minister. If no party has a clear majority, the President exercises discretionary power to appoint the person most likely to secure a vote of confidence. A person who is not a member of either House of Parliament can be appointed as Prime Minister, but they must become a member within six months, failing which they cease to hold office.
The Prime Minister is the linchpin of the Council of Ministers. Under Article 74, there shall be a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President. The Prime Minister allocates portfolios among ministers, reshuffles them, and can ask a minister to resign or advise the President to dismiss them. The Prime Minister presides over cabinet meetings and coordinates the functioning of all ministries. Furthermore, Article 78 details the Prime Minister's duties towards the President, which include communicating all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to administration and proposals for legislation, and furnishing any information the President calls for. The Prime Minister acts as the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers, and as the leader of the lower house and the chief spokesperson of the government.

### 16. a) Explore the grounds and circumstances under which a person may be disqualified from being a member of either Loksabha or Rajyasabha. (5 marks) b) Discuss the jurisdiction and powers of Supreme Court as enshrined in the Constitution of India. (9 marks)

**a) Disqualification of Members of Parliament**
The Constitution and statutory laws lay down specific grounds under which a person can be disqualified from being chosen as, and from being, a member of either the Lok Sabha or the Rajya Sabha. These provisions ensure that the legislature is composed of individuals of integrity and competence.

According to **Article 102** of the Constitution, a person is disqualified under the following circumstances:
1. **Holding an Office of Profit:** If they hold any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of any State, other than an office exempted by Parliament by law. This ensures MPs remain independent of executive influence.
2. **Unsound Mind:** If they are declared to be of unsound mind by a competent court.
3. **Insolvent:** If they are an undischarged insolvent (bankrupt), meaning they cannot manage their own finances and thus are deemed unfit to manage public finances.
4. **Not a Citizen:** If they are not a citizen of India, or have voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign state, or are under any acknowledgment of allegiance or adherence to a foreign state.
5. **Disqualified by Law:** If they are so disqualified by or under any law made by Parliament. This led to the Representation of the People Act, 1951, which details further grounds such as conviction for certain electoral offenses, conviction resulting in imprisonment for two or more years, failing to lodge election expenses accounts, having an interest in government contracts, or being dismissed from government service for corruption.

Additionally, the **Tenth Schedule** (Anti-Defection Law), introduced by the 52nd Amendment in 1985, provides grounds for disqualification on the basis of defection. A member incurs disqualification if they voluntarily give up the membership of their political party or if they vote or abstain from voting in the House contrary to the directions issued by their party whip (without prior permission). The decision on disqualification regarding the Tenth Schedule is taken by the Presiding Officer of the House (Speaker or Chairman), while for other grounds, the President decides based on the opinion of the Election Commission.

**b) Jurisdiction and Powers of the Supreme Court**
The Supreme Court of India is the highest judicial court and the final court of appeal under the Constitution. It is the guarantor of fundamental rights and the guardian of the Constitution. Its jurisdiction is extraordinarily wide and can be classified into original, appellate, advisory, and writ jurisdictions, along with inherent powers.

- **Original Jurisdiction (Article 131):** The Supreme Court functions as a federal court. It has exclusive original jurisdiction to resolve disputes between the Centre and one or more States, between the Centre and States on one side and other States on the other, or between two or more States. This jurisdiction is confined to matters involving legal rights, not political disputes.
- **Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32):** The Supreme Court is the defender of the fundamental rights of citizens. Any person whose fundamental rights are violated can directly approach the Supreme Court. The Court is empowered to issue prerogative writs—Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, and Certiorari—for the enforcement of these rights.
- **Appellate Jurisdiction:** The Supreme Court is primarily a court of appeal and hears appeals against judgments of the High Courts. This jurisdiction spans three categories:
  1. *Constitutional Matters (Article 132):* Appeals in civil, criminal, or other proceedings if the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law regarding the interpretation of the Constitution.
  2. *Civil Matters (Article 133):* Appeals in civil cases if the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance.
  3. *Criminal Matters (Article 134):* Appeals in criminal cases. In some situations (like if the High Court reverses an acquittal and sentences the accused to death), an appeal lies as a matter of right. In others, a High Court certificate is needed. Additionally, under Article 136, the Supreme Court has the extraordinary power to grant Special Leave to Appeal from any judgment or order passed by any court or tribunal in India.
- **Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143):** The President can seek the opinion of the Supreme Court on any question of law or fact of public importance. The Court's opinion is advisory and not binding on the President, but it holds great persuasive value.
- **Court of Record & Judicial Review:** Under Article 129, the Supreme Court is a court of record, meaning its judgments are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony, and it has the power to punish for contempt of itself. Crucially, the Court possesses the power of Judicial Review to examine the constitutionality of legislative enactments and executive orders, striking them down if they violate the Constitution.

### 17. a) Discuss the composition and functions of State Executive. (10 marks) b) Explain the Constitutional provisions outlining the qualifications required for an individual to become a member of state legislature. Also discuss the disqualifications. (4 marks)

**a) Composition and Functions of the State Executive**
The constitutional framework for the state executive mirrors that of the Union executive, reflecting the parliamentary system of government at the regional level. The State Executive, governed by Part VI of the Constitution (Articles 153 to 167), comprises the Governor, the Chief Minister, the Council of Ministers, and the Advocate General of the State. 

1. **The Governor:** The Governor is the constitutional head of the state, analogous to the President at the Centre. Appointed by the President, the Governor holds office during the President's pleasure. The Governor possesses significant executive, legislative, financial, and judicial powers. All executive actions are formally taken in the Governor's name. They appoint the Chief Minister, other ministers, the Advocate General, and members of the State Public Service Commission. Legislatively, the Governor summons and prorogues the state legislature, can dissolve the Legislative Assembly, addresses the legislature, and gives assent to bills. Crucially, they can reserve certain bills for the President's consideration and promulgate ordinances when the legislature is not in session. Unlike the President, the Governor has certain constitutionally defined discretionary powers.
2. **The Chief Minister and Council of Ministers:** While the Governor is the nominal head, the Chief Minister is the real executive authority. The Chief Minister is the leader of the majority party in the Legislative Assembly and is appointed by the Governor. The CM advises the Governor on appointing other ministers, allocates portfolios, and presides over cabinet meetings. The Council of Ministers, headed by the CM, is collectively responsible to the State Legislative Assembly. They formulate state policies, implement laws, and manage the day-to-day administration of the state. The CM serves as the primary link of communication between the Cabinet and the Governor.
3. **The Advocate General:** Appointed by the Governor under Article 165, the Advocate General is the highest law officer in the state. They give advice to the state government on legal matters and perform other duties of a legal character. They have the right to speak and take part in proceedings of the state legislature without the right to vote.

The primary function of the State Executive is to implement the laws made by the State Legislature and to govern the state in accordance with the Constitution. It oversees maintaining public order, managing state finances, executing development plans, and administering subjects listed in the State and Concurrent Lists.

**b) Qualifications and Disqualifications for State Legislature**
To be chosen as a member of a State Legislature (Legislative Assembly or Legislative Council), an individual must possess qualifications laid down in Article 173 and the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
**Qualifications:**
1. The person must be a citizen of India.
2. They must take an oath or affirmation before an authority prescribed by the Election Commission.
3. Age criterion: For the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha), the minimum age is 25 years. For the Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad), the minimum age is 30 years.
4. They must be registered as an elector for any assembly constituency in that state.
**Disqualifications:**
Under Article 191, a person is disqualified if:
1. They hold an office of profit under the Union or State government (unless exempted).
2. They are of unsound mind as declared by a court.
3. They are an undischarged insolvent.
4. They are not an Indian citizen or have voluntarily acquired citizenship of a foreign state.
5. They are disqualified under any law made by Parliament (like the Representation of the People Act for corrupt electoral practices, criminal convictions, etc.).
Furthermore, the Tenth Schedule disqualifies members on grounds of defection (changing party allegiance).

### 18. a) Discuss the role of High Courts in state judiciary. (8 marks) b) Discuss the qualifications, appointment process and functions of the Advocate General. (6 marks)

**a) Role of High Courts in State Judiciary**
The High Court occupies the apex position in the judicial administration of a state in India. Established under Part VI, Chapter V of the Constitution (Articles 214 to 231), every state is meant to have a High Court, though Parliament can establish a common High Court for two or more states. The High Court's role is pivotal in maintaining the rule of law, protecting fundamental rights, and exercising supervisory control over the subordinate judiciary within the state.

The High Court functions as the principal court of original and appellate jurisdiction in the state. 
- **Original Jurisdiction:** While primarily an appellate court, High Courts have original jurisdiction in matters like disputes relating to the election of members of Parliament and state legislatures, revenue matters, admiralty, and contempt of court. Crucially, under Article 226, the High Court has extensive writ jurisdiction. It can issue writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, Certiorari) not only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights but also "for any other purpose" (i.e., enforcement of ordinary legal rights), making its writ jurisdiction wider than that of the Supreme Court.
- **Appellate Jurisdiction:** The High Court hears appeals against the judgments of subordinate courts in both civil and criminal matters. It serves as a vital link between the district courts and the Supreme Court.
- **Supervisory Role (Article 227):** The High Court holds the power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals (except military tribunals) functioning within its territorial jurisdiction. It can call for returns, make general rules prescribing practices and proceedings, and prescribe forms for book-keeping by lower courts.
- **Control over Subordinate Courts (Article 235):** The High Court exercises administrative control over the subordinate judiciary. Matters relating to the posting, promotion, and grant of leave to district judges and other judicial officers fall under its purview.
- **Court of Record:** Like the Supreme Court, the High Court is a court of record. Its judgments are recognized as legal precedents within the state, and it has the power to punish for its contempt. Through the power of judicial review, it can declare state laws unconstitutional if they violate fundamental rights or are beyond the legislative competence of the state.

**b) Advocate General: Qualifications, Appointment, and Functions**
The Advocate General is the highest law officer of a state, corresponding to the Attorney General of India at the central level. The office is created by Article 165 of the Constitution.

- **Appointment:** The Advocate General is appointed by the Governor of the state. However, the Governor acts on the advice of the State Council of Ministers. The Advocate General holds office during the pleasure of the Governor, which means they can be removed by the Governor at any time, typically when the government that appointed them resigns or is replaced.
- **Qualifications:** To be appointed as an Advocate General, a person must be qualified to be appointed as a judge of a High Court. This implies they must be a citizen of India and must have held a judicial office in India for ten years or have been an advocate of a High Court for at least ten years.
- **Functions and Duties:** The primary duty of the Advocate General is to give advice to the Government of the State upon legal matters and to perform other duties of a legal character assigned by the Governor. They discharge functions conferred on them by the Constitution or any other law. They represent the state government in major cases before the High Court and the Supreme Court. A significant constitutional privilege granted to the Advocate General (under Article 177) is the right to speak and take part in the proceedings of both Houses of the State Legislature, and any committee of the Legislature to which they are named, but they are not entitled to vote. This allows the state government to seek their expert legal opinion during legislative debates.

### 19. a) Outline the functions and significance of the Finance Commission. (6 marks) b) Explore Constitutional provisions related to Public services in India. (8 marks)

**a) Functions and Significance of the Finance Commission**
The Finance Commission is a quasi-judicial, constitutional body established under Article 280 of the Indian Constitution. It is constituted by the President of India every five years or earlier if deemed necessary. Its primary objective is to define the financial relations between the central government and the state governments, ensuring equitable distribution of resources in India's federal setup.

**Functions:** The core functions of the Finance Commission include making recommendations to the President regarding:
1. **Vertical Devolution:** The distribution between the Union and the States of the net proceeds of taxes that are to be divided between them.
2. **Horizontal Devolution:** The allocation among the States of their respective shares of such proceeds, based on a formula considering factors like population, area, income distance, and demographic performance.
3. **Grants-in-aid:** The principles that should govern the grants-in-aid of the revenues of the States out of the Consolidated Fund of India to states in need of assistance.
4. **Local Bodies:** The measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of the Panchayats and Municipalities in the State on the basis of the recommendations made by the Finance Commission of the State.
5. Any other matter referred to the Commission by the President in the interests of sound finance.

**Significance:** The Finance Commission is crucial for maintaining the fiscal federalism of India. Because the Centre has greater revenue-generating powers (like Income Tax, GST) while States bear heavy expenditure responsibilities (like health, education, law and order), a vertical fiscal imbalance exists. The Commission corrects this imbalance by ensuring states receive a fair share of central taxes. Its objective formula-based approach prevents political bias in resource allocation, ensuring that poorer states or those with geographical disadvantages receive adequate funds to maintain a minimum standard of public services, thus fostering balanced regional development.

**b) Constitutional Provisions related to Public Services in India**
Public services are the backbone of the administrative machinery, essential for executing government policies and maintaining continuity in administration. Part XIV of the Constitution (Articles 308 to 323) comprehensively deals with the Services under the Union and the States.

The Constitution recognizes three categories of services: All-India Services (like IAS, IPS, IFoS), Central Services, and State Services. 
- **Article 309** empowers the Parliament (for Union/All-India services) and State Legislatures (for state services) to regulate the recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services. 
- **Article 310 (Doctrine of Pleasure):** This article states that every person who is a member of a defense service or a civil service of the Union or an All-India service holds office during the *pleasure of the President*. Similarly, a member of a civil service of a State holds office during the *pleasure of the Governor*. This means they can theoretically be dismissed at any time.
- **Article 311 (Safeguards):** To prevent arbitrary dismissal and provide security of tenure, Article 311 places two important restrictions on the 'Doctrine of Pleasure'. First, a civil servant cannot be dismissed or removed by an authority subordinate to that by which they were appointed. Second, they cannot be dismissed, removed, or reduced in rank except after an inquiry in which they have been informed of the charges and given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. 
- **Article 312 (All-India Services):** This is a unique feature of Indian federalism. If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting, declaring it is necessary in the national interest, Parliament may by law create one or more All-India services common to the Union and the States (e.g., the creation of the Indian Forest Service in 1966). These officers are recruited and trained by the Centre but allotted to serve in various State cadres, promoting national integration and high administrative standards.

### 20. a) Explain the composition, functions and powers of Union Public Service Commission. (6 marks) b) Discuss the qualifications, appointment and functions of Comptroller and Auditor General. (8 marks)

**a) Composition, Functions, and Powers of the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC)**
The Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) is a central recruiting agency in India. It is an independent constitutional body established under Articles 315 to 323 in Part XIV of the Constitution. Its independence is vital to ensure that recruitment to the civil services is based purely on merit, free from political patronage.

**Composition:** The UPSC consists of a Chairman and other members appointed by the President of India. The Constitution does not specify the strength of the Commission, leaving it to the President's discretion (usually it consists of 9 to 11 members). However, it mandates that at least one-half of the members must be persons who have held office for at least ten years either under the Government of India or under the Government of a State. The Chairman and members hold office for a term of six years or until they attain the age of 65 years, whichever is earlier. To ensure independence, their conditions of service cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment, and they can only be removed by the President on grounds of misbehavior after an inquiry by the Supreme Court.

**Functions and Powers:** The primary function of the UPSC is to conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the Union, which includes All-India Services (IAS, IPS, etc.), Central Civil Services, and defense services (NDA, CDS). Furthermore, the Constitution mandates that the UPSC must be consulted by the government on several matters relating to personnel management:
1. All matters relating to methods of recruitment to civil services and for civil posts.
2. The principles to be followed in making appointments, promotions, and transfers from one service to another, and on the suitability of candidates.
3. On all disciplinary matters affecting a person serving under the Government of India in a civil capacity, including memorials or petitions relating to such matters.
4. Any claim for the award of a pension in respect of injuries sustained while serving under the government.
While the UPSC's recommendations are advisory in nature, they carry immense weight, and the government must explain any departure from its advice to Parliament.

**b) Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG): Qualifications, Appointment, and Functions**
The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India is the supreme audit institution of the country, established under Article 148 of the Constitution. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar considered the CAG to be the most important officer under the Constitution of India, as they are the guardian of the public purse and ensure that not a single rupee is spent out of the Consolidated Fund without parliamentary authorization.

- **Appointment and Qualifications:** The CAG is appointed by the President of India by a warrant under their hand and seal. The Constitution does not prescribe specific qualifications for the post, but conventionally, a senior Indian Administrative Service (IAS) officer is appointed. Before taking office, the CAG makes an oath or affirmation before the President. The CAG holds office for a term of six years or up to the age of 65 years, whichever is earlier.
- **Independence:** To ensure impartial auditing, the CAG is provided with strict security of tenure. They can be removed by the President only on the same grounds and in the same manner as a judge of the Supreme Court (proved misbehavior or incapacity requiring a special majority resolution in both Houses of Parliament). After retirement, the CAG is ineligible for further office under the Government of India or any State. Their administrative expenses are charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India and are not subject to parliamentary vote.
- **Functions and Duties (Article 149):** The primary duty of the CAG is to uphold the Constitution and the laws of Parliament in the field of financial administration. Their functions include:
  1. Auditing all receipts and expenditures from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and Union Territories having legislative assemblies.
  2. Auditing all transactions relating to the Contingency Funds and Public Accounts at both central and state levels.
  3. Auditing all trading, manufacturing, profit and loss accounts, and balance sheets kept by any department of the Central or State Governments.
  4. Auditing the receipts and expenditures of government companies and corporations substantially financed by central or state revenues.
  5. The CAG submits audit reports relating to the accounts of the Union to the President, who causes them to be laid before both Houses of Parliament (Article 151). These reports form the basis for the scrutiny conducted by the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of Parliament, thereby enforcing the accountability of the executive to the legislature in financial matters.
