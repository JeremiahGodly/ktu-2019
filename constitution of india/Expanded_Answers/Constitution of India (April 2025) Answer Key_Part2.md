### 11 a) Explain the significance of the preamble of Indian constitution.
### 11 b) Explain the historical background of the Indian constitution.
**Significance of the Preamble:**
The Preamble is the introductory statement of the Indian Constitution, often referred to as its soul or its identity card. It was based on the "Objectives Resolution" drafted and moved by Jawaharlal Nehru and adopted by the Constituent Assembly. Its significance lies in several dimensions. Firstly, it indicates the source of the Constitution’s authority. The opening words, "We, the people of India," unequivocally declare that the ultimate sovereignty rests with the Indian citizens, and the Constitution is not a gift from a foreign power but a document created and adopted by the people themselves. Secondly, it lays down the foundational nature of the Indian State, declaring it to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic entity. Each word holds immense weight: 'Sovereign' implies absolute independence in internal and external affairs; 'Socialist' (added by the 42nd Amendment) aims to reduce inequalities in income and status; 'Secular' (also added by the 42nd Amendment) ensures the state has no official religion and treats all religions equally; 'Democratic' denotes a government by the people; and 'Republic' means the head of state is elected, not a hereditary monarch.
Thirdly, the Preamble outlines the grand objectives the state strives to secure for all its citizens: Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and of opportunity), and Fraternity (assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation). While the Preamble is non-justiciable (cannot be enforced in a court of law), the Supreme Court in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) held that it is an integral part of the Constitution. It serves as a guiding light for constitutional interpretation. Whenever there is ambiguity in the text of the Constitution, the courts rely on the Preamble to ascertain the true intent of the framers. It represents the philosophical bedrock and the ultimate goals of the Indian constitutional project.

**Historical Background of the Indian Constitution:**
The Indian Constitution was not created in a vacuum; it is the culmination of a long historical process, deeply influenced by the British colonial administration and the Indian independence movement. The genesis can be traced back to the various acts passed by the British Parliament to regulate the governance of India. The Regulating Act of 1773 was the first step towards centralizing administration in British India, creating the office of the Governor-General of Bengal. The Charter Acts of 1833 and 1853 further centralized legislative powers and began separating the executive and legislative functions.
After the Revolt of 1857, the Government of India Act 1858 transferred the power from the East India Company directly to the British Crown, establishing the office of the Viceroy and the Secretary of State for India. The subsequent Indian Councils Acts of 1861, 1892, and 1909 slowly introduced the concept of representation and elections, albeit on a very limited scale, and allowed Indians to participate in the legislative councils. The Government of India Act 1919 introduced 'Dyarchy' in the provinces, separating subjects into transferred and reserved categories, marking a step towards responsible government. 
However, the most significant precursor to the present Constitution was the Government of India Act 1935. It proposed an All-India Federation and introduced provincial autonomy, abolishing dyarchy in the provinces but introducing it at the center. It provided a detailed administrative framework, much of which was later adopted into the 1950 Constitution, including the federal scheme, the office of the governor, judiciary, public service commissions, and emergency provisions. Finally, the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 proposed the formation of a Constituent Assembly to frame a constitution for an independent India. The Assembly, comprising indirectly elected representatives, worked for nearly three years, debating and drafting the Constitution, which was finally adopted on November 26, 1949, and came into full effect on January 26, 1950, marking India's transition to a fully sovereign republic.

### 12 a) Explain the modes of acquiring citizenship under citizenship Act.1955.
### 12 b) Explain about deprivation of citizenship.
**Modes of Acquiring Citizenship:**
Part II of the Constitution (Articles 5 to 11) deals with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution. For matters subsequent to 1950, Article 11 empowered Parliament to enact laws. Consequently, Parliament passed the Citizenship Act, 1955, which has been amended several times. This Act prescribes five primary modes of acquiring Indian citizenship:
1. **By Birth (Jus Soli):** Initially, anyone born in India after January 26, 1950, was a citizen irrespective of parents' nationality. However, to curb illegal immigration, the law was amended. Currently, a person born in India on or after December 3, 2004, is considered a citizen by birth only if both parents are citizens of India, or if one parent is a citizen and the other is not an illegal migrant at the time of birth.
2. **By Descent (Jus Sanguinis):** This applies to persons born outside India. A person born outside India on or after December 3, 2004, is not a citizen by descent unless their birth is registered at an Indian consulate within one year of its occurrence, with a declaration that the minor does not hold a passport of another country.
3. **By Registration:** The Central Government may, on an application, register as a citizen of India any person (not being an illegal migrant) if they belong to specific categories. These include persons of Indian origin who are ordinarily resident in India for seven years before making an application, persons of Indian origin who are ordinarily resident in any country outside undivided India, persons who are married to a citizen of India and are ordinarily resident in India for seven years, and minor children of persons who are citizens of India.
4. **By Naturalization:** A foreigner (not an illegal migrant) can acquire Indian citizenship on application for naturalization to the Central Government. The applicant must fulfill qualifications detailed in the Third Schedule of the Act, which generally include renouncing their previous citizenship, residing in India or being in the service of the Government of India for a specified period (usually 11 of the previous 14 years, plus the continuous 12 months immediately preceding the application), being of good character, and having an adequate knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule. The Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019, relaxed the residence requirement from 11 years to 5 years for specific minority communities (Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis, and Christians) from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan who entered India on or before December 31, 2014.
5. **By Incorporation of Territory:** If any new territory becomes a part of India, the Government of India specifies the persons who among the people of the territory shall be the citizens of India. Such persons become the citizens of India from the notified date. For example, when Pondicherry became a part of India, the Government of India issued the Citizenship (Pondicherry) Order, 1962.

**Deprivation of Citizenship:**
Deprivation is a compulsory termination of Indian citizenship by the Central Government. It applies specifically to citizens who acquired citizenship by naturalization or by registration. A natural-born citizen cannot be deprived of their citizenship. The Central Government may deprive a person of citizenship if it is satisfied that:
1. The registration or certificate of naturalization was obtained by means of fraud, false representation, or the concealment of any material fact.
2. The citizen has shown themselves by act or speech to be disloyal or disaffected towards the Constitution of India as by law established.
3. The citizen has, during any war in which India may be engaged, unlawfully traded or communicated with an enemy or been engaged in, or associated with, any business that was to their knowledge carried on in such manner as to assist an enemy in that war.
4. The citizen has, within five years after registration or naturalization, been sentenced in any country to imprisonment for a term of not less than two years.
5. The citizen has been ordinarily resident out of India for a continuous period of seven years, and during that period, they have neither been at any time a student of any educational institution in a country outside India or in the service of a Government in India or of an international organization of which India is a member, nor registered annually in the prescribed manner at an Indian consulate their intention to retain their citizenship of India.
Before passing an order of deprivation, the Central Government must give the person a written notice containing the grounds on which the order is proposed to be made, and the person has the right to have their case referred to a Committee of Inquiry.

### 13 a) Explain the nature of the right to freedom of religion in India.
### 13 b) Explain Cultural and Educational Rights?
**Right to Freedom of Religion:**
The right to freedom of religion is a fundamental pillar of Indian secularism, enshrined in Part III of the Constitution under Articles 25 to 28. Unlike Western models of secularism, which enforce a strict separation between religion and the state, Indian secularism is characterized by "principled distance," where the state respects all religions equally and intervenes only when religious practices infringe upon public order, morality, health, or other fundamental rights.
Article 25 guarantees to all persons (not just citizens) the freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate religion. However, this right is not absolute. It is subject to public order, morality, and health. The state can also regulate secular activities associated with religious practice (economic, financial, political) and enact laws for social welfare and reform, such as throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus. 
Article 26 grants specific rights to religious denominations or sections thereof. Subject to public order, morality, and health, every religious denomination has the right to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes, to manage its own affairs in matters of religion, to own and acquire movable and immovable property, and to administer such property in accordance with law. The distinction is that while Article 25 protects individual rights, Article 26 protects collective rights.
Article 27 ensures that no person shall be compelled to pay any taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated in payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination. The state can levy a fee for secular administration of religious institutions, but not a tax to promote a specific religion.
Article 28 prohibits religious instruction in any educational institution wholly maintained out of state funds. However, this prohibition does not apply to institutions administered by the state but established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction. In state-recognized or state-aided institutions, religious instruction can be provided, but attending it is completely voluntary; no person can be compelled to participate without their consent (or their guardian's consent if a minor).

**Cultural and Educational Rights:**
Enshrined in Articles 29 and 30, these rights are designed to protect the interests of minorities in India. Given India's vast diversity, safeguarding the cultural and linguistic identity of minority groups is essential for a true democracy, preventing the majority from imposing its culture on minorities.
Article 29 protects the interests of minorities. Article 29(1) provides that any section of the citizens residing in the territory of India or any part thereof having a distinct language, script, or culture of its own shall have the fundamental right to conserve the same. This applies broadly to any section of citizens, whether they are a majority or minority in the overall national context, as long as they have a distinct culture. Article 29(2) states that no citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language, or any of them. This protects individual citizens against discrimination in educational access.
Article 30 specifically deals with the rights of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions. Article 30(1) grants all minorities, whether based on religion or language, the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. This ensures that minorities can preserve and transmit their culture through education. The state cannot impose its educational policies in a way that destroys the minority character of these institutions, although it can prescribe reasonable regulations for academic standards, health, and sanitation. Furthermore, Article 30(2) mandates that the state shall not, in granting aid to educational institutions, discriminate against any educational institution on the ground that it is under the management of a minority, whether based on religion or language. These rights ensure that minorities do not feel culturally alienated and can actively participate in the nation's educational landscape while preserving their unique heritage.

### 14 a) Right to Constitutional Remedies is the soul of Indian constitution. Elucidate.
### 14 b) When might Fundamental Rights are not guaranteed or suspended?
**Right to Constitutional Remedies:**
Article 32 of the Constitution provides the Right to Constitutional Remedies. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar unequivocally described Article 32 as the "heart and soul" of the Constitution, and rightly so, because a declaration of fundamental rights is meaningless unless there is an effective, accessible, and guaranteed machinery for enforcing them. Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right, which means the right to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of fundamental rights is guaranteed and cannot be suspended except as provided by the Constitution.
Under Article 32, the Supreme Court has the original jurisdiction to issue directions, orders, or writs for the enforcement of any of the fundamental rights enshrined in Part III. The five prerogative writs available are Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, and Quo Warranto. 
- **Habeas Corpus** ("to have the body") is issued against unlawful detention, commanding the detainer to produce the detained person before the court to examine the legality of the detention. It is a powerful bulwark of personal liberty.
- **Mandamus** ("we command") is a command issued by the court to a public official, public body, corporation, lower court, tribunal, or government, asking them to perform their official duties that they have failed or refused to perform.
- **Prohibition** is issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction that it does not possess. It is preventive in nature.
- **Certiorari** ("to be certified") is issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal either to transfer a case pending with them or to squash their order. It is both preventive and curative, usually issued on grounds of excess of jurisdiction or error of law.
- **Quo Warranto** ("by what authority") is issued to inquire into the legality of a claim which a person asserts to a public office, preventing illegal usurpation of public office.
Article 32 empowers any affected individual to directly approach the highest court of the land, bypassing the lower courts, if their fundamental rights are violated. This makes the Supreme Court the defender and guarantor of fundamental rights. Without Article 32, the fundamental rights would remain mere paper promises without any practical utility.

**Suspension of Fundamental Rights:**
Fundamental Rights in India are not absolute; they are subject to reasonable restrictions and can be suspended under specific extraordinary circumstances to safeguard the sovereignty, security, and integrity of the nation. The Constitution makers recognized that during grave national crises, the life of the state is paramount, and individual liberties might need to be temporarily curtailed.
The primary mechanism for the suspension of Fundamental Rights is during a National Emergency proclaimed under Article 352 (on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion). The effects of a National Emergency on Fundamental Rights are detailed in Articles 358 and 359.
According to Article 358, when a proclamation of National Emergency is made on the grounds of war or external aggression (but not armed rebellion), the six fundamental rights guaranteed by Article 19 (freedoms of speech, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession) are automatically suspended. The state is freed from the restrictions imposed by Article 19, allowing it to make any law or take any executive action inconsistent with these rights. Once the emergency ceases, Article 19 automatically revives.
According to Article 359, the President may by order suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency (declared on any ground). Under Article 359, the rights themselves are not theoretically suspended, but the remedy to enforce them is suspended. This means citizens cannot approach the courts seeking enforcement of the specified rights. 
Crucially, the 44th Amendment Act of 1978 introduced significant safeguards to prevent the misuse of these provisions. It mandated that the President cannot suspend the right to move the court for the enforcement of Articles 20 (protection in respect of conviction for offenses) and 21 (protection of life and personal liberty) even during a National Emergency. Furthermore, martial law (Article 34) also allows for the restriction of fundamental rights in any area where it is in force, aiming to restore order in cases of severe disturbance.

### 15 a) Explain the powers of the President of India under the constitution.
### 15 b) Explain the writ jurisdiction of Supreme Court.
**Powers of the President of India:**
The President of India is the head of the State, the first citizen of India, and acts as the symbol of unity, integrity, and solidarity of the nation. While India follows a parliamentary system where the real executive power lies with the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers, the Constitution vests vast formal powers in the President, categorized under various heads:
1. **Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the Government of India are formally taken in the name of the President. The President appoints the Prime Minister and, on their advice, other ministers. The President appoints key constitutional functionaries including the Attorney General of India, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner and other Election Commissioners, the Chairman and members of the UPSC, and Governors of states. The President directly administers the Union Territories through administrators.
2. **Legislative Powers:** The President is an integral part of Parliament. The President summons and prorogues the Parliament and can dissolve the Lok Sabha. The President addresses the Parliament at the commencement of the first session after each general election and the first session of each year. The President can promulgate Ordinances (Article 123) when Parliament is not in session, which have the same force as an Act of Parliament, though they must be approved by Parliament within six weeks of its reassembly. No bill can become an Act without the President's assent, and the President has veto powers (absolute, suspensive, and pocket veto) over bills passed by Parliament.
3. **Financial Powers:** Money bills can be introduced in the Parliament only with the prior recommendation of the President. The President causes the Annual Financial Statement (Budget) to be laid before the Parliament. The President can make advances out of the Contingency Fund of India to meet unforeseen expenditure and constitutes a Finance Commission every five years to recommend the distribution of revenues between the Centre and the states.
4. **Judicial Powers:** The President appoints the Chief Justice and judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. Under Article 72, the President has the sovereign power to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment or to suspend, remit, or commute the sentence of any person convicted of any offense, particularly in cases of court-martial, offenses against Union laws, and death sentences.
5. **Diplomatic and Military Powers:** International treaties and agreements are negotiated and concluded on behalf of the President, though subject to parliamentary approval. The President is the Supreme Commander of the defense forces of India and appoints the chiefs of the Army, Navy, and Air Force. The President can declare war or conclude peace, subject to parliamentary approval.
6. **Emergency Powers:** The President possesses extraordinary powers to deal with national crises, including the declaration of National Emergency (Article 352), President's Rule in states (Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360). 

**Writ Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court:**
The Supreme Court of India is constituted as the protector and guarantor of the Fundamental Rights of the citizens. To effectively discharge this role, Article 32 of the Constitution confers upon the Supreme Court the original and wide-ranging jurisdiction to issue directions, orders, or writs.
The writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court is strictly limited to the enforcement of Fundamental Rights enshrined in Part III of the Constitution. A citizen can directly approach the Supreme Court if they believe their fundamental rights have been violated, without going through the appeals process from lower courts. The five types of writs that the Supreme Court can issue are:
1. **Habeas Corpus:** Issued to secure the release of a person who has been illegally detained. The court directs the detaining authority to produce the person and justify the detention. If the detention is found to be unlawful, the person is set free.
2. **Mandamus:** Issued to a public authority, directing it to perform a public duty which it is legally bound to perform but has failed or refused to do. It cannot be issued against a private individual or body.
3. **Prohibition:** Issued by a superior court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or acting contrary to the rules of natural justice. It is preventive in nature.
4. **Certiorari:** Issued by a superior court to a lower court or tribunal to quash an order or decision that has been made without jurisdiction or in excess of jurisdiction, or where there is an error of law apparent on the face of the record. It is both preventive and curative.
5. **Quo Warranto:** Issued to inquire into the legality of a person's claim to a public office. If the court finds that the person is not entitled to hold that office, it can oust them from it.
It is important to note that while the Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction under Article 32 is exclusive for fundamental rights, the High Courts have a broader writ jurisdiction under Article 226. High Courts can issue writs not only for the enforcement of fundamental rights but also "for any other purpose" (i.e., enforcement of ordinary legal rights). However, the territorial jurisdiction of the Supreme Court extends across the entire territory of India, whereas a High Court's jurisdiction is generally confined to the territorial limits of the state over which it exercises jurisdiction.

### 17 a) Explain the steps in the appointment of Chief Minister.
### 17 b) What are the qualifications needed for Governor?
**Steps in the Appointment of Chief Minister:**
The Chief Minister is the real executive authority at the state level in India, functioning as the head of the state government. Article 164 of the Constitution merely states that the Chief Minister shall be appointed by the Governor. It does not contain any specific procedure for the selection and appointment of the Chief Minister. However, in practice, the Governor is bound by the conventions of the parliamentary system.
The standard procedure involves the following steps:
1. **Elections and Majority:** Following a general election to the State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha), the political party or a pre-poll alliance that secures an absolute majority (more than half of the total seats) elects its leader.
2. **Invitation by the Governor:** The Governor is conventionally obligated to invite the leader of the majority party or alliance to form the government. The Governor does not have discretionary power in this scenario. The chosen leader is appointed as the Chief Minister.
3. **Discretionary Situations:** The Governor exercises personal discretion in the selection and appointment of the Chief Minister only when no single party secures a clear majority (a hung assembly). In such situations, the Governor usually invites the leader of the single largest party or the leader of a post-poll coalition that appears most capable of providing a stable government.
4. **Proving Majority:** When appointed under discretionary circumstances, the Governor directs the newly appointed Chief Minister to seek a vote of confidence in the Legislative Assembly within a stipulated timeframe (often one month) to prove their majority support on the floor of the house.
5. **Non-Member Appointment:** A person who is not a member of either house of the state legislature can be appointed as Chief Minister. However, under Article 164(4), they must become a member of the state legislature (either the Legislative Assembly or the Legislative Council, if it exists) within six months of their appointment; otherwise, they cease to be a minister (and thereby Chief Minister). 
6. **Oath of Office:** Before entering upon their office, the Governor administers to the Chief Minister the oaths of office and secrecy, swearing allegiance to the Constitution, upholding the sovereignty and integrity of India, and faithfully discharging duties without fear or favor.

**Qualifications needed for Governor:**
The Governor is the constitutional head of the State executive, appointed by the President of India. Despite being a high constitutional office, the Constitution lays down very simple and minimal qualifications for the appointment of a Governor. According to Article 157 and Article 158, the qualifications and conditions of the office are as follows:
1. **Citizenship:** The most fundamental requirement is that the person must be a citizen of India.
2. **Age:** The individual must have completed the age of 35 years. This ensures that the person appointed has the requisite maturity and experience to handle the constitutional responsibilities.
3. **No Office of Profit:** Under Article 158(2), the Governor shall not hold any other office of profit under the government. 
4. **Not a Member of Legislature:** Article 158(1) states that the Governor shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or of a House of the Legislature of any State specified in the First Schedule. If a member of a legislature is appointed as Governor, they are deemed to have vacated their seat in that House on the date they enter their office as Governor.
In addition to these constitutionally mandated qualifications, two prominent conventions have developed over time regarding the appointment of Governors:
1. **Outsider Convention:** The person appointed should ideally be an outsider, meaning they should not belong to the state where they are appointed. This convention aims to ensure that the Governor remains free from local political entanglements and partisan politics, acting as a neutral constitutional arbiter.
2. **Consultation Convention:** While appointing the Governor, the President (acting on the advice of the Union Cabinet) generally consults the Chief Minister of the state concerned to ensure smooth functioning and a harmonious relationship between the constitutional head and the elected head of the state government. However, these conventions are often violated in practice by various central governments.

### 18 a) Explain the jurisdiction of High court. What are the essential qualifications required for the appointment of High court Judge
### 18 b) Explain the qualifications and disqualifications of membership of the state legislature.
**Jurisdiction of High Court and Qualifications of a Judge:**
The High Court is the highest judicial authority in a state. Its jurisdiction and powers are extensive, encompassing civil, criminal, original, and appellate matters. 
1. **Original Jurisdiction:** High Courts possess original jurisdiction in matters like admiralty, matrimony, probate, contempt of court, and election disputes concerning members of Parliament and State Legislatures. Some High Courts (like Bombay, Madras, and Calcutta) have original civil jurisdiction in cases involving higher financial value.
2. **Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226):** A High Court is empowered to issue writs (habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto, and certiorari) not only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights but also for "any other purpose," meaning ordinary legal rights. This makes the writ jurisdiction of a High Court broader than that of the Supreme Court (which is restricted to fundamental rights).
3. **Appellate Jurisdiction:** It is the primary appellate court within the state. It hears appeals against the judgments of subordinate courts in both civil and criminal matters. In criminal cases, death sentences awarded by a sessions judge must be confirmed by the High Court before execution.
4. **Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227):** A High Court has the power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals (except military tribunals) functioning within its territorial jurisdiction.
5. **Control over Subordinate Courts:** It is consulted by the Governor in matters of appointment, posting, and promotion of district judges and deals with the administrative control of the subordinate judiciary.
6. **Court of Record:** The judgments, proceedings, and acts of the High Courts are recorded for perpetual memory and testimony, holding evidential value, and it has the power to punish for its contempt.

**Qualifications for High Court Judge (Article 217):** To be appointed as a judge of a High Court, a person must possess the following qualifications:
1. They must be a citizen of India.
2. They must have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years; OR
3. They must have been an advocate of a High Court (or High Courts in succession) for at least ten years.
Unlike the Supreme Court, there is no provision for the appointment of a distinguished jurist as a judge of a High Court.

**Qualifications and Disqualifications of State Legislature Membership:**
The Constitution specifies qualifications and disqualifications for individuals seeking election to the State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) or Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad).
**Qualifications (Article 173):**
1. **Citizenship:** Must be a citizen of India and make and subscribe an oath or affirmation bearing true faith and allegiance to the Constitution.
2. **Age:** Must be not less than 25 years of age in the case of the Legislative Assembly and not less than 30 years of age in the case of the Legislative Council.
3. **Other Qualifications:** Must possess such other qualifications as may be prescribed by Parliament. Under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, a person must be an elector for any assembly constituency in the concerned state to contest elections. For seats reserved for SCs or STs, the candidate must belong to those categories.

**Disqualifications (Article 191):**
A person is disqualified for being chosen as, and for being, a member of the Legislative Assembly or Legislative Council of a State if:
1. They hold any office of profit under the Government of India or the Government of any State specified in the First Schedule, other than an office declared by the Legislature of the State by law not to disqualify its holder.
2. They are of unsound mind and stand so declared by a competent court.
3. They are an undischarged insolvent.
4. They are not a citizen of India, or have voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State, or are under any acknowledgment of allegiance or adherence to a foreign State.
5. They are disqualified by or under any law made by Parliament (e.g., under the Representation of the People Act, 1951, for certain electoral offenses, corrupt practices, or conviction for certain crimes resulting in imprisonment for two or more years).
Additionally, a member can be disqualified on the grounds of defection under the provisions of the Tenth Schedule (Anti-Defection Law) if they voluntarily give up membership of their political party or vote contrary to the party whip.

### 19 a) What are the various functions of Finance Commission.
### 19 b) Explain the need of Administrative Tribunals.
**Functions of the Finance Commission:**
The Finance Commission is a constitutional body established under Article 280 of the Constitution. It is constituted by the President every five years or earlier, if considered necessary, to mediate the financial relations between the Centre and the States. Its primary role is to ensure an equitable distribution of financial resources, which is crucial for the smooth functioning of India's federal structure. 
The core functions of the Finance Commission include making recommendations to the President on:
1. **Distribution of Net Proceeds of Taxes:** The most crucial function is recommending the basis for the distribution of the net proceeds of taxes which are to be, or may be, divided between the Union and the States (vertical devolution). It also determines the allocation between the States of the respective shares of such proceeds (horizontal devolution). The Commission uses complex formulas based on factors like population, area, income distance, forest cover, and demographic performance to ensure fairness and equity among states.
2. **Grants-in-Aid:** It determines the principles which should govern the grants-in-aid of the revenues of the States out of the Consolidated Fund of India (under Article 275). These grants are typically given to states in need of assistance, often to cover non-plan revenue deficits or for specific purposes like local body grants or disaster relief.
3. **Augmenting State Consolidated Funds for Local Bodies:** Based on the recommendations made by the State Finance Commissions, the national Finance Commission suggests measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of the Panchayats and Municipalities in the State. This function was added following the 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments to strengthen grassroots democracy.
4. **Any Other Matter:** It advises on any other matter referred to the Commission by the President in the interests of sound finance. 
The Finance Commission acts as a balancing wheel of fiscal federalism in India. While its recommendations are advisory in nature and not legally binding on the government, they are generally accepted due to the constitutional stature and expert nature of the body. 

**Need of Administrative Tribunals:**
Administrative Tribunals were introduced in India by the 42nd Amendment Act of 1976, which added Part XIV-A (Articles 323A and 323B) to the Constitution. The establishment of these tribunals arose from practical necessities and challenges faced by the traditional judicial system, particularly in the realm of service and specialized administrative law. 
The primary need for Administrative Tribunals stems from the massive backlog and pendency of cases in traditional courts. Prior to the tribunals, disputes regarding the recruitment and service conditions of civil servants were litigated in civil courts and High Courts. The rigid, formal procedures and the application of complex rules of evidence in regular courts caused immense delays, meaning civil servants often spent years seeking redress for service-related grievances. Tribunals were designed to offer a speedy and inexpensive mechanism for dispute resolution. They are not bound by the strict procedural codes like the Code of Civil Procedure or the Indian Evidence Act, but rather operate on the principles of natural justice, allowing for much greater flexibility and quicker disposal of cases.
Secondly, administrative disputes often involve complex technical and administrative rules that require specialized knowledge. Traditional judges, while experts in law, might not possess the nuanced understanding of bureaucratic functioning or specialized departmental regulations. Administrative tribunals address this gap by comprising a mix of judicial members (legal experts) and administrative members (experienced civil servants). This blend ensures that decisions are legally sound but also practically viable and informed by an understanding of government administration.
Furthermore, specialized tribunals under Article 323B can be created for various other subjects like taxation, foreign exchange, industrial and labor disputes, land reforms, and elections. This specialization allows for expert handling of specific socio-economic laws, unburdening the High Courts and ensuring that technical disputes are resolved by bodies equipped with the requisite subject-matter expertise. Ultimately, administrative tribunals are essential for providing accessible, expert, and timely justice in the modern administrative state.

### 20 a) Explain the procedure for amending the constitution of India.
### 20 b) Explain the effects of National and Financial emergencies.
**Procedure for Amending the Constitution:**
Article 368 in Part XX of the Indian Constitution deals with the powers of Parliament to amend the Constitution and its procedure. The Constitution makers designed an amending process that strikes a balance between rigidity (to protect fundamental features) and flexibility (to adapt to changing times). 
The procedure for amendment under Article 368 involves the following steps:
1. **Initiation:** An amendment can be initiated only by the introduction of a bill for the purpose in either House of Parliament (Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha). It cannot be initiated in the state legislatures.
2. **Introduction:** The bill can be introduced either by a minister or by a private member and does not require the prior permission of the President.
3. **Special Majority:** The bill must be passed in each House by a "special majority," which means a majority of the total membership of the House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of the House present and voting. 
4. **Joint Sitting:** Each House must pass the bill separately. In case of a disagreement between the two Houses, there is no provision for holding a joint sitting of the two Houses for the purpose of deliberation and passage of the amendment bill.
5. **Ratification by States:** If the bill seeks to amend the federal provisions of the Constitution (such as the election of the President, extent of executive power of the Union and states, the Supreme Court and High Courts, distribution of legislative powers, or Article 368 itself), it must also be ratified by the legislatures of at least half of the states by a simple majority (a majority of the members of the house present and voting).
6. **Presidential Assent:** After being passed by both Houses of Parliament and ratified by the state legislatures (where necessary), the bill is presented to the President for assent. The President must give assent to the bill (made mandatory by the 24th Amendment Act of 1971); they can neither withhold assent nor return the bill for reconsideration. After the President’s assent, the bill becomes an Act.

**Effects of National and Financial Emergencies:**
The Indian Constitution allows the central government to acquire extraordinary powers during crises, fundamentally altering the federal structure into a unitary one temporarily.
**Effects of National Emergency (Article 352):**
A National Emergency can be declared on grounds of war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. Its effects are far-reaching:
1. **Executive:** The executive power of the Centre extends to directing any state regarding the manner in which its executive power is to be exercised. State governments are not suspended but come under the complete control of the Centre.
2. **Legislative:** Parliament becomes empowered to make laws on any subject mentioned in the State List. Although state legislatures are not suspended, parliamentary laws override state laws in case of conflict. Laws made by Parliament on state subjects become inoperative six months after the emergency ceases. 
3. **Financial:** The President can modify the constitutional distribution of revenues between the Centre and the states to ensure adequate funds for the Union.
4. **Fundamental Rights:** As per Article 358, the six fundamental rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended if the emergency is declared on grounds of war or external aggression. Under Article 359, the President can suspend the right to move courts for the enforcement of other specified fundamental rights (except Articles 20 and 21).

**Effects of Financial Emergency (Article 360):**
A Financial Emergency can be declared if the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened. Its effects include:
1. **Executive Directions:** The executive authority of the Union extends to giving directions to any state to observe specified canons of financial propriety.
2. **Salary Reductions:** The President may issue directions for the reduction of salaries and allowances of all or any class of persons serving in connection with the affairs of a state or the Union. This includes the judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
3. **Reservation of Bills:** The President can direct that all money bills or other financial bills, after being passed by the state legislature, must be reserved for the consideration of the President. 
A Financial Emergency allows the Centre to take complete control over the financial matters of the states to restore national economic stability. Notably, a Financial Emergency has never been declared in India to date.
