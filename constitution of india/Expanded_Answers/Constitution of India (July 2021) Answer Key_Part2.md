# Constitution of India (July 2021) Answer Key - Part 2

## PART B (Expanded Answers for 14-mark questions)

**11. a) What is Preamble? Can it be used for the interpretation of the constitution? Also explain its significance. (8)**
**11. b) Describe the salient features of Indian Constitution. (6)**

The Preamble to the Indian Constitution serves as a brief introductory statement that sets out the guiding purpose, principles, and philosophy of the document. It is often referred to as the 'soul of the Constitution' or its 'identity card.' The Preamble is based on the 'Objectives Resolution' drafted and moved by Jawaharlal Nehru, and adopted by the Constituent Assembly. It declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic, and Republic state. It outlines the primary objectives to secure for all citizens: Justice (social, economic, and political), Liberty (of thought, expression, belief, faith, and worship), Equality (of status and of opportunity), and to promote Fraternity, assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the nation.
Regarding its role in the interpretation of the Constitution, the Supreme Court's stance has evolved. Initially, in the Berubari Union case (1960), the Court held that the Preamble is not a part of the Constitution and therefore cannot be used to interpret ambiguous provisions. However, this view was decisively overturned in the landmark Kesavananda Bharati case (1973). Here, the Supreme Court recognized that the Preamble is indeed an integral part of the Constitution. The Court established that while the Preamble itself does not grant power or impose limitations, it is of extreme importance for interpreting the Constitution. Whenever there is ambiguity in the text of the Constitution, the courts rely on the Preamble to ascertain the true meaning and intent of the framers. It acts as a guiding light for judicial interpretation. The significance of the Preamble lies in its embodiment of the fundamental values and the philosophical vision of the Indian state. It serves as a yardstick by which the performance of the government can be measured.
The Indian Constitution, adopted in 1949 and enacted in 1950, possesses several salient features that distinguish it from other global constitutions. Firstly, it is the lengthiest written constitution in the world, comprehensive and detailed, reflecting the country's vast diversity and administrative complexities. Secondly, it exhibits a unique blend of rigidity and flexibility; some provisions require a special majority for amendment, while others can be amended by a simple parliamentary majority. Thirdly, it establishes a Parliamentary form of government, characterized by the presence of nominal and real executives, majority party rule, and collective responsibility of the executive to the legislature. Fourthly, it features a federal system with unitary bias; while it divides power between the center and states, the center remains exceptionally strong, especially during emergencies. Fifthly, the Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights to all citizens, safeguarding individual liberties against state encroachment, while simultaneously prescribing Fundamental Duties. Furthermore, it incorporates Directive Principles of State Policy, serving as a moral compass for the government to establish a welfare state. Other notable features include an independent and integrated judiciary, universal adult franchise, single citizenship, and the provision for emergency powers.

**12. a) Give detail account on the historical background of Indian Constitution. (6)**
**12. b) What is citizenship? Discuss the various methods of acquiring Indian citizenship. (8)**

The historical background of the Indian Constitution is deeply rooted in the various acts passed by the British Parliament during their rule in India. The journey towards a constitutional framework began with the Regulating Act of 1773, which initiated the centralization of administration under the East India Company. Subsequent acts, such as the Pitt's India Act of 1784 and the Charter Acts of 1833 and 1853, further refined the administrative machinery and introduced nascent forms of legislative processes. The watershed moment came after the Revolt of 1857, leading to the Government of India Act 1858, which transferred power directly to the British Crown. The Indian Councils Acts of 1861, 1892, and 1909 gradually increased Indian representation in legislative councils, though on a limited scale. The Government of India Act 1919 introduced 'dyarchy' in the provinces, dividing subjects between the executive and the legislature. However, the most significant precursor to the current Constitution was the Government of India Act 1935. It proposed an All India Federation, introduced provincial autonomy, and established a Federal Court. The drafting of the Constitution itself was the monumental task of the Constituent Assembly, formed in 1946 under the Cabinet Mission Plan. The Assembly, comprising diverse representatives, debated intensely for nearly three years, drawing inspiration from various global constitutions and the 1935 Act, culminating in the adoption of the Constitution on November 26, 1949.
Citizenship, in legal terms, denotes the formal relationship between an individual and a state, granting the individual certain rights and imposing specific duties. It represents full and equal membership in a political community. In India, citizenship is primarily governed by Part II of the Constitution (Articles 5-11) and the Citizenship Act of 1955. India follows the principle of single citizenship, meaning individuals are citizens of India, not of their respective states. The Citizenship Act, 1955, amended several times, prescribes five principal methods for acquiring Indian citizenship. 
1. By Birth: A person born in India on or after specific dates (with conditions regarding the parents' citizenship status) automatically becomes a citizen.
2. By Descent: A person born outside India on or after specific dates can acquire citizenship if one or both parents are Indian citizens, subject to registration requirements.
3. By Registration: Individuals of Indian origin ordinarily resident in India for a specified period, or those married to Indian citizens, can apply for citizenship through registration.
4. By Naturalization: A foreigner can acquire Indian citizenship by naturalization if they have resided in India for a prolonged period (usually 11 of the previous 14 years), renounce their previous citizenship, are of good character, and possess adequate knowledge of a recognized Indian language.
5. By Incorporation of Territory: If a new territory becomes a part of India, the Government of India can specify the persons from that territory who shall become citizens of India.

**13. a) Describe the Right to Constitutional Remedies and explain its significance. (6)**
**13. b) Explain the needs and importance of fundamental duties of an Indian Citizen. (8)**

The Right to Constitutional Remedies, enshrined in Article 32 of the Indian Constitution, is described by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. This right guarantees individuals the ability to approach the Supreme Court directly for the enforcement of their Fundamental Rights. Without this provision, the Fundamental Rights would be mere declarations without any practical utility. Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to issue directions, orders, or writs—specifically Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, and Certiorari—for the enforcement of any right conferred by Part III. This right itself is a Fundamental Right, meaning it cannot be suspended except as provided by the Constitution (such as during a National Emergency). The significance of Article 32 cannot be overstated. It acts as the ultimate guarantor of individual liberties, providing a swift and potent mechanism against state excesses or arbitrary actions. It ensures that the state apparatus respects the constitutional boundaries and protects the citizens from systemic abuses of power. By making the enforcement of rights a fundamental right itself, the framers of the Constitution ensured that democracy in India remains substantive and not just procedural.
Fundamental Duties were not part of the original Constitution but were added by the 42nd Amendment Act in 1976 upon the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Enshrined in Article 51A, these duties outline the moral obligations of all citizens to help promote a spirit of patriotism and uphold the unity of India. The need for these duties arises from the principle that rights and duties are correlative and inseparable. A society where citizens only demand rights without recognizing their responsibilities towards the state and fellow citizens is inherently unstable. The duties serve several crucial purposes. Firstly, they act as a constant reminder to citizens that while enjoying their rights, they must also be conscious of their obligations to their country, society, and fellow beings. Secondly, they serve as a warning against anti-national and anti-social activities, such as burning the national flag, destroying public property, or promoting communal discord. Thirdly, they promote a sense of discipline and commitment among the citizenry, fostering active participation in the realization of national goals. Lastly, they help the courts in examining and determining the constitutional validity of a law. If a law seeks to give effect to any fundamental duty, the courts may consider it reasonable in relation to Article 14 or 19. While non-justiciable, they establish a normative framework for civic behavior.

**14. a) What are Fundamental Rights? Examine each of them. (8)**
**14. b) State the Directive Principles of State Policy and explain its significance. (6)**

Fundamental Rights, contained in Part III (Articles 12-35) of the Indian Constitution, are the basic human rights guaranteed to all citizens. They are 'fundamental' because they are essential for the holistic development (material, intellectual, moral, and spiritual) of individuals. They are justiciable, meaning individuals can move the courts for their enforcement if violated. The Constitution guarantees six broad categories of Fundamental Rights:
1. Right to Equality (Articles 14-18): Ensures equality before the law, prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth, guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment, and abolishes untouchability and titles.
2. Right to Freedom (Articles 19-22): Guarantees six essential freedoms: speech and expression, assembly, association, movement, residence, and profession. It also provides protection in respect of conviction for offenses, protection of life and personal liberty (Article 21), and protection against arbitrary arrest and detention.
3. Right against Exploitation (Articles 23-24): Prohibits human trafficking, forced labor (begar), and the employment of children under 14 years in hazardous jobs.
4. Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25-28): Guarantees freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practice, and propagate any religion. It also provides the freedom to manage religious affairs and freedom from paying taxes for the promotion of any particular religion.
5. Cultural and Educational Rights (Articles 29-30): Protects the interests of minorities by allowing them to conserve their distinct language, script, or culture, and grants them the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
6. Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32): Empowers citizens to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of the aforementioned rights through the issuance of writs.
The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP), found in Part IV (Articles 36-51), are guidelines or principles given to the central and state governments to keep in mind while framing laws and policies. Though non-justiciable, they are fundamental in the governance of the country. Their significance lies in their aim to establish a welfare state, focusing on social and economic democracy, as opposed to the political democracy aimed at by Fundamental Rights. The DPSPs direct the state to ensure equitable distribution of material resources, prevent the concentration of wealth, secure a living wage, provide public assistance in cases of unemployment or old age, and organize agriculture on modern lines. They embody the aspirations of the people and provide a benchmark to evaluate the government's performance. By constantly striving to implement these principles, the state endeavors to create a society based on social justice and economic equality.

**15. a) Explain the various jurisdictions of the Supreme Court of India. (7)**
**15. b) Describe the constitutional duties and powers of the Prime Minister. (7)**

The Supreme Court of India stands at the apex of the judicial system and possesses extensive jurisdictions, making it one of the most powerful courts in the world. Its jurisdictions can be broadly classified into:
1. Original Jurisdiction (Article 131): The Supreme Court functions as a federal court. It has exclusive original jurisdiction to hear disputes between the Government of India and one or more States, or between two or more States. This ensures the resolution of federal conflicts. Furthermore, under Article 32, it has original jurisdiction regarding the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
2. Appellate Jurisdiction: The Supreme Court is the highest court of appeal. Its appellate jurisdiction extends to constitutional, civil, and criminal matters. It hears appeals against the judgments of High Courts if a substantial question of law regarding constitutional interpretation is involved. In civil cases, it hears appeals if the High Court certifies that a substantial question of law of general importance is involved. In criminal cases, appeals lie if the High Court has, on appeal, reversed an order of acquittal and sentenced the accused to death.
3. Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143): The President can refer to the Supreme Court any question of law or fact of public importance for its opinion. While the Court's advice is not binding on the President, it carries immense legal weight and helps the executive navigate complex constitutional issues.
4. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32): It has the power to issue prerogative writs (Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Quo Warranto, and Certiorari) for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
5. Review Jurisdiction (Article 137): The Court has the power to review any judgment pronounced or order made by it, ensuring self-correction and the rectification of manifest errors.
The Prime Minister of India is the real executive head and the head of the government. The constitutional duties and powers of the Prime Minister are vast. Firstly, concerning the Council of Ministers, the Prime Minister recommends persons to the President for appointment as ministers, allocates and reshuffles their portfolios, and presides over cabinet meetings, thus steering the policies of the government. The Prime Minister's resignation or death automatically dissolves the Council of Ministers. Secondly, concerning the President, the Prime Minister is the principal channel of communication between the President and the Council of Ministers. It is the Prime Minister's duty to communicate all decisions of the Council relating to administration and proposed legislation to the President. They advise the President on crucial appointments like the Attorney General, CAG, and Election Commissioners. Thirdly, in relation to Parliament, the Prime Minister advises the President on summoning and proroguing parliamentary sessions and can recommend the dissolution of the Lok Sabha at any time. As the chief spokesperson of the government on the floor of the House, the Prime Minister announces major policies. Thus, the Prime Minister holds the most pivotal position in the Indian politico-administrative system.

**16. a) Discuss the functions and powers of the President of India. (8)**
**16. b) Explain in detail about the Union Government structure and functions. (6)**

The President of India is the head of the state, the first citizen of India, and the symbol of national unity. While the President is a nominal executive (de jure head), the office carries significant constitutional powers and functions.
1. Executive Powers: All executive actions of the Government of India are formally taken in the President's name. The President appoints the Prime Minister and, on their advice, other ministers. The President appoints key constitutional functionaries including the Attorney General, the Comptroller and Auditor General, the Chief Election Commissioner, the Chairman of the UPSC, and Governors of states. The President also administers Union Territories directly through appointed administrators.
2. Legislative Powers: The President is an integral part of the Parliament. The President summons and prorogues the Parliament and addresses it at the commencement of the first session after each general election. A bill passed by Parliament becomes an act only after receiving the President's assent. The President has veto powers (Absolute, Suspensive, and Pocket) over bills. Crucially, when Parliament is not in session, the President can promulgate ordinances (Article 123) having the same force as parliamentary acts.
3. Judicial Powers: The President appoints the Chief Justice and other judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts. The President possesses the pardoning power (Article 72), allowing them to grant pardons, reprieves, respites, or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit, or commute the sentence of any person convicted of an offense.
4. Emergency Powers: The President has extraordinary powers to declare National Emergency (Article 352), State Emergency/President's Rule (Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360).
5. Military and Diplomatic Powers: The President is the Supreme Commander of the defense forces. Diplomatic relations, treaties, and international agreements are negotiated and concluded in the President's name.
The Union Government structure in India follows a parliamentary system based on the Westminster model. It comprises three interdependent branches: the Executive, the Legislature, and the Judiciary. The Executive consists of the President, the Vice-President, and the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. Its primary function is the implementation of laws and the day-to-day administration of the country. The real executive power vests in the Council of Ministers, which is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. The Legislature is a bicameral Parliament consisting of the President, the Rajya Sabha (Council of States), and the Lok Sabha (House of the People). Its main function is law-making, holding the executive accountable, and controlling the national finances. The Judiciary is an integrated system headed by the Supreme Court, independent of the executive and legislature. Its function is to interpret laws, protect the Constitution, and adjudicate disputes, ensuring the rule of law. This structure ensures a system of checks and balances, vital for the functioning of a democratic republic.

**17. a) Explain the various writs issued by High court of Kerala. (6)**
**17. b) Discuss the constitutional position and powers of Governor. (8)**

The High Court of Kerala, like any other High Court in India, is empowered under Article 226 of the Constitution to issue various prerogative writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose. These writs act as powerful constitutional remedies.
1. Habeas Corpus: Meaning "to have the body of," this writ is a bulwark of individual liberty against arbitrary detention. It directs the detaining authority to produce the detained person before the court to examine the legality of the detention. If the detention is found illegal, the court orders the person's immediate release.
2. Mandamus: Meaning "we command," this writ is issued to a public official, public body, corporation, or inferior court directing them to perform their mandatory legal duties, which they have failed or refused to perform. It ensures public authorities do not abdicate their responsibilities.
3. Prohibition: This writ is issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent it from exceeding its jurisdiction or usurping a jurisdiction with which it is not legally vested. It acts primarily in a preventive capacity.
4. Certiorari: Similar to prohibition, but issued to quash the order or decision of a lower court or tribunal after it has been made, usually on grounds of excess of jurisdiction, lack of jurisdiction, or an error of law apparent on the face of the record. It acts in both preventive and curative capacities.
5. Quo Warranto: Meaning "by what authority," this writ is issued to inquire into the legality of a person's claim to a public office. It prevents the illegal usurpation of public offices by individuals.
The Governor is the constitutional head of a state, functioning similarly to the President at the Union level. Appointed by the President, the Governor holds office during the President's pleasure. The Governor acts as a vital link between the Union and the State. While primarily a nominal head, the Governor possesses significant executive, legislative, and discretionary powers. All executive actions of the state government are taken in the Governor's name. The Governor appoints the Chief Minister and other ministers, the Advocate General, and the members of the State Public Service Commission. Legislatively, the Governor summons and prorogues the state legislature and gives assent to bills passed by it. The Governor can withhold assent, return ordinary bills for reconsideration, or reserve certain bills for the President's consideration. The Governor can also promulgate ordinances when the legislature is not in session. Crucially, the Governor holds discretionary powers, such as inviting a leader to form the government when no party has a clear majority, reporting to the President about the breakdown of constitutional machinery (Article 356), and reserving bills for the President. This dual role—as a constitutional head acting on ministerial advice and as a representative of the Union center—often places the Governor in a constitutionally sensitive position.

**18. a) Explain the functions of the State Legislature. (8)**
**18. b) Explain the responsibilities and functions of Council of Ministers to the State Legislative Assembly. (6)**

The State Legislature, consisting of the Governor and the legislative house(s), is the supreme law-making body at the state level in India. Its functions are multifaceted and crucial for state governance.
1. Legislative Functions: This is the primary function. The State Legislature has the exclusive power to make laws on subjects enumerated in the State List (like police, public health, local government) and concurrent power over subjects in the Concurrent List (like education, criminal law). The process involves introducing bills, debating them, and passing them by a majority vote before they receive the Governor's assent.
2. Financial Functions: The State Legislature holds the 'purse strings' of the state. No tax can be levied or collected, and no expenditure can be incurred without its approval. It scrutinizes and passes the Annual Financial Statement (Budget). Through mechanisms like demands for grants and appropriation bills, it exercises control over the state's finances.
3. Control over the Executive: In a parliamentary system, the state executive (Council of Ministers) is collectively responsible to the State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha). The Legislature exercises this control by asking questions (Question Hour), moving resolutions, raising debates on matters of public importance, and moving Adjournment Motions. The ultimate weapon of control is the No-Confidence Motion; if passed, the entire ministry must resign.
4. Electoral Functions: The elected members of the State Legislative Assembly participate in the election of the President of India. They also elect the members of the Rajya Sabha representing their state and, where applicable, one-third of the members of the State Legislative Council.
The Council of Ministers in a state, headed by the Chief Minister, exercises the real executive power. Its primary responsibility is to the State Legislative Assembly. This is known as collective responsibility, meaning the entire ministry operates as a team and swims or sinks together. If a no-confidence motion is passed against one minister or the entire cabinet by the Legislative Assembly, all ministers must resign. Their functions include formulating state policies, drafting legislation to be presented before the Assembly, and executing the laws passed by the Legislature. They prepare the state budget and present it to the Assembly for approval. The Council of Ministers advises the Governor on administrative matters and key appointments. They must answer questions posed by the legislators regarding the administration of their respective departments. Essentially, they bridge the gap between policy formulation and execution, remaining constantly accountable to the elected representatives of the people in the Assembly.

**19. a) Describe the duties and role of Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG). (8)**
**19. b) Examine the administrative and financial relations between the Union and the State. (6)**

The Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) of India, appointed by the President under Article 148, is an independent constitutional authority who serves as the supreme audit institution of the country. The CAG is the guardian of the public purse and controls the entire financial system of the country at both the Union and the State levels. The duties and role of the CAG are critical for ensuring public accountability.
1. Auditing Expenditure: The primary duty of the CAG is to audit all expenditures from the Consolidated Fund of India, the Consolidated Fund of each State, and the Consolidated Fund of each Union Territory having a Legislative Assembly. The CAG ascertains whether the money spent was legally available for and applicable to the service or purpose to which it was applied and whether the expenditure conforms to the authority that governs it.
2. Auditing Receipts and Accounts: The CAG audits all receipts and expenditures of bodies and authorities substantially financed from Union or State revenues, government companies, and other corporations when required by related laws. The CAG also audits the accounts of any other authority when requested by the President or Governor.
3. Reporting: The CAG submits audit reports relating to the accounts of the Union to the President, who causes them to be laid before Parliament. Similarly, audit reports relating to state accounts are submitted to the Governor, who lays them before the state legislature.
4. Role as a Guide: The CAG's role is not just to find faults but also to act as a guide, friend, and philosopher to the Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of the Parliament, helping it scrutinize government spending and ensure financial propriety.
The administrative and financial relations between the Union and the States in India are designed to maintain a balance between a strong center and autonomous states. Administratively, while the executive power of the state extends to its own legislative subjects, it must be exercised in a way that does not impede the Union's executive power. The Union can give directives to the states to ensure compliance with central laws, construct means of communication of national importance, and protect railways. The center also has the power to deploy armed forces in a state without its consent to maintain public order. Financially, the Constitution delineates independent tax powers. The Union collects major taxes like Income Tax (non-agricultural) and Corporate Tax, while states collect taxes on agricultural income, land revenue, and state excise. However, to address vertical fiscal imbalances, the Constitution provides for the mandatory sharing of certain central taxes with states based on the recommendations of the Finance Commission (Article 280). Furthermore, the Union provides grants-in-aid to states in need of assistance. During a Financial Emergency, the Union acquires total control over state finances.

**20. a) Enumerate the powers and functions of Public Service Commission. (8)**
**20. b) Explain the characteristics of Administrative Tribunals. What are the reasons for the growth of Administrative Tribunals in India? (6)**

Public Service Commissions in India, including the Union Public Service Commission (UPSC) at the center and State Public Service Commissions (SPSC) at the state level, are independent constitutional bodies established under Articles 315 to 323. Their primary objective is to ensure a merit-based, impartial, and efficient civil service.
1. Recruitment: The core function of the UPSC and SPSCs is to conduct examinations for appointments to the services of the Union and the States, respectively. This involves conducting written examinations and interviews to select candidates for prestigious services like the IAS, IPS, and state civil services.
2. Advisory Functions: They are consulted on all matters relating to methods of recruitment to civil services and for civil posts. They advise on the principles to be followed in making appointments, promotions, and transfers from one service to another, and on the suitability of candidates for such actions.
3. Disciplinary Matters: They are consulted on all disciplinary matters affecting a person serving under the Government of India or the Government of a State in a civil capacity. This includes issues like censure, withholding of increments, dismissal, or compulsory retirement.
4. Claims and Pensions: They advise on any claim for the award of a pension in respect of injuries sustained by a person while serving the government and any claim by a civil servant for reimbursement of legal expenses incurred in defending legal proceedings instituted against them for acts done in the execution of their duty. While their recommendations are generally advisory, conventions ensure they are almost always accepted by the government, thus safeguarding civil servants from political patronage or vindictiveness.
Administrative Tribunals are specialized, quasi-judicial bodies established outside the traditional court system to adjudicate specific administrative disputes. Their characteristics include: (a) statutory origin, meaning they are created by an act of Parliament or state legislature; (b) they are not courts in the strict sense but exercise quasi-judicial functions; (c) they are bound by the principles of natural justice rather than the strict, complex rules of evidence and civil procedure; (d) they possess specialized expertise relevant to the disputes they handle; and (e) they offer a faster and less formal process compared to regular courts.
The growth of Administrative Tribunals in India, particularly accelerated by the 42nd Amendment Act (which added Articles 323A and 323B), is driven by several factors. The expansion of the welfare state drastically increased the volume and complexity of government functions, leading to a surge in administrative litigation. Regular courts were burdened with massive backlogs, causing unacceptable delays in justice. Administrative disputes often require specialized knowledge (e.g., tax, environment, armed forces) that generalist judges might lack. Tribunals provide this necessary expertise. Furthermore, tribunals offer a cheaper, more accessible, and expeditious mechanism for resolving disputes between citizens and the state, avoiding the procedural rigidities of the traditional legal system.
